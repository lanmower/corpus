# Respirology

## Asthma

**Q:** A 22-year-old has episodic wheezing, dyspnea, and cough worsening at night. Spirometry shows FEV1/FVC 0.68 (predicted 0.80) that improves to 0.79 after bronchodilator. Diagnosis?

**A:** Asthma; bronchodilator reversibility ≥12% and ≥200 mL improvement in FEV1 confirms diagnosis

GINA and CTS asthma guidelines diagnose asthma by characteristic symptoms plus objective confirmation of variable airflow limitation. Bronchodilator reversibility, diurnal PEF variability >10%, or positive bronchoprovocation test confirms diagnosis.

**Q:** What is the stepwise treatment approach for asthma per GINA/CTS guidelines?

**A:** Step 1: SABA PRN; Step 2: low-dose ICS + SABA PRN; Step 3: low-dose ICS/LABA or medium ICS; Step 4: medium-high ICS/LABA; Step 5: add-on tiotropium, anti-IL5, or oral steroids

CTS 2021 guidelines now recommend low-dose ICS-formoterol as preferred reliever at Steps 3-5 (MART strategy) over SABA alone, based on reduced exacerbations. ICS is the cornerstone of asthma treatment.

**Q:** A 30-year-old with asthma uses salbutamol >3 days/week and wakes at night 2x/month. Current level of control and next step?

**A:** Partially controlled asthma; step up therapy — add or increase ICS (step up one level)

GINA defines controlled asthma as: daytime symptoms ≤2 days/week, no nighttime symptoms, SABA use ≤2 days/week, no activity limitation. Partially controlled = 1-2 of these criteria not met. Step-up for uncontrolled.

**Q:** What are features of a severe acute asthma exacerbation requiring ICU?

**A:** SpO2 <90%, PEF <40% predicted, pulsus paradoxus >25 mmHg, silent chest, altered consciousness, inability to speak in sentences

Severe/life-threatening asthma: silent chest (no wheeze), cyanosis, altered mental status, respiratory failure (rising PaCO2 indicates fatigue). Management: high-flow O2, continuous nebulized SABA, IV/IM corticosteroids, IV magnesium sulfate.

**Q:** What is the role of inhaled corticosteroids in asthma management?

**A:** ICS reduce airway inflammation, decrease exacerbations and hospitalizations, and improve lung function — cornerstone of asthma treatment from Step 2 onward

GINA and CTS guidelines recommend ICS for all patients with persistent asthma. ICS do not cure asthma but control symptoms and prevent exacerbations. Common side effects: oral candidiasis (use spacer, rinse mouth), dysphonia.

**Q:** A 45-year-old has asthma uncontrolled on medium-dose ICS/LABA. What add-on biologics are available and how are they chosen?

**A:** Anti-IgE (omalizumab for allergic asthma), anti-IL5 (mepolizumab, benralizumab for eosinophilic asthma), anti-IL4/13 (dupilumab)

CTS guidelines recommend biologics for severe uncontrolled asthma at Step 5 based on phenotype. Eosinophil count (≥300) guides anti-IL5 choice. Omalizumab requires elevated serum IgE and allergen sensitization.

**Q:** What is the MART strategy in asthma and what are its benefits?

**A:** Maintenance And Reliever Therapy using ICS-formoterol (budesonide-formoterol) as both daily controller and as-needed reliever — reduces exacerbations vs. SABA PRN approach

GINA 2021 recommends ICS-formoterol as preferred reliever at Steps 3-5 because each reliever use delivers anti-inflammatory medication. This reduces exacerbation frequency and asthma-related deaths compared to SABA-only relief.

**Q:** How is occupational asthma diagnosed and managed?

**A:** Serial PEF monitoring at work vs. away, specific bronchoprovocation testing; management: remove from exposure — symptoms may not fully resolve even after removal

Occupational asthma accounts for 9-15% of adult-onset asthma. Common causes: isocyanates (spray painters), flour dust (bakers), latex, animals. Early diagnosis and removal from exposure improves prognosis.

**Q:** A pregnant patient has moderate persistent asthma. Can ICS and LABAs be continued?

**A:** Yes — poorly controlled asthma is more dangerous to fetus than ICS/LABA. Continue ICS (budesonide preferred), LABA, and SABA

CTS guidelines state poorly controlled asthma increases risk of preterm birth, IUGR, and maternal complications. Benefits of ICS far outweigh risks. Oral corticosteroids can be used for exacerbations. Most asthma medications are safe in pregnancy.

**Q:** What triggers should patients with asthma be counselled to avoid?

**A:** Allergens (dust mites, pet dander, mold, pollen), respiratory infections, tobacco smoke, cold air, NSAIDs/ASA, exercise, workplace exposures, GERD

Trigger avoidance is a key non-pharmacologic intervention. ASA/NSAID sensitivity (Samter triad: asthma + nasal polyps + ASA sensitivity) requires substitution with acetaminophen. Beta-blockers are relatively contraindicated in asthma.

**Q:** A 16-year-old with asthma has SpO2 94%, moderate respiratory distress, and PEF 55% predicted after 2 puffs of salbutamol. Next management steps?

**A:** Continue salbutamol q20min x3, add ipratropium bromide, systemic corticosteroids (oral prednisone or IV methylprednisolone), supplemental O2

Moderate exacerbation: PEF 40-69%, SpO2 >90%, speaking in phrases. Adding ipratropium to SABA reduces hospitalization. Systemic corticosteroids should be given early. Reassess after initial treatment to determine if hospitalization needed.

**Q:** What is the role of leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast) in asthma?

**A:** Add-on therapy for persistent asthma not controlled on ICS alone; also useful for allergic rhinitis comorbidity and exercise-induced bronchoconstriction

Montelukast is a Step 3 add-on option per CTS guidelines. It is less effective than LABA as add-on therapy. Health Canada has a boxed warning for neuropsychiatric adverse effects (depression, suicidal ideation) — counsel patients.

**Q:** How is exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) managed?

**A:** SABA 15 min before exercise for prevention; warm-up period; consider LTRA or ICS if frequent; diagnose with exercise challenge test (FEV1 drop ≥10%)

EIB occurs in up to 90% of asthmatics. Diagnosis confirmed by ≥10% FEV1 drop on standardized exercise challenge. SABA pre-treatment is most effective. ICS reduces EIB with regular use.

**Q:** What defines asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) and how is it managed?

**A:** Persistent airflow limitation (post-BD FEV1/FVC <0.70) plus features of both asthma (variable airflow) and COPD (age >40, smoking history); manage with ICS/LABA — avoid LABA without ICS

ACO has increased exacerbation risk. CTS recommends ICS as cornerstone since ICS monotherapy is inappropriate in COPD but ICS-containing therapy is appropriate in ACO. Smoking cessation is critical.

**Q:** What are the indications for referral to a respirologist for asthma management?

**A:** Uncontrolled asthma despite Step 3-4 therapy, severe exacerbations requiring ICU, uncertain diagnosis, occupational asthma, consideration for biologic therapy, frequent ED visits

CTS guidelines recommend specialist referral for difficult-to-treat asthma. Specialist evaluation includes allergy testing, comprehensive spirometry, bronchoprovocation testing, and assessment for comorbidities (GERD, rhinosinusitis, OSA, obesity) that may worsen asthma.

**Q:** What are the spirometry criteria for diagnosing asthma?

**A:** FEV1/FVC <0.75-0.80 with >=12% and >=200mL improvement post-bronchodilator

Per CTS, reversible airflow obstruction is hallmark. If normal, methacholine challenge PC20 <4mg/mL confirms hyperresponsiveness.

**Q:** What is the stepwise approach to asthma management per CTS?

**A:** Step 1: PRN SABA; Step 2: low-dose ICS; Step 3: low ICS+LABA; Step 4: med ICS+LABA; Step 5: high ICS+LABA+add-on

CTS 2021 supports PRN ICS-formoterol as reliever across all steps. Step-up if uncontrolled >=2 times.

**Q:** What FEV1 threshold indicates severe asthma exacerbation?

**A:** FEV1 or PEF <40% predicted; <25% = life-threatening

Additional red flags: inability to speak in sentences, RR>30, HR>120, SpO2<92%, silent chest.

**Q:** Name 3 biologics for severe asthma and their targets.

**A:** Omalizumab (anti-IgE), mepolizumab (anti-IL-5), dupilumab (anti-IL-4R/IL-13)

Indicated for severe eosinophilic asthma uncontrolled on high-dose ICS+LABA. Tezepelumab (anti-TSLP) newest.

**Q:** What are the spirometry criteria for diagnosing asthma in adults per Canadian Thoracic Society (CTS) guidelines?

**A:** FEV1/FVC ratio below lower limit of normal PLUS reversibility: ≥12% AND ≥200 mL improvement in FEV1 post-bronchodilator

CTS guidelines require objective documentation of variable airflow obstruction. If spirometry is normal, methacholine challenge (PC20 <4 mg/mL) or exercise challenge can confirm diagnosis. Serial PEF variability >20% over 2 weeks is an alternative.

**Q:** How is asthma severity classified in the stepwise approach per GINA/CTS?

**A:** Step 1: SABA PRN; Step 2: low-dose ICS; Step 3: low-dose ICS/LABA; Step 4: medium-dose ICS/LABA; Step 5: high-dose ICS/LABA ± add-on (tiotropium, biologics, OCS)

CTS recommends assessing control (symptoms, exacerbations, lung function) before stepping up. LTRA is an alternative at Step 2 but less effective than ICS. As of 2023 GINA, PRN ICS-formoterol is preferred reliever at all steps.

**Q:** What defines well-controlled asthma per CTS criteria?

**A:** Daytime symptoms <4 days/week, nighttime symptoms <1 night/week, SABA use <4 doses/week, no exacerbations, no missed activities, FEV1 ≥90% personal best

CTS uses these 6 criteria to assess control. If ANY criterion is unmet, asthma is uncontrolled. The mnemonic is based on the 'Rule of 4s' for Canadian practice. Reassess in 2-4 weeks after treatment change.

**Q:** A 30-year-old asthmatic on medium-dose ICS/LABA still has poor control. Eosinophils are 450 cells/μL and IgE is elevated. What biologic therapy is most appropriate?

**A:** Anti-IL-5 (mepolizumab, benralizumab) for eosinophilic phenotype, or omalizumab if allergic phenotype with elevated IgE

Biologics are Step 5 add-on therapy. Phenotyping guides selection: anti-IL-5/IL-5R for eosinophils ≥300, anti-IgE (omalizumab) for allergic asthma, anti-IL-4Rα (dupilumab) for eosinophilic or type 2 inflammation. CTS recommends phenotyping before biologic initiation.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of ICS in asthma and why is it the cornerstone of therapy?

**A:** ICS suppress airway inflammation by inhibiting transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13), reducing eosinophilic infiltration, mucus hypersecretion, and airway remodeling

ICS target the underlying inflammatory pathology rather than just bronchospasm. Early initiation prevents irreversible airway remodeling. Systemic bioavailability varies: fluticasone and ciclesonide have lower oral bioavailability, reducing systemic side effects.

**Q:** In acute severe asthma (status asthmaticus), what are the critical signs indicating need for ICU admission and intubation?

**A:** Silent chest, altered consciousness, PaCO2 normal or rising (≥40 mmHg), PEF <25% predicted, bradycardia, hypotension, unable to speak

A normal or elevated PaCO2 in an acute asthma attack is ominous because patients should be hyperventilating (low PaCO2). A rising PaCO2 indicates respiratory muscle fatigue and impending arrest. IV magnesium sulfate 2g over 20 min is indicated.

**Q:** What is the acute asthma exacerbation management algorithm in the ED?

**A:** Continuous salbutamol nebs + ipratropium q20min x3 + systemic corticosteroids (prednisone 40-50 mg PO or methylprednisolone IV) + O2 to SpO2 93-95%. Add IV MgSO4 2g if severe.

CTS recommends ipratropium only in the first hour (no benefit after). Steroids take 4-6 hours for effect so must be given early. Reassess at 60-90 min for disposition. Aminophylline is NOT recommended routinely.

**Q:** What are the side effects of high-dose ICS therapy in asthma?

**A:** Local: oral candidiasis, dysphonia, pharyngitis. Systemic (high-dose/prolonged): adrenal suppression, osteoporosis, cataracts, glaucoma, skin thinning, growth suppression in children

Spacer use and mouth rinsing reduce local effects. Budesonide and fluticasone at equivalent doses >1000 μg/day BDP-equivalent carry systemic risk. CTS recommends bone density monitoring for patients on high-dose ICS >3 months.

**Q:** What methacholine challenge result confirms asthma diagnosis?

**A:** PC20 (provocative concentration causing 20% fall in FEV1) <4 mg/mL is diagnostic; 4-16 mg/mL is borderline; >16 mg/mL makes asthma unlikely

Methacholine challenge has high sensitivity (>95%) but lower specificity. A negative test essentially rules out asthma. Must withhold SABA 8h, LABA 48h, and antihistamines before testing. FEV1 must be >65% predicted to safely perform the test.

**Q:** What is exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) and how is it managed?

**A:** ≥10% fall in FEV1 after standardized exercise challenge. Managed with SABA 15 min pre-exercise; daily ICS if frequent. LTRA (montelukast) is alternative pre-treatment.

EIB occurs in 80-90% of asthmatics but also 10% of general population. Triggered by airway cooling and drying during exercise. Warm-up period can exploit refractory period. CTS recommends ICS as first-line if EIB indicates poor underlying control.

**Q:** How does the fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) test aid asthma management?

**A:** FeNO >50 ppb in adults suggests eosinophilic airway inflammation and predicts ICS responsiveness. FeNO <25 ppb suggests non-eosinophilic asthma or good adherence.

FeNO is a non-invasive biomarker of type 2 inflammation. Useful for confirming eosinophilic phenotype, monitoring ICS adherence, and predicting exacerbation risk. Not diagnostic alone. Levels affected by atopy, smoking (lowers), and viral infections.

**Q:** What is the Asthma-COPD Overlap (ACO) and how does its management differ?

**A:** ACO features persistent airflow limitation with features of both asthma and COPD. Treat with ICS/LABA as first-line (never LABA monotherapy). Avoid ICS withdrawal.

ACO is suspected in smokers >40 with airflow obstruction AND history of atopy/asthma or eosinophils >300. Unlike pure COPD, these patients benefit significantly from ICS. GINA/GOLD recommend starting ICS-based therapy and referring for specialist confirmation.

**Q:** A pregnant woman with asthma asks about medication safety. What is the approach?

**A:** Continue ICS (budesonide preferred), SABA, and LABA as needed. Uncontrolled asthma poses greater risk to fetus than medications. Montelukast can continue if previously effective.

CTS and SOGC guidelines emphasize that poorly controlled asthma increases risk of preeclampsia, preterm birth, and low birth weight. Budesonide has the most safety data in pregnancy. Step down is NOT recommended during pregnancy. Monthly monitoring advised.

**Q:** What occupational exposures cause occupational asthma and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Common sensitizers: isocyanates (paint), flour dust (bakers), wood dust, latex, animal dander. Diagnosed by serial PEF monitoring (at work vs away ≥2 weeks) or specific inhalation challenge.

Occupational asthma is the most common occupational lung disease in Canada. Two types: sensitizer-induced (latency period, IgE-mediated) and irritant-induced (RADS, no latency). Early removal from exposure improves prognosis; compensation through provincial WCB.

**Q:** What is the role of allergen immunotherapy in asthma?

**A:** Indicated for mild-moderate allergic asthma with clear allergen trigger. Subcutaneous (SCIT) or sublingual (SLIT) for 3-5 years can induce tolerance and reduce symptoms, medication use, and new sensitizations.

CTS supports immunotherapy as adjunct when allergen avoidance and pharmacotherapy are insufficient. Contraindicated in severe/uncontrolled asthma (risk of anaphylaxis). Must have FEV1 >70% at time of injection. Only proven disease-modifying treatment for allergic asthma.

**Q:** What are the four most common causes of chronic cough (>8 weeks) in a non-smoking adult with a normal chest X-ray?

**A:** 1) Upper airway cough syndrome (post-nasal drip) — nasal congestion, throat clearing, responds to antihistamines/nasal steroids. 2) Asthma (cough-variant) — episodic, nocturnal, responds to inhaled bronchodilators/steroids. 3) GERD — worse after meals, lying flat, associated heartburn (though may be silent). 4) ACE inhibitor-related — dry tickling cough, develops weeks to months after starting ACEi.

These four causes account for >90% of chronic cough in immunocompetent non-smokers with normal CXR. A sequential empiric treatment approach (treat most likely cause first) is often used when investigations are non-diagnostic. ACEi cough resolves within 1-4 weeks of discontinuation and should be switched to an ARB.

**Q:** A 55-year-old non-smoker on lisinopril presents with a persistent dry cough for 3 months. CXR is normal. What is the most likely cause, the mechanism, and the management?

**A:** ACE inhibitor-induced cough. Mechanism: ACE inhibitors prevent the breakdown of bradykinin and substance P in the lungs, which stimulate cough receptors. Management: discontinue the ACEi and switch to an ARB (e.g., losartan). Cough resolves within 1-4 weeks of discontinuation. Incidence is 5-20%, more common in women and those of East Asian descent.

ACEi cough is a class effect — switching to a different ACEi will not help. ARBs do not affect bradykinin metabolism and are appropriate substitutes. If the cough does not resolve after 4 weeks of ACEi discontinuation, pursue workup for other causes of chronic cough.

**Q:** How do you approach the workup of chronic cough (>8 weeks) when the etiology is not immediately apparent?

**A:** 1) Stop ACEi if applicable (reassess in 4 weeks). 2) CXR (rule out mass, infiltrate, effusion). 3) Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility and/or methacholine challenge (cough-variant asthma). 4) Empiric trial of nasal corticosteroids + antihistamine (UACS). 5) Empiric PPI trial for 8 weeks (GERD). 6) If all negative: high-resolution CT, sputum cytology (eosinophilic bronchitis), laryngoscopy, and consider psychogenic cough.

The approach is stepwise, targeting the most common causes sequentially. Methacholine challenge has a high negative predictive value — a negative test essentially excludes asthma. Non-acid reflux may cause cough without typical GERD symptoms, requiring impedance-pH monitoring for diagnosis.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of asthma?

**A:** Chronic airway inflammation (Th2-mediated: IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) causing bronchial hyperresponsiveness, mucus hypersecretion, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and reversible airflow obstruction

Mast cells, eosinophils, and T-lymphocytes are key. Early phase: IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation. Late phase: eosinophilic inflammation (4-8h). Airway remodeling occurs with chronic uncontrolled disease.

**Q:** What are the contraindications for thiopental?

**A:** Porphyria, liver disease, status asthmaticus, myxedema; also contraindicated with TCA medication interaction

Note: As of 2021, thiopental has been discontinued from production in North America.

**Q:** What are the beta-2 agonist drugs and their primary use?

**A:** Albuterol and terbutaline - primarily used as bronchodilators for asthma and COPD

Beta-2 selectivity makes these drugs useful for respiratory conditions without significant cardiac (beta-1) side effects.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of albuterol?

**A:** Selective beta-2 agonist used as a bronchodilator

Albuterol selectively stimulates beta-2 receptors in bronchial smooth muscle, causing bronchodilation. Used in asthma and COPD.

**Q:** What is the difference between affinity and efficacy for an agonist?

**A:** Affinity is the ability and strength of an agonist to bind to a receptor (e.g., salbutamol has greater affinity for β2-receptors than β1-receptors). Efficacy is the ability to elicit a response via receptor interaction once bound (e.g., salbutamol binding to β2-receptors causes smooth muscle relaxation).

A drug can have high affinity but low efficacy (partial agonist) or high affinity with high efficacy (full agonist). Drug effectiveness measures real-world performance, which may differ from efficacy determined under ideal conditions.

**Q:** What is affinity in the context of drug-receptor interaction?

**A:** The ability and strength of an agonist to bind to the receptor (e.g., salbutamol has greater affinity for β2-receptors than β1-receptors)

Affinity determines how strongly a drug binds to its receptor, influencing selectivity and potency.

**Q:** What is efficacy in the context of drug-receptor interaction?

**A:** The ability to elicit a response via receptor interaction (e.g., binding of salbutamol to β2-receptors results in smooth muscle relaxation)

Efficacy is distinct from affinity—a drug may bind well but not produce a strong response.

**Q:** What are the short-term risks of smoking?

**A:** Shortness of breath, asthma exacerbation, impotence, infertility, pregnancy complications, heartburn, upper respiratory tract infections

These symptoms can occur soon after starting smoking and are important to discuss with patients.

**Q:** What are the short-term risks of smoking to discuss with patients?

**A:** Shortness of breath, asthma exacerbation, impotence, infertility, pregnancy complications, heartburn, upper respiratory tract infections

These immediate effects often motivate patients more than long-term risks.

**Q:** List the short-term risks of smoking

**A:** Shortness of breath, asthma exacerbation, impotence, infertility, pregnancy complications, heartburn, upper respiratory tract infection

Short-term effects are often reversible but significantly impact quality of life and can worsen existing conditions.

**Q:** What are the short-term risks of smoking that should be discussed with patients?

**A:** Shortness of breath, asthma exacerbation, impotence, infertility, pregnancy complications, heartburn, upper respiratory tract infections

Short-term consequences are often more motivating for patients than long-term cancer risks.

**Q:** What are the environmental risks of second-hand smoke?

**A:** Higher risk of lung cancer, SIDS, asthma, and respiratory infections in spouse/children

Second-hand smoke affects non-smokers, particularly children and vulnerable populations.

**Q:** What is the safety concern with salmeterol (LABA) in asthma?

**A:** Salmeterol increases risk of respiratory-related and asthma-related deaths, especially in African American patients

The SMART trial found increased risk of asthma-related deaths with salmeterol, particularly in African Americans not using inhaled corticosteroids.

**Q:** What is the role of omalizumab in severe allergic asthma?

**A:** Omalizumab reduces asthma exacerbations (0.66 vs 0.88 per year) in severe allergic asthma inadequately controlled with high-dose ICS plus LABA

The EXTRA trial showed omalizumab provides additional clinical benefits including reduced exacerbations and improved control.

**Q:** What is the mechanism and benefit of tezepelumab in asthma?

**A:** Tezepelumab is a TSLP inhibitor that reduces exacerbations by 62-71% in uncontrolled asthma despite LABA and medium-high dose ICS

The PATHWAY trial demonstrated tezepelumab significantly reduces annualized exacerbation rates compared to placebo.

**Q:** What is the primary endpoint outcome with omalizumab in severe allergic asthma?

**A:** Reduced rate of asthma exacerbations (0.66 vs 0.88 per patient-year)

EXTRA 2011: 48-week treatment with omalizumab significantly reduced exacerbation rate, p=0.006

**Q:** What is EGPA (Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis, formerly Churg-Strauss)?

**A:** Granulomatous inflammation of vessels with hypereosinophilia and tissue infiltration; associated with MPO-ANCA in 40-50% of cases; triad: allergic rhinitis, asthma, eosinophilic pneumonia; other features: peripheral neuropathy (70%), GI involvement, myocarditis

EGPA typically presents in middle age with asthma history and eosinophilic lung disease.

**Q:** Which adrenergic receptor mediates bronchodilation, and which muscarinic effect opposes it?

**A:** β2 receptors mediate sympathetic bronchodilation (smooth muscle relaxation). Muscarinic (M) receptors mediate parasympathetic bronchoconstriction and increased bronchial gland secretion.

This receptor physiology underlies the treatment of asthma/COPD: β2-agonists (salbutamol, formoterol) cause bronchodilation; anticholinergics (ipratropium, tiotropium) block muscarinic-mediated bronchoconstriction. Epinephrine acts on both α and β receptors.

**Q:** How is asthma severity classified in the ED, and what defines a 'silent chest'?

**A:** Classification: Mild (FEV1 >80%, exertional SOB), Moderate (FEV1 50–80%, SOB at rest, speaking in phrases), Severe (FEV1 <50%, O2 sat <90%, speaking in words), Respiratory arrest imminent (silent chest, decreased HR, RR >30, pCO2 >45 mmHg, O2 sat <90% despite O2). Silent chest = no audible wheeze due to severely reduced airflow — a medical emergency.

A silent chest indicates near-total airway obstruction with minimal air movement — paradoxically, the wheeze disappears as airflow becomes too low to generate sound. This is a pre-arrest state requiring immediate aggressive treatment including intubation. Intubation in severe asthma carries high risk and should be avoided if possible with maximal medical therapy.

**Q:** What is the acute management of severe asthma in the ED?

**A:** O2 to achieve sat >92%, short-acting beta-agonist (salbutamol 5 mg nebulizer continuously), short-acting anticholinergic (ipratropium 0.5 mg nebulizer x3), IV steroids (methylprednisolone 125 mg), IV magnesium sulfate 2 g over 20 min (50–75 mg/kg in pediatrics).

Combined SABA + SAAC therapy reduces hospitalization rates compared to SABA alone in severe (not mild/moderate) asthma exacerbations. Magnesium sulfate acts as a bronchodilator by inhibiting smooth muscle contraction. For respiratory arrest imminent: intubate with ketamine induction (bronchodilatory properties), and IV epinephrine if associated hypotension.

**Q:** What criteria define well-controlled asthma?

**A:** Daytime symptoms <4 days/week, nocturnal symptoms <1x/week, no limitation in activity, no absence from work/school due to asthma, no AER (acute emergency room visit) from asthma, rescue inhaler use <4x/week, FEV1 ≥90% personal best, PEF variation <10–15% diurnal.

These criteria from the Canadian Thoracic Society guide assessment of asthma control. Patients not meeting these criteria have uncontrolled asthma and require step-up therapy. Assessment at each visit is important as asthma control can vary over time.

**Q:** When is it safe to discharge a patient after an acute asthma exacerbation?

**A:** FEV1 or PEF >60% predicted (safe); may be safe if 40–60% predicted depending on risk factors. Risk factors for recurrence: frequent ED visits, hospitalizations, recent steroid use, recent exacerbation, poor medication compliance, prolonged high-dose beta-agonist use.

On discharge: SABA MDI with aerochamber 2–4 puffs q2–4h then PRN, initiate inhaled corticosteroids, prednisone 30–60 mg/day for 5–10 days (no taper for short courses), counsel on aerochamber use and medication adherence, follow-up with primary care or asthma specialist.

**Q:** What is the definition of asthma?

**A:** Chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways resulting in episodes of reversible bronchospasm causing airflow obstruction, associated with reversible airflow limitation and airway hyper-responsiveness to endogenous or exogenous stimuli.

Key pathologic changes include hypertrophy of airway smooth muscle and hyperplasia of mucous-producing goblet cells. Unlike COPD, airflow limitation is reversible. Canadian prevalence is approximately 10.8% (3.8 million Canadians).

**Q:** What are the six criteria for well-controlled asthma according to Canadian guidelines?

**A:** 1) Daytime symptoms <2 days/week; 2) Night-time symptoms <1 night/week; 3) Beta-2 agonist use <2 times/week; 4) Physical activity normal (unimpaired); 5) FEV1 or PEF ≥90% of personal best; 6) Exacerbations mild and infrequent (no ER visit, hospitalization, or prednisone use). Also: PEF diurnal variation <10-15%, no asthma-related absence from work/school.

These criteria from Can J Respir Crit Care Sleep Med 2021 guide step-up or step-down of therapy. The mnemonic 'DANGERS' captures signs of poor control: Daytime Sx ≥3 d/wk, Activities reduced, Night-time Sx >1/wk, GP visits unscheduled, ER visits, Rescue puffer use ≥3×/wk, School/work absences.

**Q:** What spirometric criteria confirm a diagnosis of asthma in patients aged 6 and older?

**A:** Preferred: FEV1/FVC below lower limit of normal (adults typically <0.75–0.8; children 6+: typically <0.8–0.9) PLUS FEV1 ≥12% (and >200 mL in adults) after bronchodilator or course of controller therapy. Alternatives: PEF increase >60 L/min (and ≥20%) or diurnal variability >8% (twice daily) or >20% (multiple readings); positive methacholine challenge (FEV1 ↓≥20% at <4 mg/mL); post-exercise FEV1 ↓≥10–15%.

Objective testing is mandatory as clinical diagnosis alone is inaccurate—asthma is frequently misdiagnosed. Methacholine 4–16 mg/mL required is borderline positive. Spirometry also shows causes of decreased FEV1 (asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis).

**Q:** What are the common triggers of asthma?

**A:** URTIs, emotion/anxiety, cold air, humidity, exercise, GERD, cigarette smoke, air pollution, strong scents, allergens (pet dander, house dust, cockroaches, seasonal pollens/ragweed), NSAIDs (Samter's triad: asthma + NSAID sensitivity + nasal polyps), beta-blockers (especially non-cardioselective), hormonal fluctuations.

Work-related asthma includes occupational asthma (caused by high or low molecular weight sensitizer exposure) and work-exacerbated asthma. Identifying and avoiding triggers is a core non-pharmacologic intervention. NSAIDs cause bronchospasm via arachidonic acid pathway shunting to leukotrienes.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of a severe asthma attack?

**A:** Airway obstruction → V/Q mismatch → hypoxemia → increased ventilation → initial ↓PaCO2 → muscle fatigue → ↓ventilation → ↑PaCO2 → ↓pH (respiratory acidosis). Rising PaCO2 in an acute asthma attack is an ominous sign indicating impending respiratory failure.

Central cyanosis is not detectable until SpO2 <85%. It is more easily detected in polycythemia and less readily in anemia. Red flags include: tachypnea/tachycardia, respiratory muscle fatigue, diminished expiratory effort, cyanosis, and silent chest (most ominous).

**Q:** Outline the stepwise pharmacological management of chronic asthma in Canadian adults.

**A:** Step 1: Daily inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), OR ICS-formoterol as-needed (for patients ≥12y with expected low adherence to daily ICS). Step 2: Add LABA to low-dose ICS (combination inhaler). Step 3: Escalate ICS dose. Step 4: Consider LTRA, long-acting anticholinergics, oral corticosteroids, biologics (omalizumab [anti-IgE], mepolizumab [anti-IL5], dupilumab [anti-IL4/13]).

Any patient with poor control or at risk of exacerbations should be on ICS-containing regimen. Risk of exacerbation includes: prior exacerbation requiring steroids/ED/hospitalization, poorly-controlled asthma, or SABA overuse (>2 inhalers/year). Step down therapy once control achieved. Biologics target severe eosinophilic or allergic asthma.

**Q:** What is the emergency management of acute severe asthma?

**A:** 1) Inhaled β2-agonist first line (MDI + spacer preferred); 2) Systemic steroids (PO or IV if severe); 3) If severe: anticholinergic therapy + magnesium sulfate; 4) SC/IV adrenaline if anaphylaxis-triggered or unresponsive to inhaled β2-agonist; 5) Rapid sequence intubation for life-threatening cases (plus 100% O2, monitoring, IV access); 6) ICS maintenance therapy at discharge.

At discharge, ICS should be initiated or continued to reduce risk of future exacerbations. Magnesium sulfate causes bronchodilation by inhibiting calcium-mediated smooth muscle contraction. Spacer devices improve MDI drug delivery to airways. Non-invasive ventilation is less supported in asthma than in COPD.

**Q:** What does the DANGERS mnemonic identify in asthma management?

**A:** Signs of poor asthma control: D - Daytime symptoms ≥3 days/week; A - Activities (physical) reduced; N - Night-time symptoms >1 night/week; G - GP visits (unscheduled for exacerbations requiring steroids); E - ER visits or hospitalizations for exacerbations; R - Rescue puffer (SABA) use ≥3 times/week; S - School or work absences.

Identifying poor control triggers escalation of therapy. Note the difference from well-controlled criteria: daytime symptoms <2 d/wk for control vs. ≥3 d/wk for poor control. The written Asthma Action Plan uses 'green/yellow/red' zones to guide self-management.

**Q:** What is the role of ICS-formoterol as-needed versus ICS + SABA in mild-to-moderate asthma?

**A:** ICS-formoterol as-needed reduces exacerbation rate and odds versus ICS + as-needed SABA (RR 0.85; OR 0.86). Canadian guidelines still recommend ICS + as-needed SABA as first-line for symptom control, but consider ICS-formoterol equal for those not otherwise at high risk. GINA 2021 prefers ICS-formoterol as-needed over ICS maintenance for mild asthma.

ICS-formoterol as reliever provides anti-inflammatory coverage at each rescue dose, potentially reducing exacerbation risk. Budesonide-formoterol (bud/form) is approved as a reliever for ≥12 years, only when also used as maintenance therapy. Canadian and GINA guidelines are somewhat different in their first-line recommendations.

**Q:** What is the addition of LABA to ICS shown to do in asthma management (evidence)?

**A:** In adults symptomatic on low to high doses of ICS monotherapy, addition of a daily LABA reduces rate of exacerbations requiring oral steroids by 23% and leads to significantly greater improvement in FEV1 compared to ICS monotherapy. In children, the effects are uncertain (Cochrane DB Syst Rev 2010).

This evidence supports the step 2 recommendation in asthma management: add LABA to ICS in adults not controlled on ICS alone. A combination inhaler (not separate ICS and LABA inhalers) is recommended to ensure LABA not used without ICS. LABAs must never be used as monotherapy in asthma due to increased asthma mortality risk without ICS.

## COPD & Emphysema

**Q:** A 65-year-old smoker (45 pack-years) has progressive dyspnea, chronic productive cough, and spirometry showing FEV1/FVC 0.62 post-bronchodilator. Diagnosis and GOLD staging?

**A:** COPD; GOLD stage based on FEV1: Stage I ≥80%, Stage II 50-79%, Stage III 30-49%, Stage IV <30% predicted

CTS/GOLD guidelines define COPD by post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70. GOLD classification guides pharmacotherapy. Clinical assessment (exacerbation history, mMRC dyspnea score, CAT score) determines GOLD group A-D.

**Q:** What is the single most important intervention for COPD management?

**A:** Smoking cessation — the only intervention proven to slow FEV1 decline and reduce mortality

CTS guidelines strongly recommend smoking cessation for all COPD patients. Pharmacotherapy (varenicline preferred, NRT, bupropion) combined with behavioral support doubles cessation rates. Even after decades of smoking, cessation slows disease progression.

**Q:** What is the first-line pharmacotherapy for mild-moderate symptomatic COPD (GOLD group A-B)?

**A:** Long-acting bronchodilator: LAMA (tiotropium, umeclidinium) or LABA (salmeterol, formoterol) — LAMA preferred for dyspnea reduction

CTS COPD guidelines recommend LAMA as first-line for most symptomatic COPD patients. LAMA (tiotropium) superior to LABA for reducing exacerbations and hospitalizations (UPLIFT trial). LAMA+LABA combination for persistent symptoms.

**Q:** When are inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) indicated in COPD?

**A:** ICS added to LAMA+LABA when: ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe exacerbation per year AND eosinophil count ≥300 cells/μL (or ≥100 with frequent exacerbations)

CTS guidelines recommend triple therapy (ICS+LAMA+LABA) for high-risk COPD patients (Group D/E). ICS alone is inappropriate in COPD. Blood eosinophil count guides ICS benefit — higher counts predict greater response.

**Q:** A 68-year-old with COPD has PaO2 54 mmHg at rest on room air. What is indicated?

**A:** Long-term home oxygen therapy (LTOT) for ≥15-18 hours/day — improves survival in severe hypoxemia

NOCTURNAL O2 and MRC trials showed LTOT survival benefit when PaO2 ≤55 mmHg or SaO2 ≤88%. Indications: PaO2 ≤55 mmHg (SaO2 ≤88%), or PaO2 56-59 mmHg with polycythemia, cor pulmonale, or pulmonary hypertension. Minimum 15h/day for benefit.

**Q:** What characterizes an acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) and how is it managed?

**A:** AECOPD: acute worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal variation requiring change in therapy; treat with short-acting bronchodilators, systemic corticosteroids (prednisone 40mg x5 days), antibiotics if purulent sputum

CTS guidelines: antibiotics for AECOPD with purulent sputum or requiring hospitalization. Preferred antibiotics: doxycycline, amoxicillin-clavulanate, azithromycin. NIV (BiPAP) for AECOPD with hypercapnic respiratory failure reduces intubation and mortality.

**Q:** What is the role of pulmonary rehabilitation in COPD?

**A:** Pulmonary rehab reduces dyspnea, improves exercise capacity and quality of life, reduces hospitalizations — recommended for all COPD patients with MRC dyspnea ≥2 or after exacerbation

CTS guidelines strongly recommend pulmonary rehabilitation as a core COPD treatment. Programs include exercise training, education, and psychosocial support. Benefits are greatest when started within 4 weeks of an exacerbation.

**Q:** What is the difference between emphysema and chronic bronchitis in COPD?

**A:** Emphysema: destruction of alveolar walls and airspace enlargement (pink puffer: thin, barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing); Chronic bronchitis: productive cough ≥3 months/year for ≥2 consecutive years (blue bloater: cyanotic, edematous)

These are clinical phenotypes within COPD spectrum. Most patients have features of both. Emphysema is a pathological diagnosis; chronic bronchitis is clinical. CT findings of emphysema correlate with worse prognosis.

**Q:** What are indications for NIV (BiPAP) in AECOPD?

**A:** Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure (pH <7.35, PaCO2 >45 mmHg) with moderate-severe dyspnea, accessory muscle use — reduces intubation and mortality

NICE and CTS guidelines recommend NIV as first-line for AECOPD with acute hypercapnic failure when pH 7.25-7.35. NIV reduces mortality by ~48% and intubation rate. Contraindications: unconscious, vomiting, hemodynamic instability.

**Q:** What vaccinations are recommended for COPD patients?

**A:** Influenza (annual), pneumococcal (PCV13 + PPSV23), COVID-19, RSV vaccine (age ≥60), Tdap booster

CTS guidelines recommend all COPD patients receive influenza and pneumococcal vaccines to reduce exacerbations and pneumonia. Influenza vaccination reduces AECOPD hospitalizations by ~40%. RSV vaccine now recommended for older COPD patients.

**Q:** A 70-year-old with severe COPD is in respiratory failure. When should intubation and mechanical ventilation be considered?

**A:** When NIV fails or is contraindicated; clinical indicators: pH <7.25, severe encephalopathy, hemodynamic instability, inability to protect airway, respiratory arrest

Invasive MV in COPD has significant complications (VAP, prolonged weaning, diaphragm atrophy). Goals of care discussion is critical before intubation. Non-invasive strategies (NIV, high-flow NC) should be maximized first.

**Q:** What alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency causes and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** A1AT deficiency (ZZ genotype) causes early-onset panacinar emphysema (especially lower lobes), liver disease; diagnose with serum A1AT level and genotyping

A1AT deficiency should be suspected in COPD patients <45 years, non-smokers, or with family history. Treatment: smoking cessation, standard COPD therapy, A1AT augmentation therapy for severe deficiency with emphysema.

**Q:** What spirometry pattern defines COPD and how does it differ from restrictive lung disease?

**A:** COPD: obstructive pattern — FEV1/FVC <0.70 post-bronchodilator, FVC normal or reduced; Restrictive: FEV1/FVC normal or elevated, TLC <80% predicted

COPD is defined by persistent airflow obstruction. Restrictive disease (fibrosis, obesity, NMD) shows reduced TLC with preserved or increased FEV1/FVC ratio. Mixed patterns occur (bronchiectasis with fibrosis).

**Q:** A 66-year-old COPD patient develops worsening dyspnea and peripheral edema. CXR shows enlarged heart and bilateral pleural effusions. What complication is present?

**A:** Cor pulmonale — right heart failure from pulmonary hypertension secondary to chronic hypoxemia

COPD-related pulmonary hypertension results from hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling. Treatment: LTOT is most effective intervention. Diuretics for edema. Sildenafil and other pulmonary arterial HTN drugs have limited evidence in COPD-associated PH.

**Q:** What are the criteria for hospital admission in AECOPD?

**A:** Severe dyspnea, SpO2 <88%, altered mental status, inability to manage at home, high social risk, respiratory failure (pH <7.35, PaCO2 >45), failure of outpatient treatment

CTS guidelines provide criteria for safe outpatient management vs. hospitalization. Patients with comorbidities (pneumonia, CHF, PE) require admission. ICU transfer for pH <7.25, hemodynamic instability, or NIV failure.

**Q:** What are the GOLD spirometric criteria for COPD?

**A:** Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.70

GOLD stages: 1(>=80%), 2(50-79%), 3(30-49%), 4(<30%). Fixed ratio may overdiagnose in elderly.

**Q:** When should ICS be added to COPD therapy?

**A:** Frequent exacerbations (>=2/yr) despite LAMA+LABA AND blood eos >=300

ICS increases pneumonia risk. Triple therapy (ICS+LAMA+LABA) reduces mortality per ETHOS/IMPACT trials.

**Q:** What are indications for long-term O2 in COPD?

**A:** PaO2 <=55 or SpO2 <=88% at rest, OR PaO2 56-59 with cor pulmonale/polycythemia

Must use >=15 hrs/day for mortality benefit. Nocturnal O2 alone does not reduce mortality.

**Q:** What is the GOLD classification system for COPD severity by spirometry?

**A:** GOLD 1: FEV1 ≥80% predicted (mild); GOLD 2: 50-79% (moderate); GOLD 3: 30-49% (severe); GOLD 4: <30% (very severe). All require post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70.

GOLD spirometric grading is separate from the ABE assessment (symptoms + exacerbation history). Post-bronchodilator values are used to confirm persistent airflow limitation. FEV1/FVC naturally declines with age; using fixed ratio may over-diagnose in elderly.

**Q:** Describe the GOLD ABE assessment tool for COPD management decisions.

**A:** Group A: low symptoms (mMRC 0-1, CAT <10) + 0-1 non-hospitalized exacerbations. Group B: high symptoms + 0-1 non-hospitalized exacerbations. Group E: ≥2 moderate exacerbations OR ≥1 hospitalized exacerbation (any symptoms).

GOLD 2023 replaced ABCD with ABE. Group A: bronchodilator PRN. Group B: LABA or LAMA. Group E: LAMA/LABA (or LABA/LAMA/ICS if eosinophils ≥300). This separates spirometric severity from treatment group assignment.

**Q:** When should ICS be added to COPD therapy and when should it be avoided?

**A:** Add ICS if: eosinophils ≥300, asthma-COPD overlap, or ≥2 exacerbations/year despite LAMA/LABA. Avoid ICS if: recurrent pneumonia, mycobacterial infection, eosinophils <100.

ICS in COPD increases pneumonia risk (especially fluticasone). Blood eosinophils are the best biomarker for ICS response. CTS and GOLD recommend eosinophil-guided ICS use. Triple therapy (ICS/LAMA/LABA) reduces exacerbations and mortality in selected patients (IMPACT trial).

**Q:** What is the management of an acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD)?

**A:** Short-acting bronchodilators (salbutamol + ipratropium), systemic corticosteroids (prednisone 40 mg x 5 days), antibiotics if purulent sputum or severe exacerbation, O2 to target SpO2 88-92%.

CTS recommends antibiotics for Anthonisen Type 1 exacerbations (increased dyspnea + sputum volume + purulence). First-line: amoxicillin, doxycycline, or TMP-SMX. Short steroid courses (5 days) are as effective as 14 days. NIV if pH <7.35 with hypercapnia.

**Q:** What are the indications for non-invasive ventilation (NIV/BiPAP) in AECOPD?

**A:** Respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35, PaCO2 >45), severe dyspnea with accessory muscle use, persistent hypoxemia despite supplemental O2

NIV reduces intubation rates by 60% and mortality by 50% in AECOPD. Contraindications: facial trauma, GCS <8, inability to protect airway, hemodynamic instability, undrained pneumothorax. Reassess in 1-2 hours; intubate if pH worsens.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of COPD including the protease-antiprotease imbalance?

**A:** Chronic inflammation (neutrophils, macrophages, CD8+ T-cells) → protease excess (neutrophil elastase, MMP) over antiproteases (α1-antitrypsin) → alveolar wall destruction (emphysema) + goblet cell hyperplasia/mucus (chronic bronchitis)

Unlike asthma (eosinophils, CD4+, Th2), COPD is driven by neutrophils, CD8+, Th1. Oxidative stress from smoking inactivates antiproteases and activates NF-κB. This explains why ICS (targeting eosinophilic inflammation) are less effective in COPD than asthma.

**Q:** When should α1-antitrypsin (AAT) deficiency be tested in COPD?

**A:** Test all COPD patients at least once (CTS), especially if: onset <45 years, minimal smoking history, basilar emphysema, family history, associated liver disease, or panniculitis

AAT deficiency (PiZZ genotype) is the most common genetic cause of emphysema. Serum AAT level <11 μmol/L (or <57 mg/dL) warrants genotyping. IV augmentation therapy is available for select patients. Affects 1:3000-5000 Canadians of European descent.

**Q:** What are the indications for long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) in COPD?

**A:** PaO2 ≤55 mmHg or SpO2 ≤88% at rest; OR PaO2 56-59 mmHg with cor pulmonale, polycythemia (Hct >55%), or pulmonary hypertension. Must be on optimal therapy, measured twice 3 weeks apart.

LTOT ≥15 hours/day improves survival in hypoxemic COPD (NOTT and MRC trials). Target SpO2 88-92% to avoid hypercapnia. Does NOT improve survival in moderate resting or exercise-induced desaturation without severe resting hypoxemia (LOTT trial).

**Q:** What are the criteria for lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) in COPD?

**A:** Upper-lobe predominant emphysema + low exercise capacity after pulmonary rehab. FEV1 20-45% predicted. Contraindicated if FEV1 <20% with homogeneous emphysema or DLCO <20%.

NETT trial showed survival benefit only in upper-lobe emphysema with low exercise capacity. Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (endobronchial valves) is an alternative for selected patients with intact interlobar fissures and no collateral ventilation.

**Q:** What vaccinations are recommended for COPD patients in Canada?

**A:** Annual influenza, pneumococcal (PCV20 or PCV15+PPSV23), COVID-19 per NACI, Tdap once if not received in adulthood, RSV vaccine for ≥60 years, shingles (Shingrix) ≥50 years

CTS and NACI strongly recommend influenza and pneumococcal vaccination to reduce AECOPD. Pneumococcal vaccine reduces invasive pneumococcal disease. NACI updated to preferentially recommend PCV20 for immunocompromised and those with chronic lung disease.

**Q:** What is the role of pulmonary rehabilitation in COPD?

**A:** Recommended for all COPD patients with persistent symptoms despite optimal pharmacotherapy (especially mMRC ≥2). Improves exercise capacity, dyspnea, quality of life, and reduces hospitalizations. 6-8 week minimum program.

CTS recommends pulmonary rehab as a cornerstone of COPD management. Components: supervised exercise training, education, nutritional counseling, psychosocial support. Benefit is independent of GOLD stage. Post-exacerbation rehab within 4 weeks reduces readmissions.

**Q:** How does COPD affect gas exchange and what is the V/Q mismatch pattern?

**A:** Emphysema: destroyed alveoli → high V/Q (dead space). Chronic bronchitis: mucus plugging → low V/Q (shunt physiology). Both cause hypoxemia; chronic bronchitis more prone to hypercapnia.

The 'pink puffer' (emphysema-predominant) maintains ventilation drive with near-normal PaCO2 but is dyspneic. The 'blue bloater' (bronchitis-predominant) has chronic hypercapnia, cyanosis, and cor pulmonale. Most patients have mixed phenotype.

**Q:** What pharmacologic options reduce COPD exacerbation frequency beyond bronchodilators?

**A:** ICS (if eosinophils ≥300), roflumilast (PDE4 inhibitor if FEV1 <50% + chronic bronchitis phenotype + frequent exacerbations), azithromycin prophylaxis (250 mg daily), NAC

Roflumilast has GI side effects (diarrhea, weight loss) and psychiatric effects. Azithromycin reduces exacerbations but risks QTc prolongation, hearing loss, and antimicrobial resistance. CTS recommends these as add-on to optimized inhaler therapy.

**Q:** What defines cor pulmonale and how does it develop in COPD?

**A:** Right heart failure secondary to pulmonary hypertension from chronic hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Features: JVD, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, loud P2, right axis deviation on ECG.

Chronic hypoxemia causes pulmonary arterial smooth muscle remodeling and fixed pulmonary hypertension. LTOT is the primary treatment by correcting hypoxemia. Diuretics for fluid overload. Calcium channel blockers only if vasoreactivity testing positive (rare in COPD-related PH).

**Q:** What is the BODE index in COPD and what does it predict?

**A:** B=BMI (<21=1pt), O=airflow Obstruction (FEV1%), D=Dyspnea (mMRC), E=Exercise capacity (6MWD). Score 0-10; higher scores predict higher mortality. BODE >7: median survival ~3 years.

BODE is a better mortality predictor than FEV1 alone because it captures systemic effects of COPD. Used to guide goals-of-care discussions and lung transplant referral (BODE ≥7). Also helps identify patients who benefit most from pulmonary rehabilitation.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of COPD?

**A:** Chronic inflammation (neutrophil/macrophage-mediated, protease-antiprotease imbalance, oxidative stress) causing small airway fibrosis/obstruction + parenchymal destruction (emphysema) = irreversible airflow limitation

Emphysema: elastase destroys alveolar walls, reducing elastic recoil and gas exchange surface. Chronic bronchitis: mucus gland hyperplasia, goblet cell metaplasia, chronic productive cough. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency in young non-smokers.

**Q:** What is obstructive sleep apnea and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Recurrent upper airway collapse during sleep. STOP-BANG: Snoring, Tired, Observed apnea, Pressure(HTN), BMI>35, Age>50, Neck>40cm, Gender(male). >=3 = high risk. Diagnose: polysomnography AHI >=5

AHI 5-15 mild, 15-30 moderate, >30 severe. Treatment: CPAP (first-line for moderate-severe), weight loss, positional therapy, mandibular advancement device. Untreated OSA: HTN, AF, stroke, MVA risk, metabolic syndrome.

**Q:** What is the STOP-BANG questionnaire, and how is it used to screen for obstructive sleep apnea?

**A:** STOP-BANG: Snoring, Tiredness, Observed apneas, Pressure (hypertension), BMI >35, Age >50, Neck circumference >40 cm, Gender (male). Score ≥3: intermediate risk; ≥5: high risk for moderate-severe OSA.

OSA affects ~5-15% of the general population and is underdiagnosed. It is associated with resistant hypertension, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and motor vehicle accidents. Screening is particularly important preoperatively, as undiagnosed OSA increases risk of postoperative respiratory complications.

**Q:** How is obstructive sleep apnea diagnosed, and what does the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) indicate?

**A:** Diagnosis: polysomnography (PSG) or home sleep apnea test. AHI = apneas + hypopneas per hour of sleep. Mild OSA: AHI 5-14, Moderate: 15-29, Severe: ≥30. CPAP is first-line for moderate-severe OSA.

Polysomnography is the gold standard and also evaluates sleep stages, oxygen desaturation, and limb movements. Home sleep testing is appropriate for patients with high pretest probability and no significant comorbidities. CPAP reduces daytime sleepiness, blood pressure, and cardiovascular risk when used ≥4 hours/night.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for obstructive sleep apnea besides CPAP?

**A:** Weight loss (most effective long-term), mandibular advancement devices (mild-moderate OSA), positional therapy (supine-predominant OSA), uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP, select patients), hypoglossal nerve stimulation (moderate-severe OSA intolerant of CPAP).

A 10% weight loss can reduce AHI by ~50%. Mandibular advancement devices are less effective than CPAP but have better adherence. UPPP success rates are only ~40-50%. Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Inspire device) is emerging as an option for patients who cannot tolerate CPAP and meet selection criteria.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, and when is pharmacotherapy appropriate?

**A:** CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia) is first-line. Pharmacotherapy is appropriate when CBT-I is unavailable, ineffective, or as a short-term bridge. Options: short-acting benzodiazepine receptor agonists (zopiclone), melatonin receptor agonists, or low-dose trazodone.

CBT-I addresses the cognitive and behavioral perpetuating factors of insomnia (stimulus control, sleep restriction, cognitive restructuring) and has durable effects beyond treatment. Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs should be avoided long-term due to tolerance, dependence, and fall risk in elderly. Sleep hygiene alone is insufficient for chronic insomnia.

**Q:** What are the components of good sleep hygiene counseling?

**A:** Maintain consistent sleep-wake schedule, avoid screens 1 hour before bed, keep bedroom dark/cool/quiet, avoid caffeine after noon, avoid alcohol before bed, exercise regularly but not within 3 hours of bedtime, use bed only for sleep and intimacy, avoid daytime napping >30 minutes.

Sleep hygiene is a necessary but usually insufficient standalone treatment for chronic insomnia. It is most effective as part of CBT-I. Caffeine has a half-life of 5-7 hours, and alcohol, while sedating initially, fragments sleep architecture and worsens OSA. Blue light from screens suppresses melatonin secretion.

**Q:** What is narcolepsy type 1, and what are the classic tetrad of symptoms?

**A:** Narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy): excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion), sleep paralysis, and hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations. Caused by loss of hypocretin (orexin)-producing neurons.

Cataplexy is pathognomonic for narcolepsy type 1 and distinguishes it from type 2 (without cataplexy). Diagnosis is confirmed by MSLT showing mean sleep latency ≤8 minutes with ≥2 sleep-onset REM periods (SOREMPs), or low CSF hypocretin-1 levels. Treatment includes stimulants (modafinil) for sleepiness and sodium oxybate for cataplexy.

**Q:** How does narcolepsy type 2 differ from type 1, and what are the treatment options for excessive daytime sleepiness?

**A:** Type 2 lacks cataplexy and has normal CSF hypocretin levels. Treatment for EDS: modafinil/armodafinil (first-line), methylphenidate or amphetamines (second-line), solriamfetol, pitolisant (histamine H3 antagonist).

Narcolepsy type 2 is diagnosed by MSLT criteria in the absence of cataplexy and other causes of hypersomnia. Scheduled naps (15-20 min) can supplement pharmacotherapy. Patients should be counseled about driving safety, and many jurisdictions require physician reporting of conditions affecting fitness to drive.

**Q:** A patient on CPAP for OSA reports persistent daytime sleepiness despite good adherence (>6 hours/night) and AHI <5 on download. What should you consider?

**A:** Consider comorbid sleep disorders (narcolepsy, periodic limb movement disorder, insufficient sleep syndrome), depression, hypothyroidism, medication side effects, or residual sleepiness of OSA. Formal in-lab polysomnography and MSLT may be needed.

Residual excessive sleepiness despite optimal CPAP therapy occurs in ~5-10% of OSA patients. Solriamfetol and modafinil are approved for residual sleepiness in OSA. It is important to verify adherence data, ensure adequate total sleep time (≥7 hours), and evaluate for depression and other sleep disorders before adding wake-promoting agents.

**Q:** What are the physiological consequences of untreated severe OSA, and which cardiovascular conditions are most strongly associated?

**A:** Intermittent hypoxia causes sympathetic activation, oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. Strongly associated conditions: resistant hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, pulmonary hypertension, and sudden cardiac death (nocturnal).

OSA is the most common identifiable cause of resistant hypertension. AF recurrence after cardioversion or ablation is significantly higher in untreated OSA. CPAP treatment reduces blood pressure by ~2-3 mmHg on average and may reduce AF recurrence. Screening for OSA is recommended in patients with resistant hypertension or recurrent AF.

**Q:** What is central sleep apnea, and how does it differ from obstructive sleep apnea in pathophysiology and treatment?

**A:** CSA involves absent respiratory effort due to impaired central respiratory drive (no airflow AND no chest/abdominal movement). OSA has respiratory effort against a collapsed airway. CSA causes: heart failure (Cheyne-Stokes), opioids, stroke, high altitude. Treatment: address underlying cause, adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) — but ASV is contraindicated in HFrEF (EF ≤45%).

The SERVE-HF trial showed increased cardiovascular mortality with ASV in HFrEF patients with CSA, leading to a contraindication. CPAP may be used cautiously in CSA with heart failure. Opioid-induced CSA is treated by reducing opioid dose when possible. Cheyne-Stokes respiration with CSA in heart failure indicates poor prognosis.

**Q:** Why are beta-blockers often contraindicated in MAT treatment?

**A:** Beta-blockers may be contraindicated due to severe underlying pulmonary disease (COPD) commonly associated with MAT

MAT frequently occurs in patients with COPD or hypoxemia, where beta-blockers can precipitate bronchospasm and worsen respiratory function.

**Q:** What is the hemodynamic threshold for severe COPD that contraindicates cardiac transplant?

**A:** FEV1 <1L

Severe airflow obstruction compromises the ability to clear secretions and tolerate the physiological demands of transplant surgery and recovery.

**Q:** What are secondary causes of osteoporosis?

**A:** Chronic malnutrition or malabsorption, chronic liver disease, COPD, IBD, chronic inflammatory conditions

Secondary osteoporosis occurs due to other medical conditions that affect bone health indirectly through malabsorption, chronic inflammation, or other mechanisms.

**Q:** List causes of high dead space ventilation leading to hypercapnia

**A:** COPD, CF, chest wall disorders, rapid shallow breathing pattern

Dead space ventilation means perfusion without ventilation. Despite normal or increased minute ventilation, alveolar ventilation is inadequate for CO2 removal.

**Q:** What is the benefit of adding tiotropium to LABA plus ICS in uncontrolled asthma?

**A:** Tiotropium improves FEV1 (86-154 mL improvement) and delays time to first severe exacerbation by 56 days

The PrimoTinA-asthma trials showed tiotropium added to LABA+ICS provides better lung function and fewer exacerbations.

**Q:** What FEV1 improvement does tiotropium add to LABA plus ICS?

**A:** 86-154 mL improvement in peak FEV1

PrimoTinA-asthma trials 2021: significant improvements in FEV1 and 56-day delay to first severe exacerbation

**Q:** What are the cardinal symptoms of acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD)?

**A:** Increased dyspnea, increased coughing frequency or severity, increased sputum volume or purulence. Common triggers: viral infection, pneumonia, PE, CHF, MI, drugs.

The Anthonisen criteria define AECOPD by these three cardinal symptoms. The presence of all three (or two including increased sputum purulence) indicates a need for antibiotics. Only one symptom indicates mild exacerbation not requiring antibiotics. Key differentials (CHF, PE, pneumonia, ACS) must be excluded.

**Q:** What is the target oxygen saturation in AECOPD and why?

**A:** Target O2 saturation 88–92%. Do not withhold oxygen if hypoxic, but avoid over-oxygenating. Many COPD patients are chronic CO2 retainers who rely on hypoxic drive; excessive O2 can worsen hypercapnia.

The Haldane effect and V/Q mismatch explain hyperoxia-induced hypercapnia in COPD. However, hypoxia is immediately life-threatening and must be corrected. A controlled target of 88–92% balances these risks. Titrated oxygen delivery is preferable to high-flow O2.

**Q:** When are antibiotics indicated in AECOPD?

**A:** Antibiotics are indicated when all 3 cardinal symptoms are present (increased dyspnea, sputum volume, purulence), OR 2 cardinal symptoms including increased sputum purulence, OR mechanical ventilation. Choices: TMP/SMX, cephalosporins, respiratory quinolones. No antibiotics for mild exacerbation (only 1 of 3 cardinal symptoms).

Approximately 50% of AECOPD exacerbations are caused by bacteria (most commonly H. influenzae, S. pneumoniae, M. catarrhalis). The Anthonisen criteria guide antibiotic decisions. Purulent sputum is the best clinical predictor of bacterial etiology. Fluoroquinolones have better coverage for atypical organisms.

**Q:** What are the indications for non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV/BiPAP) in AECOPD?

**A:** Severe distress or signs of fatigue, arterial pH <7.35, or hypercapnia. NIPPV reduces need for intubation and mortality in AECOPD. Consider intubation if NIPPV not tolerated (decreased LOC, vomiting).

NIPPV (BiPAP or CPAP) reduces work of breathing, improves alveolar ventilation, and decreases respiratory muscle fatigue. It is preferred over intubation when tolerated. Contraindications include altered mental status, inability to protect airway, and cardiovascular instability.

**Q:** What is dyspnea and what are the broad categories in its differential diagnosis?

**A:** Dyspnea: uncomfortable, abnormal awareness of breathing / subjective SOB. Differential: Pulmonary (pneumonia, COVID, COPD, asthma, PE, lung cancer, congenital); Cardiac (HF, CAD, MI, cardiomyopathy, valvular, pericardial); Mixed/Other (deconditioning, psychological trauma, neuromuscular); Functional (anxiety, panic attack, hyperventilation).

Dyspnea assessment requires history (associated symptoms, occupational/environmental exposure, travel, medications, smoking), physical exam (vitals, respiratory, precordial), and investigations (CXR, ECG, PFTs, ABG if acutely indicated).

**Q:** What is the definition of COPD and what spirometric value is required for diagnosis?

**A:** Progressive and irreversible condition of the lung characterized by chronic obstruction to airflow, with many patients having periodic exacerbations, gas trapping, lung hyperinflation, and at end stages weight loss. Diagnosis requires spirometry showing post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 (or below lower limit of normal).

COPD is often 'overdiagnosed' due to inaccuracy of clinical diagnosis alone—objective spirometry is mandatory. Two phenotypes: chronic bronchitis (defined clinically) and emphysema (defined pathologically); most patients have elements of both. Gradual decrease in FEV1 over time, more rapidly with each acute exacerbation.

**Q:** Compare and contrast the 'Blue Bloater' (chronic bronchitis) and 'Pink Puffer' (emphysema) phenotypes of COPD.

**A:** Blue Bloater (chronic bronchitis): productive cough, purulent sputum, cyanosis, peripheral edema (cor pulmonale), crackles/wheezes, frequently obese, CXR shows increased bronchovascular markings. PFTs: ↓FEV1, ↓FEV1/FVC, normal TLC, normal or ↓DLCO. Pink Puffer (emphysema): dyspnea on exertion, minimal cough, tachypnea, pursed-lip breathing, cachectic, barrel chest, hyperresonant, ↓breath sounds, CXR shows hyperinflation, flat diaphragm, ↑retrosternal space, bullae. PFTs: ↓FEV1, ↓FEV1/FVC, ↑TLC (hyperinflation), ↑RV (gas trapping), ↓DLCO.

These are extreme ends of the spectrum—most COPD patients have elements of both. These terms are outdated and rarely used in clinical practice. Chronic bronchitis is defined clinically (productive cough most days for ≥3 consecutive months in 2 successive years); emphysema is defined pathologically (dilation and destruction of air spaces distal to terminal bronchiole).

**Q:** What are the two types of emphysema and their associations?

**A:** 1) Centrilobular: respiratory bronchioles predominantly affected; typical form in smokers, primarily affects upper lung zones. 2) Panacinar (panlobular): all parts of acinus affected; accounts for ~1% of emphysema cases, typically from alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, primarily affects lower lobes.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin (A1AT) deficiency is an important treatable risk factor for COPD that should be excluded, especially in non-smokers or younger patients with emphysema. It causes panacinar emphysema with lower lobe predominance, contrasting with the upper lobe predominance of smoking-related emphysema.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for COPD?

**A:** 1) Smoking: #1 risk factor in Western countries; 2) Environmental: wood smoke/biomass fuel exposure (especially developing countries), air pollution, occupational exposures; 3) Treatable: alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, HIV (accelerated COPD progression), concurrent bronchial hyperactivity (asthma-COPD overlap, 'ACO'); 4) Demographic: age, history of childhood respiratory infections, low socioeconomic status.

Smoking cessation is the single most important intervention for patients with COPD who smoke. Work-related exposures and biomass fuels are underappreciated causes globally. Asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) has features of both conditions and may benefit from combined therapy.

**Q:** What are the GOLD spirometric severity classifications for COPD?

**A:** Based on post-bronchodilator FEV1 (% predicted) in patients with FEV1/FVC <0.70: GOLD 1 (Mild): FEV1 ≥80% predicted; GOLD 2 (Moderate): 50% ≤ FEV1 < 80%; GOLD 3 (Severe): 30% ≤ FEV1 < 50%; GOLD 4 (Very Severe): FEV1 <30% predicted.

Note that FEV1 correlates weakly with symptoms; the COPD Assessment Tool (CAT) and mMRC dyspnea scale are used for comprehensive symptom assessment. Treatment decisions are based on both spirometric severity and symptom burden (CAT ≥10 and/or mMRC ≥2).

**Q:** What are the indications for home oxygen therapy in COPD and what are its benefits?

**A:** Indicated if: 1) PaO2 <55 mmHg; or 2) PaO2 56–59 mmHg with cor pulmonale or polycythemia. Benefits: prevents cor pulmonale and decreases mortality when used >15 hours/day. For CO2 retainers, maintain O2 saturation 88–92% to prevent worsening hypercapnia.

Mechanisms of hypercapnia worsening with excess O2 include: V/Q mismatch worsening, the Haldane effect (O2 displaces CO2 from hemoglobin), and decreased respiratory drive. Home oxygen is one of few interventions proven to prolong survival in COPD (along with smoking cessation and triple therapy in high-risk patients).

**Q:** Outline the stepwise pharmacological treatment for stable COPD.

**A:** All patients: short-acting bronchodilators PRN (SABA e.g. salbutamol; SAMA e.g. ipratropium; SABA/SAMA combination). For dyspnea (mMRC ≥1): add long-acting bronchodilators (LABA e.g. salmeterol/formoterol/indacaterol; or LAMA e.g. tiotropium/glycopyrronium). LAMA preferred over LABA for dyspnea, exercise, exacerbations. LABA/LAMA dual: for CAT >10 and mMRC >2 with FEV1 <80%. LABA/LAMA/ICS triple: for high exacerbation risk with high symptom burden (CAT ≥10 and/or mMRC ≥2) and FEV1 <80%. Add-ons for persistent exacerbations despite triple therapy: macrolide, PDE4 inhibitor (roflumilast, chronic bronchitic phenotype), N-acetylcysteine (NAC, chronic bronchitic phenotype).

ICS monotherapy increases pneumonia risk in COPD; ICS only as part of combination. Theophylline has insufficient evidence and high toxicity. Roflumilast is a PDE4 inhibitor that reduces exacerbations in chronic bronchitic phenotype. LAMA-containing therapies are superior to therapies without LAMA for exacerbation reduction.

**Q:** What are the indications for LABA/LAMA/ICS triple therapy in COPD and what mortality benefit does it provide?

**A:** Indications: high exacerbation risk with high symptom burden and/or health status impairment (CAT ≥10 and/or mMRC ≥2) and FEV1 <80% predicted. Mortality benefit demonstrated in COPD patients meeting these criteria. Also used when LABA/LAMA fails to control symptoms, or for concomitant asthma.

ICS monotherapy is not recommended in COPD due to increased pneumonia risk. Triple therapy (LABA/LAMA/ICS) is superior to dual therapy for exacerbation reduction in high-risk patients. LABA/LAMA reduces moderate-to-severe exacerbations compared to LABA/ICS (HR 0.86) and LABA alone (HR 0.80) in high-risk populations.

**Q:** What is the management of acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD)?

**A:** Definition: sustained (>48h) worsening of dyspnea, cough, or sputum production. Management: ABCs + consider NIV early if high CO2/reduced pH; O2 target 88–92% for CO2 retainers; bronchodilators (SABA + anticholinergic via MDI/spacer or nebulizer); systemic corticosteroids (oral prednisone, 5 days is sufficient; IV methylprednisolone if absorption issues); antibiotics if purulent exacerbation (no risk factors: doxycycline/macrolide/amoxicillin; risk factors for resistance: amoxicillin-clavulanate or respiratory fluoroquinolone); pulmonary rehabilitation within 2 weeks post-exacerbation.

Etiology includes viral URTI, bacteria, air pollution, CHF, PE, MI. Systemic corticosteroids reduce treatment failure by >50% vs placebo (Cochrane 2014). 5 days of oral corticosteroids is likely sufficient (Cochrane 2018). ICU admission for life-threatening exacerbations; NIV (BiPAP) preferred over intubation when possible.

**Q:** What is the BODE index for COPD prognosis?

**A:** 10-point index predicting risk of death from COPD (greater score = higher mortality): B - Body mass index <21 (+1); O - Obstruction FEV1: 50–64% (+1), 36–49% (+2), <35% (+3); D - Dyspnea mMRC: walks slower than peers on level (+1), stops at 100 yards on level (+2), too breathless to leave house or dress (+3); E - Exercise capacity 6-min walk: 250–349 m (+1), 150–249 m (+2), <149 m (+3). Score also predicts hospitalization.

FEV1 alone is a poor predictor of COPD mortality. The BODE index incorporates functional, anthropometric, and exercise capacity data. Prognostic factors include: frequency/severity of exacerbations (single best predictor), lung function tests, mMRC scale, and development of hypoxemia/hypercapnia/cor pulmonale. 5-year survival: FEV1 <1L ≈ 50%, FEV1 <0.75L ≈ 33%.

**Q:** What target O2 saturation should be maintained in COPD patients with CO2 retention, and why?

**A:** Target O2 saturation 88–92%. Excess O2 can worsen hypercapnia through: 1) Worsening V/Q mismatch; 2) Haldane effect (O2 displaces CO2 from hemoglobin, increasing dissolved CO2); 3) Decreased hypoxic respiratory drive.

CO2 retainers have chronically elevated CO2 levels, usually with near-normal pH due to metabolic (bicarbonate) compensation. Giving high-flow O2 removes the hypoxic drive and worsens CO2 retention. The 88–92% target provides adequate oxygenation while avoiding CO2 accumulation.

**Q:** What are the complications of COPD?

**A:** 1) Chronic hypoxemia; 2) Polycythemia (secondary to hypoxemia); 3) Cor pulmonale (right heart failure); 4) Pneumothorax (rupture of bullae); 5) Depression; 6) COPD exacerbations; 7) Weight loss; 8) Pulmonary hypertension; 9) Respiratory failure.

Cor pulmonale results from pulmonary hypertension secondary to hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and vascular bed destruction. Polycythemia is a compensatory response to chronic hypoxemia. Spontaneous pneumothorax can be life-threatening in severe emphysema due to bullae rupture. PE occurs in 5.9% of COPD patients admitted for worsening symptoms.

**Q:** A 55-year-old male smoker presents with worsening dyspnea on exertion, cachectic appearance, pursed-lip breathing, and barrel chest. PFTs show FEV1/FVC 0.55, FEV1 42% predicted, TLC increased, RV increased, DLCO decreased. CXR shows hyperinflation, flat diaphragm, and bullae. What is the diagnosis and GOLD stage?

**A:** Emphysema (Pink Puffer phenotype). GOLD Stage 3 (Severe): FEV1 30–50% predicted.

The clinical picture is classic for emphysema: cachectic, pursed-lip breathing (auto-PEEP creation), barrel chest (increased TLC), hyperresonance, decreased breath sounds, ↓DLCO (destroyed alveolar-capillary units). FEV1/FVC <0.7 confirms obstructive defect. FEV1 42% = GOLD 3. These patients are pink (maintained oxygenation by increased ventilatory effort) and puffer (pursed-lip breathing). BODE index should guide prognosis.

**Q:** What are the indications for antibiotics in acute COPD exacerbations?

**A:** Antibiotics are indicated for purulent COPD exacerbations. For patients with NO risk factors for resistant organisms: any of doxycycline, macrolide, or amoxicillin. For patients WITH risk factors for resistant organisms: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid or respiratory fluoroquinolone (e.g., levofloxacin, moxifloxacin).

Purulence of sputum indicates bacterial infection and is the key trigger for antibiotic use. Risk factors for resistant organisms include: frequent exacerbations, recent antibiotics, severe COPD, immunosuppression, structural lung disease. Not all AECOPD are bacterial—viral URTI is a common cause; non-purulent exacerbations do not require antibiotics.

**Q:** What is the role of pulmonary rehabilitation in COPD management?

**A:** Pulmonary rehabilitation: may reduce mortality if offered within 2 weeks after hospitalization for acute COPD exacerbation. Should be offered to any patient with high symptom burden and/or frequent exacerbations to improve symptoms, quality of life, physical endurance, and to reduce exacerbations. Offered to patients with ≥2 moderate exacerbations or ≥1 severe acute exacerbation of COPD in the last year.

Pulmonary rehabilitation combines exercise training, self-management education, smoking cessation support, and active lifestyle promotion. It is one of the most effective non-pharmacologic interventions for COPD. Early rehabilitation post-exacerbation is key to preventing readmission. The benefits extend beyond exercise tolerance to include reduced anxiety and depression.

## Pneumonia & Lung Infections

**Q:** A 55-year-old presents with productive cough, fever, pleuritic chest pain, and CXR showing right lower lobe consolidation. WBC 18,000. How is severity assessed?

**A:** Use CURB-65 score: Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, RR ≥30, BP <90/60, Age ≥65; 0-1: outpatient; 2: inpatient; ≥3: ICU consideration

CTS CAP guidelines recommend CURB-65 to guide site of care decisions. Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) is an alternative. High-severity CAP (ICU) features include: shock, need for ventilation, multilobar infiltrates.

**Q:** What are the most common organisms causing community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)?

**A:** Typical: Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common), Haemophilus influenzae; Atypical: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila

S. pneumoniae accounts for ~30-40% of CAP requiring hospitalization. Atypical organisms are more common in younger, healthier patients. Legionella associated with cooling towers, travel, smokers.

**Q:** What is the empiric antibiotic treatment for outpatient CAP in a healthy adult without comorbidities?

**A:** Amoxicillin 1g TID (preferred) OR doxycycline 100mg BID — macrolone alone not recommended due to increasing resistance in Canada

CTS 2019 CAP guidelines recommend amoxicillin or doxycycline for outpatient CAP. Azithromycin alone no longer recommended due to S. pneumoniae macrolide resistance in Canada. Duration: 5-7 days for uncomplicated outpatient CAP.

**Q:** What antibiotic regimen is recommended for hospitalized non-ICU CAP?

**A:** Beta-lactam (ampicillin-sulbactam or ceftriaxone) PLUS azithromycin OR respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin/moxifloxacin) monotherapy

CTS guidelines recommend combination beta-lactam + macrolide or monotherapy with respiratory fluoroquinolone for hospitalized non-ICU CAP. Combination therapy covers atypicals and reduces mortality.

**Q:** A 70-year-old with CAP is hypotensive, requires ICU admission. What additional antibiotic coverage is needed?

**A:** Add anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam or cefepime) if Pseudomonas risk factors; otherwise ceftriaxone + azithromycin ± oseltamivir (if influenza season)

Severe CAP (ICU): beta-lactam + macrolide is standard. Anti-MRSA (vancomycin) added if post-influenza or necrotizing pneumonia. CTS recommends considering antifungal coverage in immunocompromised.

**Q:** What is the clinical presentation of Legionella pneumonia and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** High fever, headache, GI symptoms (diarrhea), confusion, hyponatremia, lymphopenia, elevated LFTs; diagnosis: urine Legionella antigen (90% sensitive for L. pneumophila serogroup 1)

Legionella is associated with cooling towers, hot tubs, travel. Gram stain shows no organisms despite clinical pneumonia. Treatment: respiratory fluoroquinolone or azithromycin. Reportable disease in Canada.

**Q:** What is aspiration pneumonia vs. aspiration pneumonitis?

**A:** Pneumonitis: chemical aspiration of gastric acid causing sterile inflammatory response — supportive care only; Pneumonia: aspiration of oropharyngeal bacteria requiring antibiotics

Aspiration pneumonitis usually resolves in 24-48 hours without antibiotics. Aspiration pneumonia develops when bacteria are aspirated, typically in patients with impaired swallowing (stroke, ALS, intoxication). Antibiotics: ampicillin-sulbactam or clindamycin (anaerobic coverage).

**Q:** What is the standard workup for hospitalized CAP?

**A:** Sputum Gram stain and culture, blood cultures x2, CBC, electrolytes, creatinine, LFTs, urine Legionella antigen, urine pneumococcal antigen, oxygen saturation, CXR

CTS guidelines recommend blood cultures for hospitalized CAP — positive in 5-14% but guide de-escalation. Urine antigens have high specificity. Respiratory viral panel (influenza, COVID) in season. Repeat CXR at 6 weeks to confirm resolution.

**Q:** When should a parapneumonic effusion be drained?

**A:** Drain if: pH <7.20, glucose <2.2 mmol/L, LDH >1000 IU/L, positive Gram stain or culture (empyema) — complicated parapneumonic effusion

CTS guidelines use Light criteria to characterize parapneumonic effusions. Empyema (pus in pleural space) requires drainage (thoracocentesis or chest tube). VATS decortication for loculated/organized empyema.

**Q:** What is hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) vs. ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)?

**A:** HAP: pneumonia developing ≥48h after hospital admission, not intubated; VAP: pneumonia developing ≥48h after endotracheal intubation — both require broader antibiotic coverage than CAP

HAP/VAP organisms include Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella, MRSA, Acinetobacter. Empiric treatment: anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam ± aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone ± MRSA coverage depending on risk.

**Q:** What is Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) and who gets it?

**A:** Fungal pneumonia in immunocompromised patients (HIV with CD4 <200, organ transplant, high-dose steroids); presents with subacute dyspnea, non-productive cough, bilateral interstitial infiltrates on CXR

Diagnosis: BAL with immunofluorescence, serum beta-D-glucan. Treatment: TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) is drug of choice; add prednisone if PaO2 <70 mmHg. Prophylaxis: TMP-SMX when CD4 <200.

**Q:** What is the CXR pattern in different pneumonia types?

**A:** Lobar/segmental consolidation: typical bacterial; Bilateral interstitial: atypical, viral, PCP; Cavitation: TB, Staph aureus, anaerobes, Klebsiella; Upper lobe: TB; Lower lobe: aspiration

Radiographic patterns guide differential diagnosis but overlap is common. All hospitalized CAP patients require follow-up CXR at 6 weeks to confirm resolution and exclude underlying malignancy in smokers >50.

**Q:** When is antifungal treatment indicated for pneumonia?

**A:** Invasive fungal pneumonia in immunocompromised: Aspergillus (voriconazole), Candida pneumonia (fluconazole or echinocandin), Cryptococcus (amphotericin B + flucytosine then fluconazole)

Aspergillus pneumonia occurs in prolonged neutropenia or high-dose steroids. Galactomannan assay and CT (halo sign) help diagnose invasive aspergillosis. Cryptococcal pneumonia is associated with HIV; diagnose with serum cryptococcal antigen.

**Q:** What are indications for influenza testing and treatment in pneumonia?

**A:** Test for influenza (rapid PCR or antigen test) during influenza season for all hospitalized pneumonia patients; treat with oseltamivir within 48 hours of symptom onset

CTS recommends oseltamivir for hospitalized patients with confirmed or suspected influenza regardless of symptom duration. Influenza pneumonia can cause severe respiratory failure. Secondary bacterial pneumonia (Staph aureus, S. pneumoniae) should be treated with antibiotics.

**Q:** When is follow-up CXR required after community-acquired pneumonia?

**A:** CXR at 6-8 weeks post-discharge for smokers, patients >50 years, or incomplete clinical resolution — to exclude underlying malignancy

Studies show lung cancer underlies CAP in up to 10-15% of adults >50. CTS CAP guidelines recommend routine follow-up imaging in higher-risk patients. CT chest is indicated if plain CXR does not clear at 6-8 weeks.

**Q:** What is CURB-65 and how does it guide CAP management?

**A:** Confusion, Urea>7, RR>=30, BP<90/60, age>=65. 0-1: outpatient; 2: admit; 3-5: ICU

PSI is more validated but complex. IDSA/ATS major criteria for ICU: ventilation or vasopressors.

**Q:** What organisms cause HAP vs CAP?

**A:** HAP: Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, MRSA. CAP: S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, atypicals

HAP >=48h after admission. Empiric: anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam +/- MRSA coverage.

**Q:** What is the CRB-65/CURB-65 score and how does it guide pneumonia disposition?

**A:** Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L (CURB only), RR ≥30, BP systolic <90 or diastolic ≤60, age ≥65. Score 0-1: outpatient; 2: consider admission; 3-5: ICU consideration.

CURB-65 is recommended by CTS for CAP severity assessment. CRB-65 (without urea) can be used in community settings. Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI/PORT) is more complex but better validated for identifying low-risk patients suitable for outpatient care.

**Q:** What is the empiric antibiotic treatment for outpatient CAP in a previously healthy adult with no comorbidities per CTS?

**A:** Amoxicillin 1g TID x 5 days (first-line) OR doxycycline 100 mg BID if penicillin-allergic. Macrolides only if local resistance <25%.

CTS 2022 updated guidelines move away from empiric macrolide monotherapy due to rising S. pneumoniae macrolide resistance (~25% in Canada). Amoxicillin covers most typical pathogens. 5-day courses are non-inferior to longer courses if clinically improving.

**Q:** What empiric therapy is recommended for inpatient CAP (non-ICU) per CTS guidelines?

**A:** Ampicillin IV + azithromycin OR respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin 750 mg daily or moxifloxacin 400 mg daily) monotherapy

CTS recommends beta-lactam plus macrolide combination to cover typical and atypical organisms. Respiratory fluoroquinolones are reserved for patients with beta-lactam allergy or treatment failure. Ceftriaxone 1-2g IV daily is an alternative beta-lactam.

**Q:** What defines hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) vs ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and what are the key pathogens?

**A:** HAP: ≥48h after admission, not incubating at admission. VAP: ≥48h after endotracheal intubation. Key pathogens: MRSA, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, ESBL Enterobacteriaceae (in addition to typical CAP organisms).

Risk factors for MDR organisms: IV antibiotics in prior 90 days, hospitalization ≥5 days, high local MRSA/Pseudomonas prevalence, septic shock. Empiric therapy should include anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam ± MRSA coverage based on risk factors.

**Q:** What empiric antibiotics are recommended for severe CAP requiring ICU admission?

**A:** Beta-lactam (ceftriaxone or ampicillin-sulbactam) PLUS azithromycin OR fluoroquinolone. If Pseudomonas risk: anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam (pip-tazo, cefepime, meropenem) + fluoroquinolone.

Dual therapy is mandatory for ICU-level CAP. If MRSA risk present, add vancomycin or linezolid. CTS recommends obtaining sputum and blood cultures before antibiotics for all ICU admissions. Consider Legionella urinary antigen and pneumococcal urinary antigen.

**Q:** What are the most common organisms causing CAP by patient population?

**A:** Overall: S. pneumoniae (#1), H. influenzae, M. pneumoniae, respiratory viruses. Elderly/comorbid: add S. aureus, gram-negatives. Alcoholics: Klebsiella, anaerobes. Post-influenza: S. aureus, S. pneumoniae.

Atypical organisms (Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila, Legionella) are more common in younger patients. Legionella should be suspected with GI symptoms, hyponatremia, and liver enzyme elevation. Viral causes (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV) are increasingly recognized.

**Q:** What are the indications for chest tube drainage in parapneumonic effusion/empyema?

**A:** Complicated parapneumonic: pH <7.20, glucose <3.3 mmol/L, LDH >1000, positive Gram stain/culture, frank pus (empyema), loculated effusion. All require chest tube ± fibrinolytics or VATS.

Light's criteria distinguish exudate from transudate. All parapneumonic effusions are exudates. Simple parapneumonic effusions (pH >7.20, sterile) can be treated with antibiotics alone. CTS recommends early thoracic surgery consultation for empyema or failed chest tube drainage.

**Q:** How is aspiration pneumonia different from aspiration pneumonitis and how is each managed?

**A:** Pneumonitis: chemical inflammation from gastric acid, onset hours, often self-limited, no antibiotics unless no improvement in 48h. Pneumonia: bacterial infection from oropharyngeal flora, onset days, requires antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin).

Aspiration pneumonia involves anaerobes and oral flora (Bacteroides, Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium). Risk factors: impaired consciousness, dysphagia, GERD, NG tube. Lung segments affected depend on position: supine → superior segment of RLL or posterior segment of RUL.

**Q:** What clinical features suggest Legionella pneumonia?

**A:** High fever >40°C, relative bradycardia, GI symptoms (diarrhea, nausea), hyponatremia, elevated CK, liver enzyme elevation, mental status changes. CXR may show rapidly progressive consolidation.

Legionella is transmitted via contaminated water systems (cooling towers, hospital water). Diagnosed by urinary antigen (detects serogroup 1 only, ~70% of cases) or culture on BCYE agar. Treated with azithromycin or fluoroquinolone (not beta-lactams).

**Q:** When should MRSA coverage be added to empiric CAP therapy?

**A:** Prior MRSA infection/colonization, recent IV antibiotics, severe CAP with necrotizing/cavitary infiltrate, empyema, concurrent influenza, local MRSA prevalence >20%. Add vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h or linezolid 600 mg IV/PO BID.

MRSA CAP (often PVL-producing CA-MRSA) can cause necrotizing pneumonia with rapid deterioration. Linezolid may be preferred as it inhibits toxin production. De-escalate once cultures return negative. Nasal MRSA PCR has high negative predictive value.

**Q:** What is the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI/PORT score) and how many classes are there?

**A:** 20 variables including age, comorbidities, exam findings, labs. Class I-III (≤90 points): outpatient. Class IV (91-130): inpatient. Class V (>130): ICU consideration.

PSI is better than CURB-65 at identifying low-risk patients. Class I: age <50, no comorbidities, normal vitals — no scoring needed. Limitations: may underestimate severity in young patients without comorbidities. CTS accepts either PSI or CURB-65 for disposition decisions.

**Q:** What is the appropriate duration of antibiotic therapy for uncomplicated CAP?

**A:** Minimum 5 days, provided: afebrile ≥48h, no more than 1 sign of clinical instability (HR >100, RR >24, SBP <90, SpO2 <90%). CTS recommends clinical stability-guided discontinuation.

Shorter courses (5 days) are non-inferior to longer courses (7-10 days) for uncomplicated CAP. Longer courses needed for: empyema, lung abscess, MRSA, Pseudomonas, or slow clinical response. Procalcitonin-guided therapy can safely reduce antibiotic duration.

**Q:** What are the risk factors and management of lung abscess?

**A:** Risk factors: aspiration (alcoholism, seizures, poor dentition), obstruction (tumor). Organisms: anaerobes, Klebsiella, S. aureus. Treatment: amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin x 4-6 weeks minimum until CXR resolution.

Most lung abscesses respond to prolonged antibiotics with postural drainage. CT-guided drainage or surgery if: failure to improve after 7 days of antibiotics, abscess >6 cm, suspected malignancy, massive hemoptysis. Bronchoscopy to rule out endobronchial obstruction.

**Q:** What CXR patterns help differentiate typical from atypical pneumonia?

**A:** Typical (bacterial): lobar consolidation, air bronchograms, pleural effusion common. Atypical (Mycoplasma, viral): diffuse bilateral interstitial/patchy infiltrates, less consolidation, effusion uncommon.

CXR patterns are suggestive but not diagnostic. S. pneumoniae classically causes lobar consolidation. Klebsiella may cause bulging fissure sign. Staphylococcal pneumonia can cause pneumatoceles. PCP in HIV shows bilateral perihilar ground-glass opacities.

**Q:** What are the prevention strategies for pneumonia in Canada?

**A:** Pneumococcal vaccination (PCV20 for high-risk), annual influenza vaccine, smoking cessation, oral hygiene, aspiration precautions, HOB elevation in ventilated patients, hand hygiene

PCV20 is now preferred over the sequential PCV15→PPSV23 approach for most adults per NACI. VAP prevention bundles include: HOB 30-45°, daily sedation interruption, DVT/peptic ulcer prophylaxis, oral chlorhexidine, and daily assessment for extubation readiness.

**Q:** What are the indications for chest X-ray vs CT chest?

**A:** CXR first-line: cough, dyspnea, chest pain, pneumonia, CHF, pneumothorax. CT chest: PE (CTPA), lung nodule characterization, staging, interstitial lung disease, aortic dissection

CXR findings: consolidation(pneumonia), Kerley B lines(CHF), bilateral hilar LAD(sarcoidosis), air-fluid levels(abscess), widened mediastinum(aortic dissection). CT angiography for PE: filling defect in pulmonary arteries.

**Q:** What are the pediatric antibiotic regimens for community-acquired pneumonia organized by age group?

**A:** <3 months: IV ceftriaxone. 3 months–5 years: if discharged = amoxicillin or cefprozil; if admitted = ceftriaxone. >5 years: ampicillin/amoxicillin OR cefuroxime. Common pathogens include: <3 mo = viral, S. pneumoniae, C. trachomatis, B. pertussis, S. aureus, H. influenzae; 3 mo–5 yr = viral, S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, H. influenzae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae; >5 yr = as above.

Atypical pathogens (Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila) become important after age 5 years and may require macrolide coverage. The most common bacterial cause across all pediatric ages is S. pneumoniae. Viral etiologies predominate in younger children, so antibiotics should be reserved for confirmed or highly suspected bacterial pneumonia.

## Pulmonary Embolism & DVT

**Q:** A 45-year-old woman on OCP presents with sudden onset dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain. HR 112, SpO2 92%, RR 24. ECG shows sinus tachycardia. What is the pretest probability and next step?

**A:** Calculate Wells PE score; moderate-high probability → CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA); low probability → D-dimer first

Wells PE criteria: clinical signs of DVT (3), no alternative diagnosis (3), HR >100 (1.5), immobilization/surgery (1.5), prior DVT/PE (1.5), hemoptysis (1), malignancy (1). Score >4 = moderate probability.

**Q:** What is the PERC rule and when can it safely exclude PE without further testing?

**A:** PERC: all 8 criteria must be met — age <50, HR <100, SpO2 ≥95%, no unilateral leg swelling, no hemoptysis, no recent surgery/trauma, no prior DVT/PE, no estrogen use — reduces PE miss rate to <2%

PERC rule is used in low pretest probability PE to avoid unnecessary testing. If any criterion is positive, proceed to D-dimer. CTS guidelines endorse PERC in low-risk patients to reduce overtesting.

**Q:** A pregnant woman has suspected PE. What is the preferred diagnostic imaging?

**A:** V/Q scan preferred in pregnancy (lower radiation dose to fetus); CTPA is alternative if CXR abnormal

SOGC and CTS guidelines recommend V/Q scan as first-line in pregnancy due to lower fetal radiation exposure. D-dimer is unreliable in pregnancy (physiologically elevated). Bilateral leg Doppler may avoid further imaging if positive.

**Q:** What is massive PE and how is it managed?

**A:** Massive PE: hemodynamic instability (SBP <90 mmHg or 40 mmHg drop ≥15 min) caused by PE — requires systemic thrombolysis (alteplase 100mg over 2h) or catheter-directed therapy/surgical embolectomy

CCS/CTS guidelines recommend systemic thrombolysis for massive PE without absolute contraindications. Anticoagulation alone is insufficient for hemodynamic compromise. ECMO as bridge in cardiac arrest.

**Q:** What are the preferred anticoagulants for acute PE management?

**A:** DOAC preferred (rivaroxaban or apixaban — no parenteral bridging needed); LMWH + warfarin if DOAC contraindicated; LMWH preferred in cancer-associated VTE (rivaroxaban/apixaban also acceptable per CTS 2023)

CTS VTE guidelines support DOAC monotherapy for most acute PE. Rivaroxaban 15mg BID x21 days then 20mg OD; apixaban 10mg BID x7 days then 5mg BID. Avoid DOACs in antiphospholipid syndrome (triple positive) — use warfarin.

**Q:** How long should anticoagulation be continued for PE?

**A:** Provoked PE (transient risk factor): 3 months; Unprovoked PE: minimum 3 months then reassess for extended therapy; Cancer-associated PE: until cancer resolved or indefinitely

CTS guidelines recommend extended anticoagulation for unprovoked PE if bleeding risk is low/moderate, as recurrence risk is 30% at 5 years. Risk stratification tools (DASH, Vienna score) guide duration decisions.

**Q:** What is the ECG finding classically described in PE and how sensitive is it?

**A:** S1Q3T3 pattern (S wave in lead I, Q wave and inverted T wave in lead III) — sensitivity only ~20%, non-specific

The most common ECG findings in PE are sinus tachycardia and right heart strain (RBBB, right axis deviation). S1Q3T3 is classically taught but rarely seen and not diagnostic. ECG primarily helps exclude other diagnoses (ACS, pericarditis).

**Q:** What is submassive (intermediate-risk) PE and when should thrombolysis be considered?

**A:** Submassive PE: hemodynamically stable but with RV dysfunction on echo/CT and/or elevated troponin/BNP; thrombolysis controversial — consider catheter-directed therapy in deteriorating patients

CTS guidelines recommend anticoagulation alone for most submassive PE. Escalation to thrombolysis or catheter-directed therapy (EKOS) for clinical deterioration. PEITHO trial showed benefit of thrombolysis offset by increased bleeding.

**Q:** A 55-year-old with DVT of the right leg is confirmed by Doppler. No symptoms of PE. How is DVT managed?

**A:** Anticoagulation with DOAC (rivaroxaban, apixaban) for 3 months for provoked, longer for unprovoked — LMWH + warfarin if DOAC contraindicated

CTS guidelines treat proximal DVT (above knee: popliteal, femoral, iliac) with systemic anticoagulation. Distal DVT (below-knee isolated) managed on individual basis — may monitor or treat depending on symptoms and risk. IVC filter not recommended routinely.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE)?

**A:** Strong: surgery, major trauma, hospitalization, prior VTE, cancer, antiphospholipid syndrome; Moderate: OCP, HRT, pregnancy, obesity; Weak: immobility, age, varicose veins

Virchow triad: stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury. Thrombophilia testing indicated in select patients: unprovoked VTE <50 years, family history, unusual site. Timing of testing: after anticoagulation discontinued.

**Q:** What is an IVC filter and when is it indicated?

**A:** IVC filter indicated when anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated AND patient has acute proximal DVT or PE — retrievable filters preferred

CTS guidelines limit IVC filter use to patients with absolute contraindication to anticoagulation. Filters prevent PE but increase DVT recurrence and do not reduce overall mortality. Must restart anticoagulation as soon as safe and retrieve filter if retrievable.

**Q:** What is chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Persistent pulmonary hypertension (mean PAP ≥25 mmHg) due to unresolved PE clots after ≥3 months of anticoagulation; diagnosed with V/Q scan and right heart catheterization

CTEPH occurs in 2-4% of PE survivors. V/Q scan is more sensitive than CTPA for CTEPH. Treatment: pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) is curative if operable; riociguat for inoperable disease. Lifelong anticoagulation required.

**Q:** When should thrombophilia testing be performed after VTE?

**A:** After completing anticoagulation (≥3 months), not during acute event or anticoagulation; test: Factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation, antiphospholipid antibodies, protein C/S, antithrombin

Anticoagulants affect thrombophilia tests: DOAC/heparin affects AT, warfarin affects protein C/S. Antiphospholipid antibodies should be repeated at 12 weeks to confirm diagnosis. Testing affects duration of anticoagulation decisions.

**Q:** What compression stocking regimen reduces post-thrombotic syndrome after DVT?

**A:** Graduated compression stockings (30-40 mmHg knee-high) for 2 years after proximal DVT — reduces PTS development

Post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS) occurs in 20-50% of proximal DVT and causes chronic leg pain, swelling, and skin changes. Elastic compression stockings reduce PTS severity. SOX trial found below-knee stockings do not reduce PTS in non-severe cases.

**Q:** A 60-year-old with unprovoked PE has completed 3 months of anticoagulation. What factors guide decision for extended therapy?

**A:** Low bleeding risk + unprovoked PE → extend anticoagulation indefinitely; weigh annual recurrence risk (5-10%/year) vs. major bleeding risk (~1-2%/year on DOAC)

CTS guidelines recommend extended anticoagulation for most unprovoked PE in patients with low-moderate bleeding risk. Low-dose rivaroxaban (10mg OD) or apixaban (2.5mg BID) extended therapy reduces recurrence with less bleeding than full-dose.

**Q:** What is the Wells score for PE?

**A:** DVT signs(3), PE most likely(3), HR>100(1.5), immobilization(1.5), prior VTE(1.5), hemoptysis(1), cancer(1). <=4 unlikely, >4 likely

If unlikely: D-dimer. If likely or D-dimer positive: CTPA. PERC rule excludes PE in very low risk.

**Q:** What defines massive PE and its treatment?

**A:** Sustained SBP<90 for >=15min or cardiac arrest. Treatment: systemic thrombolysis (alteplase 100mg IV/2h)

Submassive: RV dysfunction but stable. Consider catheter-directed therapy if thrombolysis contraindicated.

**Q:** What is the Wells score for pulmonary embolism and its components?

**A:** Clinical signs of DVT: 3 pts; PE most likely diagnosis: 3 pts; HR >100: 1.5; immobilization/surgery <4 wks: 1.5; previous DVT/PE: 1.5; hemoptysis: 1; malignancy: 1. ≤4: PE unlikely; >4: PE likely.

Wells score dichotomizes patients for D-dimer utility. If PE unlikely (≤4) + negative age-adjusted D-dimer → PE excluded. If PE likely (>4) → proceed directly to CTPA. Canadian Emergency Department guidelines recommend this two-tier approach.

**Q:** How is the age-adjusted D-dimer cutoff calculated and applied?

**A:** For patients >50 years: cutoff = age × 10 μg/L (e.g., 65-year-old: 650 μg/L instead of standard 500). Applied only when Wells score ≤4 (PE unlikely).

Age-adjusted D-dimer increases specificity while maintaining sensitivity, reducing unnecessary CTPAs in elderly by ~12%. Only validated for excluding PE in low/intermediate probability patients. D-dimer should never be used to rule IN PE.

**Q:** What is the PERC rule and when can it safely exclude PE without D-dimer?

**A:** If clinical suspicion is LOW (<15%) AND all 8 PERC criteria met: age <50, HR <100, SpO2 >94%, no hemoptysis, no estrogen use, no prior DVT/PE, no unilateral leg swelling, no recent surgery/trauma → PE excluded, no testing needed.

PERC rule has <2% miss rate when applied to truly low-pretest-probability patients. Must NOT be applied if Wells >4 or if clinical gestalt suggests PE. Reduces unnecessary testing and false-positive cascades. Validated in Canadian ED populations.

**Q:** What is the initial anticoagulation for confirmed acute PE?

**A:** DOAC preferred: rivaroxaban 15 mg BID x 21 days then 20 mg daily, OR apixaban 10 mg BID x 7 days then 5 mg BID. Alternative: LMWH bridge to warfarin (INR 2-3).

Thrombosis Canada and CCS guidelines recommend DOACs over warfarin for most PE patients (non-inferior efficacy, less major bleeding). Exceptions requiring LMWH/warfarin: antiphospholipid syndrome, severe renal impairment (CrCl <25-30), pregnancy (LMWH only).

**Q:** What defines massive (high-risk) PE and what is the treatment?

**A:** Massive PE: sustained hypotension (SBP <90 for >15 min) or requiring vasopressors, or cardiac arrest. Treatment: systemic thrombolysis (alteplase 100 mg IV over 2h) + anticoagulation + hemodynamic support.

Massive PE has >50% short-term mortality without treatment. Thrombolysis reduces mortality despite bleeding risk (major bleeding ~10%, ICH ~2%). If thrombolysis contraindicated or failed: catheter-directed therapy or surgical embolectomy. Thrombosis Canada recommends immediate thrombolysis.

**Q:** What is submassive PE and how is it managed?

**A:** Submassive: hemodynamically stable BUT RV dysfunction (RV/LV ratio >0.9 on CT or echo) AND/OR elevated troponin/BNP. Managed with anticoagulation + close monitoring; thrombolysis reserved for clinical deterioration.

sPESI score helps risk-stratify. Submassive PE has 3-15% mortality. The PEITHO trial showed reduced hemodynamic collapse with tenecteplase but increased ICH. Current approach: anticoagulate, monitor in intermediate care, escalate to thrombolysis only if decompensation occurs.

**Q:** What are the indications for IVC filter placement in PE?

**A:** Absolute contraindication to anticoagulation (active major bleeding) with acute proximal DVT/PE. Consider if recurrent PE despite adequate anticoagulation. Retrievable filters preferred; remove when anticoagulation can resume.

IVC filters do not treat PE — they prevent new emboli from legs. They increase DVT risk long-term. Thrombosis Canada recommends retrieval within 3 months. Prophylactic IVC filters are NOT recommended in trauma or surgical patients who can receive pharmacologic prophylaxis.

**Q:** How long should anticoagulation be continued after acute PE?

**A:** Provoked (surgery/transient risk): 3 months. Unprovoked first episode: minimum 3 months then reassess for indefinite (consider HERDOO2 for women). Recurrent unprovoked or active cancer: indefinite.

Thrombosis Canada recommends indefinite anticoagulation for most unprovoked PE if bleeding risk is low. HERDOO2 rule identifies low-risk women who may safely stop after initial treatment. Men with unprovoked PE have higher recurrence and generally warrant indefinite therapy.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of hemodynamic compromise in massive PE?

**A:** Large clot burden → acute RV pressure overload → RV dilation → interventricular septum bows into LV (D-sign) → decreased LV preload → decreased cardiac output → obstructive shock → coronary hypoperfusion → RV ischemia → death spiral

This explains why the primary cause of death in massive PE is RV failure, not hypoxemia. Acute RV cannot generate mean PA pressure >40 mmHg. Hypoxemia results from V/Q mismatch, right-to-left shunting through PFO, and low mixed venous O2.

**Q:** What are the classic but often absent ECG findings in PE?

**A:** Sinus tachycardia (most common), S1Q3T3 pattern (~20%), right axis deviation, RBBB, RV strain (T-wave inversions V1-V4), P pulmonale

S1Q3T3 is suggestive but not sensitive or specific for PE. Sinus tachycardia is the most common ECG finding. RV strain pattern (TWI V1-V4) correlates with RV dysfunction and worse prognosis. Normal ECG does not exclude PE. AFib can also occur acutely.

**Q:** When is V/Q scan preferred over CTPA for PE diagnosis?

**A:** Renal insufficiency (avoid contrast), contrast allergy, pregnancy (lower breast radiation than CTPA), young women. V/Q scan is most useful when CXR is normal (higher chance of diagnostic result).

V/Q scan reports as normal, low, intermediate, or high probability. High probability V/Q with high clinical suspicion has >95% PPV. Intermediate probability is non-diagnostic and may require further testing (CTPA or leg ultrasound). SPECT V/Q improves diagnostic accuracy.

**Q:** How is PE managed in pregnancy?

**A:** LMWH therapeutic dose throughout pregnancy (DOACs and warfarin contraindicated). Switch to IV UFH around delivery (shorter half-life). LMWH safe in breastfeeding. Postpartum: can transition to warfarin or DOAC (avoid rivaroxaban/apixaban if breastfeeding per older data).

Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state (5-10x VTE risk). CTPA or V/Q scan both acceptable for diagnosis. D-dimer rises in normal pregnancy, limiting its utility. LMWH does not cross placenta. Warfarin is teratogenic (6-12 weeks: nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses).

**Q:** What is chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) and how is it managed?

**A:** Persistent organized thrombi causing PH after ≥3 months of anticoagulation. Diagnosed by V/Q scan (screening test of choice), confirmed by right heart catheterization and pulmonary angiography. Treatment: pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) is curative if surgically accessible.

CTEPH develops in 2-4% of PE survivors. All PE patients should be assessed for persistent symptoms at 3-6 month follow-up. PEA at expert centres has excellent outcomes. Riociguat or balloon pulmonary angioplasty for inoperable cases. Lifelong anticoagulation required.

**Q:** What is the role of compression ultrasonography in the PE diagnostic algorithm?

**A:** If CTPA is unavailable or contraindicated, positive proximal leg CUS (DVT found) in a patient with suspected PE can justify starting anticoagulation without CTPA. Negative CUS does not exclude PE.

30-50% of PE patients have concurrent DVT detectable by CUS. A positive CUS changes management identically to confirmed PE (anticoagulation). Serial CUS at 1 week can be used for low-probability patients with non-diagnostic V/Q scans.

**Q:** What are the contraindications to systemic thrombolysis for PE?

**A:** Absolute: active internal bleeding, recent hemorrhagic stroke, intracranial neoplasm, recent cranial surgery (<3 months), suspected aortic dissection. Relative: recent surgery, recent non-hemorrhagic stroke, anticoagulant use, pregnancy, uncontrolled HTN.

In life-threatening massive PE, many relative contraindications can be overridden given the ~50% mortality without treatment. Half-dose thrombolysis (alteplase 50 mg) may be considered for submassive PE or when bleeding risk is elevated, though evidence is limited.

**Q:** A 30-year-old woman on oral contraceptives presents with acute dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and tachycardia after a long flight. Her D-dimer is elevated. What is the most likely diagnosis and the gold standard confirmatory test?

**A:** Pulmonary embolism (PE). CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the standard confirmatory imaging. V/Q scan is an alternative if CT contrast is contraindicated (e.g., renal failure, contrast allergy).

OCP use, prolonged immobilization, pleuritic pain, and tachycardia with elevated D-dimer strongly suggest PE. The Wells score helps stratify pre-test probability. A negative D-dimer in a low-probability patient effectively excludes PE, but a positive D-dimer requires imaging confirmation.

**Q:** How do you differentiate acute decompensated heart failure from pneumonia as a cause of acute dyspnea at the bedside?

**A:** CHF: orthopnea, PND, bilateral crackles, elevated JVP, S3 gallop, peripheral edema, BNP >400 pg/mL. Pneumonia: fever, productive cough, unilateral crackles/bronchial breath sounds, leukocytosis, consolidation on CXR. CHF shows bilateral pulmonary edema with cephalization on CXR.

BNP/NT-proBNP is a key discriminator — values >400 strongly suggest CHF, while <100 make it unlikely. CXR findings of Kerley B lines, bilateral effusions, and upper lobe cephalization favor CHF, while lobar consolidation with air bronchograms favors pneumonia.

**Q:** A 22-year-old tall, thin male presents with sudden-onset unilateral pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea. Breath sounds are absent on the left. What is the diagnosis, and when does this condition require emergent intervention?

**A:** Primary spontaneous pneumothorax. Emergent needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line) is required if tension pneumothorax is suspected (tracheal deviation, hemodynamic instability, distended neck veins). Otherwise, management depends on size: observation if small (<2 cm), aspiration or chest tube if large.

Tall, thin young males have the highest risk of primary spontaneous pneumothorax due to apical blebs. Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis — do not delay treatment for imaging. Secondary pneumothorax in patients with underlying lung disease (e.g., COPD) generally requires chest tube drainage.

**Q:** What is the systematic approach to differentiating the five major causes of acute dyspnea (PE, pneumonia, CHF, asthma, pneumothorax) using bedside assessment and initial investigations?

**A:** 1) History: onset (sudden = PE/pneumothorax), fever (pneumonia), orthopnea (CHF), wheezing/triggers (asthma). 2) Exam: unilateral absent breath sounds (pneumothorax), bilateral crackles + JVD (CHF), unilateral crackles + fever (pneumonia), diffuse wheeze (asthma). 3) Investigations: CXR (all), D-dimer/CTPA (PE), BNP (CHF), peak flow (asthma), ABG (all).

A structured approach combining tempo of onset, associated symptoms, physical exam, and targeted investigations allows rapid differentiation. CXR is the single most useful initial test as it can identify pneumothorax, CHF, and pneumonia. Normal CXR with hypoxia should raise concern for PE.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of pulmonary embolism causing hemodynamic compromise?

**A:** Large PE obstructs pulmonary vasculature, increasing PVR, causing RV pressure overload, RV dilation, decreased LV filling (interventricular septum shift), reduced CO, and systemic hypotension

Massive PE: >50% vascular bed occluded. Humoral mediators (serotonin, thromboxane) also cause vasoconstriction. RV ischemia develops from increased wall stress and decreased coronary perfusion pressure.

**Q:** Interpret CHADS2 scores and their clinical implications

**A:** Score 0: Low risk (~1.9%/yr) - no anticoagulation needed. Score 1: Low-moderate risk (~2.8%/yr) - anticoagulation may be considered. Scores 4-6: High risk (~8.5-18.2%/yr) - anticoagulation strongly indicated.

This risk stratification helps guide the decision to initiate anticoagulation. The benefit of anticoagulation must be weighed against bleeding risk in each patient.

**Q:** How do heparins (unfractionated and LMWH) work?

**A:** They are antithrombin III agonists, leading to decreased clotting factor activity (primarily factor IIa and Xa)

LMWHs like enoxaparin have more predictable pharmacokinetics and less risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia compared to unfractionated heparin.

**Q:** What is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and how does it differ between UFH and LMWH?

**A:** HIT is an immune-mediated reaction where antibodies form against heparin-platelet factor 4 complexes, causing platelet activation and thrombosis. HIT is less common with LMWHs than unfractionated heparin.

HIT typically occurs 5-10 days after heparin exposure. Suspicion of HIT requires immediate heparin discontinuation and alternative anticoagulation.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of heparins (unfractionated and LMWH)?

**A:** Antithrombin III agonist, leading to decreased clotting factor activity (primarily factors IIa and Xa)

UFH and LMWHs provide immediate anticoagulation, used for ACS, PE, and venous thrombosis.

**Q:** What are the key components of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism management according to referenced guidelines?

**A:** Diagnosis, prophylaxis, and treatment of DVT and PE

**Q:** What are the key management strategies for deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Diagnosis, prophylaxis, and treatment including anticoagulation and thrombolysis in selected cases

Alexander and Giangola reviewed diagnosis, prophylaxis, and treatment approaches for DVT and PE in vascular surgery literature.

**Q:** What is the clinical use of continuous IV infusion?

**A:** Continuous infusion is used for drugs with a very short half-life when there is a need for long-term effect and multiple/frequently repeated doses would be inconvenient (e.g., nitroprusside, unfractionated heparin, naloxone).

Continuous infusions maintain steady drug levels and avoid the peaks and valleys associated with intermittent dosing.

**Q:** What is the management of tension pneumothorax in the ED?

**A:** Needle decompression: 2nd intercostal space (ICS) at mid-clavicular line OR 5th ICS in mid-axillary line if patient is unstable. Follow with finger thoracostomy; urgent surgical consult and chest tube (4th ICS). Tracheal deviation is away from the tension pneumothorax (toward non-tension side).

Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis — do not delay treatment for imaging. Air accumulates under pressure in the pleural space, causing mediastinal shift, decreased venous return, and cardiovascular collapse. The 5th ICS mid-axillary line is increasingly preferred over the 2nd ICS mid-clavicular line as it is more reliable and avoids vascular structures.

**Q:** What is the Wells criteria used for in the context of chest pain evaluation and what diagnostic test follows?

**A:** Wells criteria is used for pulmonary embolism (PE) risk stratification. Based on Wells score: low probability + D-dimer <1000 ng/mL can rule out PE without imaging; moderate probability requires D-dimer <500 ng/mL to rule out. If D-dimer elevated or high probability: CT pulmonary angiogram (most sensitive) or V/Q scan.

The YEARS algorithm and PERC criteria are alternative PE risk stratification tools. CT pulmonary angiogram is the gold standard for PE diagnosis. D-dimer is highly sensitive but non-specific — useful to rule out PE in low-probability patients. Massive PE (hypotension + cardiovascular collapse) warrants thrombolysis consideration.

**Q:** What is the management of a primary spontaneous pneumothorax in the ED?

**A:** Unstable: needle decompression at 2nd ICS mid-clavicular line (or 5th ICS mid-axillary line) → finger thoracostomy → chest tube (4th ICS). Stable moderate-to-large: conservative management (observation) is non-inferior to immediate interventional management and results in fewer serious adverse events. Small primary spontaneous pneumothorax: observation and discharge with follow-up may be appropriate.

A landmark 2020 NEJM trial showed conservative management of primary spontaneous pneumothorax was non-inferior to interventional management (re-expansion within 8 weeks: 95% vs. 94%), with lower risk of serious adverse events and recurrence. This has shifted practice toward conservative management in stable patients.

**Q:** What are the components of Virchow's triad and the clinical risk factors for VTE?

**A:** Virchow's triad: alterations in blood flow (venous stasis), endothelial injury (smoking, HTN, surgery, catheter, trauma), hypercoagulable state (pregnancy, OCP, malignancy). Risk factors mnemonic CT MARRBS OI: Cancer, Trauma/travel, Medications (OCPs), Age >60, Recent surgery, Reduced mobility, Blood disorders, Birth control, Surgery, Obesity/obstetrics, Immobilization, Sickness (CHF, MI, nephrotic syndrome, vasculitis).

Understanding risk factors guides pre-test probability scoring (Wells criteria for DVT and PE). High-risk features increase the pre-test probability and influence whether D-dimer or direct imaging is warranted. Hypercoagulable states (thrombophilia) should be investigated in unprovoked or recurrent VTE.

**Q:** How is D-dimer used in the diagnosis of DVT and PE, and what are its limitations?

**A:** D-dimer is only useful for RULING OUT DVT/PE — it has high sensitivity but low specificity. A negative D-dimer in a low-to-moderate risk patient excludes DVT/PE. False positives occur in: elderly, infection, recent surgery, trauma, hemorrhage, late pregnancy, liver disease, cancer.

D-dimer measures fibrin degradation products and is elevated whenever clot is being broken down — hence its low specificity. In high-risk patients (high Wells score), D-dimer adds little value and imaging should proceed directly. Age-adjusted D-dimer cutoffs (age x 10 ng/mL in patients >50 years) improve specificity.

**Q:** What is the Wells criteria scoring for DVT and how does it guide management?

**A:** Wells DVT criteria include: active cancer (+1), paralysis/plaster cast (+1), bedridden >3 days or surgery in 4 wk (+1), tenderness along deep venous system (+1), entire leg swollen (+1), calf swelling ≥3 cm vs contralateral (+1), pitting edema of symptomatic leg (+1), collateral superficial veins (+1), alternative diagnosis as likely or more likely (−2). Score ≤0: low probability; 1–2: moderate; ≥3: high probability.

Low probability + negative D-dimer: DVT excluded. Low/moderate + negative D-dimer: DVT excluded. Positive D-dimer or high probability: proceed to compression ultrasound. If US negative and moderate-high risk: repeat US in 5–7 days. US has 73% sensitivity for calf DVT (higher for proximal clots).

**Q:** What is the PERC score and how is it used for PE evaluation?

**A:** PERC (Pulmonary Embolism Rule-out Criteria) score: Age <50, HR <100, O2 sat ≥95% on room air, no prior DVT/PE, no recent trauma or surgery, no hemoptysis, no exogenous estrogen, no clinical signs of DVT. If all 8 criteria met (score = 0) in a LOW-risk patient, PE is excluded without further investigation.

PERC is only valid in patients with low pre-test gestalt probability of PE. If any criterion is positive (score ≥1), proceed to D-dimer. PERC should not be used in pregnant women or patients on beta-blockers (excluded from original study). The YEARS algorithm is useful in pregnant patients.

**Q:** What are the radiological signs of PE on CXR and ECG?

**A:** CXR (rare, specific): Hampton's hump (triangular/wedge-shaped density abutting pleura — pulmonary infarct), Westermark's sign (abrupt tapering/dilatation of vessels proximal to obstruction with collapse distally). CXR often normal. ECG: sinus tachycardia (most common), S1Q3T3 pattern (right heart strain), T-wave inversions in anterior and inferior leads, AFib.

CXR and ECG are primarily useful to exclude other diagnoses (ACS, pneumonia, pneumothorax) and provide supporting evidence for PE rather than to diagnose PE definitively. About 50% of CXRs show only non-specific findings (effusion, atelectasis, infiltrates). CT pulmonary angiography is the gold standard for diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the indications for thrombolysis in pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Massive PE defined as acute PE with sustained hypotension (sBP <90 mmHg for ≥15 min, or requiring inotropic support, not due to another cause). Catheter-directed thrombolysis or surgical thrombectomy may be considered in massive PE or if anticoagulation is contraindicated.

Submassive PE (right ventricular dysfunction without hypotension) is a gray zone — systemic thrombolysis carries bleeding risk and current guidelines do not recommend it routinely. The treatment duration for PE is the same as DVT: 3 months if transient cause, 6 months if unprovoked, lifelong if ongoing coagulopathy.

**Q:** What is the DOAC dosing for DVT/PE treatment?

**A:** Rivaroxaban: 15 mg PO BID for first 21 days, then 20 mg PO once daily. Apixaban: 10 mg PO BID for first 7 days, then 5 mg PO BID. LMWH alternative: dalteparin 200 IU/kg SC q24h or enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC q24h (avoid in renal failure).

DOACs are preferred over LMWH for most patients with DVT/PE due to equivalent efficacy, better convenience, and no requirement for monitoring. LMWH may be preferred in cancer-associated VTE (check local guidelines). IVC filter or surgical thrombectomy if anticoagulation contraindicated. Calf/distal DVT management varies — serial venous Doppler vs. anticoagulation are both options.

**Q:** What is the YEARS algorithm for PE evaluation, and in which patients is it particularly useful?

**A:** YEARS items: clinical signs of DVT, hemoptysis, PE as most likely diagnosis. If 0 YEARS items AND D-dimer <1000 ng/mL → PE excluded. If ≥1 YEARS item AND D-dimer <500 ng/mL → PE excluded. All other patients → CTPA. Particularly useful in hemodynamically stable patients >18 years and pregnant patients (if DVT excluded).

The YEARS algorithm uses a higher D-dimer threshold (1000 ng/mL) in patients with no YEARS items, which reduces CT scanning without compromising safety. It was validated in pregnant patients where radiation exposure is a concern. V/Q scan is preferred over CTPA in pregnancy when available.

## Interstitial Lung Disease & Sarcoidosis

**Q:** A 35-year-old Black woman presents with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on CXR, erythema nodosum, and elevated ACE. Diagnosis and staging?

**A:** Sarcoidosis; Stage I: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy alone; Stage II: BHL + pulmonary infiltrates; Stage III: infiltrates without BHL; Stage IV: pulmonary fibrosis

Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease. Definitive diagnosis requires biopsy showing non-caseating granulomas (exclude TB, fungal). ACE is elevated in ~60%. Calcium elevated in ~10%. Black patients have more severe disease.

**Q:** What are the extrapulmonary manifestations of sarcoidosis?

**A:** Eyes (uveitis, most common), skin (erythema nodosum, lupus pernio), heart (conduction block, VT), nervous system (cranial nerve palsies, esp CN VII), liver, kidneys, bones

Cardiac sarcoidosis causes sudden death from VT/heart block — all sarcoidosis patients need ECG. Neurosarcoidosis: CN VII palsy is most common. Lofgren syndrome (bilateral hilar adenopathy + erythema nodosum + polyarthritis) has excellent prognosis.

**Q:** When is treatment indicated for pulmonary sarcoidosis?

**A:** Symptomatic pulmonary disease, declining lung function (FVC decline >10%), hypercalcemia, cardiac/neurological sarcoidosis, severe skin involvement

Not all sarcoidosis requires treatment. Stage I and II asymptomatic may be observed. Corticosteroids (prednisone 20-40mg) are first-line for progressive or organ-threatening disease. Steroid-sparing agents: methotrexate, azathioprine, hydroxychloroquine.

**Q:** What is Lofgren syndrome and what is its prognosis?

**A:** Acute sarcoidosis triad: bilateral hilar adenopathy + erythema nodosum/periarthritis + fever; excellent prognosis — self-limited, resolves spontaneously in >90%

Lofgren syndrome is the most common acute presentation of sarcoidosis in Northern Europeans. It usually resolves within 2 years without treatment. NSAIDs for symptomatic relief. This presentation has better prognosis than insidious-onset sarcoidosis.

**Q:** What is interstitial lung disease (ILD) and what are the main categories?

**A:** ILD: diffuse parenchymal lung diseases causing alveolar inflammation and/or fibrosis; categories: idiopathic (IPF, NSIP), connective tissue disease-associated, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, drug-induced, sarcoidosis

ILD presents with progressive dyspnea, dry cough, bilateral crackles, and restrictive spirometry. HRCT is essential for pattern recognition. IPF has the worst prognosis; most other ILDs respond to immunosuppression.

**Q:** What HRCT pattern characterizes idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and what is its treatment?

**A:** Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern on HRCT: bilateral basal-predominant subpleural honeycombing ± traction bronchiectasis; treat with nintedanib or pirfenidone (antifibrotic agents)

IPF is the most common ILD with median survival 3-5 years from diagnosis. Diagnosis requires exclusion of other causes. Antifibrotics slow FVC decline but do not reverse fibrosis. Lung transplantation is the only curative option.

**Q:** What is hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Immune-mediated ILD from repeated inhalation of organic antigens (bird droppings, mold, farmer lung — thermophilic actinomyces); diagnose with exposure history, serum precipitins, HRCT, BAL lymphocytosis ± lung biopsy

Chronic HP can progress to fibrosis (chronic HP with fibrotic UIP pattern). Treatment: antigen avoidance (most important), corticosteroids for acute/subacute forms. Smoking paradoxically protective for HP development but worsens prognosis if fibrosis develops.

**Q:** What are classic features of pulmonary sarcoidosis?

**A:** Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, non-caseating granulomas, elevated ACE, erythema nodosum

Lofgren syndrome has excellent prognosis. Stages I-IV. Most stage I resolves spontaneously.

**Q:** What are the Scadding stages of pulmonary sarcoidosis on CXR?

**A:** Stage 0: normal CXR; Stage I: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (BHL) alone; Stage II: BHL + pulmonary infiltrates; Stage III: pulmonary infiltrates without BHL; Stage IV: pulmonary fibrosis with volume loss.

Stage predicts spontaneous remission: Stage I: 60-80% resolve spontaneously; Stage II: ~50%; Stage III: <30%; Stage IV: 0% (irreversible). Stage does not correlate perfectly with symptoms or lung function. Treatment decisions based on symptoms and organ involvement, not stage alone.

**Q:** What is the classic histological finding in sarcoidosis?

**A:** Non-caseating (non-necrotizing) granulomas composed of epithelioid cells, multinucleated giant cells (may contain Schaumann bodies and asteroid bodies), surrounded by lymphocytes and fibroblasts.

Non-caseating granulomas are the hallmark but NOT pathognomonic — must exclude infections (TB, fungi), foreign body, and lymphoma. Tissue biopsy (transbronchial, lymph node, or skin) is required for diagnosis. Kveim test is historical and no longer used.

**Q:** What are the extrapulmonary manifestations of sarcoidosis?

**A:** Eyes: anterior uveitis (most common), lacrimal gland. Skin: erythema nodosum, lupus pernio. Liver: granulomatous hepatitis. Heart: conduction blocks, cardiomyopathy. Neuro: CN VII palsy, meningitis. Joints: acute polyarthritis. Kidney: nephrocalcinosis (hypercalcemia).

Sarcoidosis is a systemic disease requiring multi-organ assessment. Löfgren syndrome (BHL + erythema nodosum + fever + polyarthralgia) has excellent prognosis >90% resolution. Cardiac sarcoid is a major cause of death — screen with ECG ± advanced imaging.

**Q:** Why does sarcoidosis cause hypercalcemia and how is it managed?

**A:** Activated macrophages in granulomas express 1α-hydroxylase, converting 25-OH vitamin D to 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D (calcitriol) independently of PTH → increased intestinal calcium absorption and bone resorption. Managed with corticosteroids + hydration + low-calcium diet.

Hypercalcemia occurs in ~10% and hypercalciuria in ~30% of sarcoidosis patients. Can cause nephrocalcinosis and renal failure. Unlike primary hyperparathyroidism, PTH is suppressed. Ketoconazole can also reduce calcitriol production. Avoid excessive sun exposure and vitamin D supplements.

**Q:** When is systemic corticosteroid therapy indicated in sarcoidosis?

**A:** Symptomatic pulmonary disease (worsening dyspnea, declining FVC), hypercalcemia, cardiac sarcoid, neurosarcoidosis, sight-threatening uveitis, disfiguring skin lesions, hepatic dysfunction

Not all sarcoidosis requires treatment — many cases self-resolve. Initial therapy: prednisone 20-40 mg daily for 4-6 weeks, then taper over 6-12 months. Monitor PFTs and imaging for response. Steroid-sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine) for relapsing or chronic disease.

**Q:** What lab findings support a diagnosis of sarcoidosis?

**A:** Elevated ACE (60% sensitivity, not specific), elevated serum and urine calcium, elevated 1,25-(OH)2-vitamin D, lymphopenia, elevated ESR, elevated alkaline phosphatase, polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia

**A:** Uveoparotid fever: anterior uveitis + parotid gland enlargement + facial nerve palsy (CN VII) + fever

**A:** Restrictive pattern (reduced TLC, FVC) with reduced DLCO is most common. Obstructive pattern can occur with endobronchial granulomas. Mixed pattern possible. Stage I may have normal PFTs.

**Q:** What steroid-sparing agents are used in refractory or chronic sarcoidosis?

**A:** First-line steroid-sparing: methotrexate (most evidence), azathioprine. Second-line: mycophenolate, leflunomide. Third-line: anti-TNF biologics (infliximab) for refractory disease.

**Q:** How is cardiac sarcoidosis screened for and diagnosed?

**A:** Screen all sarcoidosis patients with ECG. If abnormal or symptoms (palpitations, syncope, HF): cardiac MRI (gadolinium enhancement in active disease) or FDG-PET. Endomyocardial biopsy has low sensitivity (~25%) due to patchy involvement.

Cardiac sarcoid is the leading cause of sarcoidosis-related death in Japan and second in North America. Can cause complete heart block, VT, sudden death, and dilated cardiomyopathy. Treatment: corticosteroids + ICD if LVEF <35% or sustained VT. Annual ECG recommended for all.

**A:** Lofgren syndrome: acute sarcoidosis presentation with classic triad of bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy + erythema nodosum + polyarthralgia (especially ankles) ± fever. Acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis presents 4-6 hours after antigen exposure with dyspnea, cough, fever, chills, and malaise lasting 18-24 hours. Farmer's lung is caused by sensitization to Thermophilic actinomycetes in moldy hay. Acute/subacute HP is mediated by immune complex formation, while chronic HP involves type IV hypersensitivity.

**Q:** What are the two acute sarcoidosis syndromes and their clinical features?

**A:** Löfgren's syndrome: fever, erythema nodosum, bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, arthralgias. Heerfordt-Waldenström syndrome: fever, parotid enlargement, anterior uveitis, facial nerve palsy.

Sarcoidosis is an idiopathic non-infectious granulomatous multi-system disease with lung involvement in 90%. It typically affects young and middle-aged patients and is characterized by non-necrotizing granulomas. Serum ACE levels are elevated but non-specific. 85% of stage I disease resolves spontaneously within 2 years.

**Q:** What CXR staging system is used for sarcoidosis?

**A:** Stage 0: normal. Stage I: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (BHL). Stage II: BHL + pulmonary infiltrates. Stage III: pulmonary infiltrates alone. Stage IV: pulmonary fibrosis.

Sarcoidosis staging is based on CXR findings. Stage I has the best prognosis with 85% spontaneous resolution within 2 years. Stage II resolves spontaneously in 50% within 5 years. Treatment is indicated for symptoms, declining lung function, hypercalcemia, or eye/CNS/kidney/heart involvement, not for abnormal CXR alone.

**Q:** A patient with suspected PE has a low clinical probability. The D-dimer is negative. What is the next step?

**A:** PE is ruled out; no further testing needed.

D-dimer is highly sensitive and can exclude DVT/PE when pretest probability is already low. A negative D-dimer in low clinical probability effectively rules out PE. If D-dimer is positive or clinical probability is moderate/high, CT pulmonary angiography is indicated. The PERC criteria can also help exclude PE in very low-risk patients without any testing.

**Q:** What landmark trial demonstrated that low tidal volume ventilation (6 mL/kg) reduces mortality in ARDS compared to traditional ventilation (12 mL/kg)?

**A:** The ARDS Network trial (NEJM 2000)

The ARDS Network trial showed that low tidal volume ventilation (6 mL/kg predicted body weight) reduced mortality from 39.8% to 31.0% and increased ventilator-free days in patients with ARDS. This established lung-protective ventilation as the standard of care for ARDS management.

**Q:** The PROSEVA trial demonstrated what benefit of prone positioning in severe ARDS?

**A:** Early prone positioning (>16 h/day) reduced 28-day mortality from 32.8% to 16.0% in severe ARDS

The PROSEVA trial (NEJM 2013) randomized patients with severe ARDS to prone positioning for >16 hours or supine position. Prone positioning decreased 28-day mortality (HR 0.39) and 90-day mortality (23.6% vs 41.0%). The incidence of complications did not differ significantly between groups.

**Q:** What is the BODE index and what does it predict in COPD?

**A:** A 10-point index predicting risk of death in COPD using: Body mass index (<21), Obstruction (FEV1), Dyspnea (mMRC), and Exercise capacity (6-min walk distance).

The BODE index helps predict mortality in COPD patients. Higher scores indicate greater probability of death from COPD. FEV1 <1L gives 50% 5-year survival, and FEV1 <0.75L gives 33% 5-year survival. The frequency and severity of acute exacerbations is the single best predictor of prognosis.

**Q:** What is the approach to hypoxemic respiratory failure? List the types and their response to supplemental oxygen.

**A:** Types: (1) Low FiO2 - improves with O2. (2) Hypoventilation - improves with O2 and ventilation. (3) Physiologic shunt/V-Q mismatch - less effective with O2, improves with ventilation/PEEP. (4) Anatomic R-to-L shunt - O2 has no effect. (5) Low mixed venous O2 - improves with increased cardiac output. (6) Diffusion impairment - improves with O2.

Hypoxemic respiratory failure is defined as PaO2 <60 mmHg with normal or decreased PaCO2. A-a gradient helps distinguish causes: normal A-a gradient in low FiO2 and hypoventilation, elevated A-a gradient in shunt, V/Q mismatch, and diffusion impairment. True anatomic shunt (right-to-left) does not respond to supplemental O2.

**Q:** What mnemonic helps remember asthma triggers?

**A:** THE ACHES: Temperature/humidity, Humidity, Exercise, Allergens (pet dander, dust mites, cockroaches, seasonal), Cigarette smoke/air pollution, Hormonal fluctuations, Emotion/anxiety, Strong scents, plus URTIs, GERD, NSAIDs (Samter's triad), beta-blockers.

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder with reversible bronchospasm affecting 10.8% of Canadians. Samter's triad is the combination of asthma, NSAID sensitivity, and nasal polyps. Non-cardioselective beta-blockers are especially dangerous. Well-controlled asthma has daytime symptoms <2 d/wk, night symptoms <1 night/wk, FEV1 ≥90% personal best, and SABA use <2 times/wk.

**Q:** What is mesothelioma and what is the primary risk factor?

**A:** Mesothelioma is a primary malignancy of the pleura that develops decades after asbestos exposure, even with limited exposure. Smoking is NOT a risk factor for mesothelioma, but asbestos and smoking synergistically increase lung cancer risk.

Mesothelioma presents with chest pain, dyspnea, cough, bloody pleural effusion, and weight loss. Paraneoplastic syndromes may include hypercalcemia, hemolytic anemia, and hypoglycemia. Needle biopsy may seed the needle tract with tumor. Treatment involves multimodal approach (chemotherapy, radiation, surgical resection), but prognosis is poor.

**Q:** What is the definition of asthma and what are the PFT criteria for diagnosis?

**A:** Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disorder with reversible bronchospasm. Diagnosis requires: FEV1/FVC below lower limit of normal AND FEV1 increase ≥12% (and >200 mL in adults) after bronchodilator, or positive methacholine challenge (FEV1 decrease ≥20% at dose <4 mg/mL).

Asthma affects 10.8% of Canadians. It is often associated with atopy. Work-related asthma includes occupational asthma from sensitizer exposure. A significant bronchodilator response is being redefined as >10% of predicted value.

**Q:** What is the stepwise approach to asthma management?

**A:** Step 1: Low-dose ICS (or ICS-formoterol PRN for ages ≥12). Step 2: Add LABA to low-dose ICS (combination inhaler). Step 3: Escalate ICS dose. Step 4: Consider LTRA, long-acting anticholinergics, oral corticosteroids, biologics (anti-IgE, anti-IL5, anti-IL-4/13).

Any patient with poor control or at risk of exacerbations should be on an ICS-containing regimen. Risk of exacerbation includes history of prior exacerbation requiring steroids/ED/hospitalization, poorly controlled asthma, SABA overuse (>2 inhalers/year), or current smoking.

**Q:** What is the spirometric definition of COPD?

**A:** Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 (or below lower limit of normal).

COPD is progressive and irreversible, with two phenotypes: chronic bronchitis (productive cough most days for ≥3 consecutive months in 2 successive years) and emphysema (dilation and destruction of air spaces distal to terminal bronchioles). Smoking is the #1 risk factor in Western countries.

**Q:** What three interventions prolong survival in COPD?

**A:** 1. Smoking cessation, 2. Home oxygen (>15 h/d if PaO2 <55 mmHg or 56-59 with cor pulmonale/polycythemia), 3. LAMA/LABA/ICS triple therapy (in patients at high risk of exacerbations with FEV1 <80% predicted).

Smoking cessation is the single most important intervention. Annual influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations are also recommended. Pulmonary rehabilitation may reduce mortality if offered within 2 weeks after hospitalization for an acute exacerbation.

**Q:** What is the BODE index in COPD?

**A:** A 10-point index predicting mortality using: Body mass index (<21 = 1 pt), Obstruction/FEV1 (50-64% = 1, 36-49% = 2, <35% = 3), Dyspnea/mMRC scale (1-3 pts), Exercise capacity/6-min walk distance (1-3 pts).

A higher BODE score indicates higher probability of death from COPD. It can also predict hospitalization. The frequency and severity of acute exacerbations is the single best predictor of prognosis in COPD.

**Q:** What oxygen saturation target should be maintained for COPD patients who are CO2 retainers?

**A:** 88-92% SaO2.

CO2 retainers have chronically elevated CO2 with near-normal pH due to metabolic compensation. Excessive oxygen can worsen hypercapnia through worsening V/Q mismatch, the Haldane effect, and decreased respiratory drive. However, oxygen should not be withheld if the patient is hypoxic.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for IPF (Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis)?

**A:** Pulmonary fibrosis of unknown cause with UIP pattern on CT (reticulation, subpleural/basal predominant, honeycombing + traction bronchiectasis) or biopsy, in patients typically >50 years.

IPF is the most common ILD. It is progressive and irreversible. Treatment includes antifibrotic therapy (pirfenidone or nintedanib), smoking cessation, pulmonary rehab, and lung transplantation for advanced disease. Biopsy is only needed if CT shows indeterminate or alternative diagnosis pattern.

**Q:** What are the radiographic stages of sarcoidosis?

**A:** Stage 0: normal CXR. Stage I: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy. Stage II: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy + pulmonary infiltrates. Stage III: pulmonary infiltration alone. Stage IV: pulmonary fibrosis.

85% of Stage I and 50% of Stage II resolve spontaneously. Treatment (prednisone) is indicated for symptoms, declining lung function, hypercalcemia, or involvement of eyes, CNS, kidney, or heart -- not for abnormal CXR alone. Sarcoidosis is characterized by non-caseating granulomas.

**Q:** What are the CXR patterns that distinguish asbestosis from silicosis?

**A:** Asbestosis: lower lobe > upper lobe involvement. Silicosis: upper lobe > lower lobe involvement.

Asbestosis causes slowly progressive diffuse interstitial fibrosis with possible pleural plaques and calcifications. Asbestos exposure increases risk of bronchogenic carcinoma and malignant mesothelioma, synergistically with smoking. Silicosis shows nodular disease that can coalesce into progressive massive fibrosis.

**Q:** What is the Wells score for pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Clinical signs/symptoms of DVT (3.0), PE more likely than alternative (3.0), immobilization/surgery in past 4 weeks (1.5), HR >100 (1.5), previous DVT/PE (1.5), hemoptysis (1.0), malignancy (1.0). Low <2, intermediate 2-6, high >6.

Clinical probability determines the next diagnostic step. A negative D-dimer can exclude PE if pretest probability is low. CTA has 83% sensitivity and 96% specificity but PPV varies by pretest probability. V/Q scan is used when CT is contraindicated.

**Q:** What is the long-term anticoagulation strategy for pulmonary embolism?

**A:** First-line: DOAC (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, dabigatran) for most patients. Duration: 3-6 months for reversible cause, 6 months to indefinite for unprovoked, indefinite for ongoing major risk factors (active cancer, APLA).

LMWH is preferred over warfarin for pregnancy, active cancer, or high bleeding risk. If using warfarin, overlap with LMWH/heparin for ≥5 days until INR 2-3 for ≥2 days. IV thrombolytics are reserved for massive PE with hypotension or right heart failure.

**Q:** What are the Berlin Criteria for ARDS?

**A:** Acute onset within 7 days of known insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms; bilateral opacities on CT/CXR consistent with pulmonary edema; not fully explained by cardiac failure/fluid overload. Severity: Mild PaO2/FiO2 200-300, Moderate 100-200, Severe <100.

ARDS treatment is based on the ARDSNet protocol: low tidal volumes (<6 mL/kg), optimal PEEP, and treatment of the underlying cause. Prone ventilation, inhaled nitric oxide, and ECMO may be considered if conventional treatment fails. Mortality is 30-40%.

**Q:** What defines sepsis and septic shock?

**A:** Sepsis: life-threatening organ dysfunction from dysregulated host response to infection (SOFA score ≥2). Septic shock: sepsis with persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg AND lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Quick SOFA (qSOFA) criteria for bedside assessment: respiratory rate ≥22/min, altered mentation (GCS <15), systolic BP ≤100 mmHg. A positive qSOFA (≥2 criteria) should prompt SOFA scoring and evaluation for infection and organ dysfunction.

**Q:** What are the four categories of shock and their distinguishing features?

**A:** Hypovolemic: cold extremities, decreased JVP. Cardiogenic: bilateral crackles, increased JVP. Obstructive: may see pulsus paradoxus, Kussmaul's sign, tracheal deviation. Distributive: warm extremities, decreased JVP.

Hypovolemia is the most common cause of shock. Treatment modalities include fluid resuscitation (not in cardiogenic), inotropes, vasopressors, revascularization, and needle decompression for tension pneumothorax. The goal is to normalize critical organ perfusion.

**Q:** What are the causes of obstructive vs restrictive patterns on PFTs?

**A:** Obstructive (decreased FEV1/FVC): asthma, COPD, bronchiolitis, bronchiectasis/CF. Restrictive (normal/elevated FEV1/FVC, decreased TLC): ILD, pleural disease, neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease.

In obstruction, flow rates are decreased (especially during expiration) with air trapping (increased RV/TLC) and hyperinflation (increased TLC). In restriction, lung volumes and compliance are decreased. DLCO is decreased in ILD and emphysema but normal or increased in asthma.

**Q:** What factors shift the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right?

**A:** CADET face right: CO2, Acid, 2,3-DPG (now 2,3-BPG), Exercise, Temperature (increased).

A right shift means hemoglobin has decreased oxygen affinity, releasing more oxygen to tissues. This is beneficial in exercising muscles and acidotic tissues. A left shift (opposite factors) means increased oxygen affinity, holding onto oxygen more tightly.

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis for clubbing?

**A:** Pulmonary: lung CA, bronchiectasis, pulmonary fibrosis, abscess, CF, TB, empyema, AV fistula (NOT COPD). Cardiac: cyanotic congenital heart disease, endocarditis. GI: IBD, celiac, liver cirrhosis, neoplasm. Endocrine: Graves' disease.

Clubbing is assessed by comparing interphalangeal depth (IPD) to distal phalangeal depth (DPD): clubbing is present when DPD > IPD. Schamroth's sign (loss of diamond-shaped window between nail beds) is another clinical test. Importantly, clubbing is NOT seen in COPD — its presence should suggest malignancy.

**Q:** What are Light's criteria for distinguishing exudative from transudative pleural effusions?

**A:** Exudate if ANY of: pleural/serum protein >0.5, pleural/serum LDH >0.6, pleural LDH >2/3 upper limit of normal serum LDH. Sensitivity 98%, specificity 83%.

Common transudates include CHF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome. Common exudates include infections, malignancy, inflammatory conditions, and PE. A complicated parapneumonic effusion (needs drainage) has: loculation, >1/2 hemithorax, pH <7.2, glucose <2.2, LDH >1000, or positive gram stain/culture.

**Q:** What is the definition of OSA and how is severity graded?

**A:** Episodic decreases in airflow during sleep measured by Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI = apneas + hypopneas per hour). Mild: AHI 5-15, Moderate: 15-30, Severe: >30 events/hour.

OSA risk factors include obesity, crowded oropharynx, large neck circumference (>43 cm men, >40.6 cm women). Complications include hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and motor vehicle collisions. CPAP is very effective but compliance-limited. Weight loss and avoiding supine sleep may help.

**Q:** What is the anion gap formula and what causes an elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis?

**A:** Anion gap = [Na+] - ([Cl-] + [HCO3-]); normal 5-14 mmol/L. Elevated AG causes: MUDPILESCAT — Methanol, Uremia, DKA/starvation ketoacidosis, Phenformin/Paraldehyde, Isoniazid/Iron/Ibuprofen, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates, Carbon monoxide/Cyanide, Toluene/Theophylline.

When evaluating metabolic acidosis, calculate the anion gap first. If elevated, check if the change in bicarbonate matches the change in anion gap (delta-delta) to detect mixed metabolic disorders. An abnormal osmolar gap (>10) suggests the presence of alcohols.

**Q:** What are the 5 WHO classifications of pulmonary hypertension?

**A:** Group I: Pulmonary arterial HTN (idiopathic, CTD, congenital heart disease). Group II: Left heart disease. Group III: Lung disease/hypoxia. Group IV: Chronic thromboembolic. Group V: Unclear multifactorial mechanisms.

Diagnosis requires mean pulmonary arterial pressure >20 mmHg with PAWP <15 mmHg and PVR ≥3 Wood units on right heart catheterization. Treatment depends on classification. Only Group I PAH responds to advanced therapies (prostanoids, ERAs, PDE5 inhibitors). Home oxygen has proven mortality benefit for Group III.

**Q:** What is the sweat chloride test diagnostic threshold for cystic fibrosis?

**A:** Chloride concentration >60 mmol/L on two occasions supports the diagnosis.

CF is caused by CFTR gene mutations (autosomal recessive, >2100 mutations). Clinical features include sinusitis, chronic pulmonary disease, pancreatic insufficiency, and azoospermia. CFTR modulators (ivacaftor, lumacaftor-ivacaftor, tezacaftor-ivacaftor, Trikafta) target specific mutation classes and improve lung function and quality of life.

**Q:** What is the Virchow's triad for venous thromboembolism?

**A:** 1. Venous stasis, 2. Endothelial cell damage, 3. Hypercoagulable states.

Lower extremity DVTs are the source of most clinically recognized PEs. Risk factors include immobilization, surgery, malignancy, estrogen use, pregnancy, prior DVT/PE, and inherited thrombophilias (Factor V Leiden, prothrombin 20210A, antithrombin/protein C/S deficiency). PE is one of the most common causes of preventable death in hospital.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of hemoptysis?

**A:** Bronchitis (acute or chronic).

90% of massive hemoptysis originates from bronchial arteries. Massive hemoptysis is variably defined as ≥600 mL/24h or bleeding rate >100 mL/h. Other causes include bronchiectasis, bronchogenic carcinoma, pneumonia, PE, and vasculitis (GPA, Goodpasture's). The differential also includes vascular malformations and elevated pulmonary venous pressure.

**Q:** What are the 'Big Three' causes of chronic cough in non-smoking patients with a normal CXR?

**A:** 1. GERD, 2. Asthma, 3. Postnasal drip (upper airway cough syndrome).

ACEI-induced cough should also be considered. Chronic cough is defined as cough lasting >3 months. Other causes include chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, neoplasm, ILD, CHF, and drug-induced (ACEI). A systematic approach evaluating these common causes usually identifies the etiology.

**Q:** A patient with OSA has an apnea-hypopnea index of 25 events/hour. What are the cardiovascular consequences of untreated OSA, and what is the primary treatment?

**A:** Cardiovascular consequences: HTN (2x risk), CAD (3x risk), stroke (4x risk), arrhythmias, pulmonary HTN, cor pulmonale. Primary treatment: CPAP (maintains patent airway in 95% of cases).

OSA is diagnosed by nocturnal polysomnography with >15 apneic/hypopneic episodes per hour. OSA is an independent risk factor for CAD. Patients must be reported to the Ministry of Transportation if OSA is not controlled by CPAP.

**Q:** A patient presents with acute onset of pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea. CXR shows absence of lung markings at the left apex with a visible pleural line. Trachea is midline and the patient is hemodynamically stable. What is the diagnosis and management?

**A:** Primary spontaneous pneumothorax. If small (<3 cm) with minimal symptoms: observation + supplemental O2. If large (>3 cm) or symptomatic: aspiration or chest tube.

Primary spontaneous pneumothorax occurs from rupture of apical subpleural blebs, typically in young male smokers. Tension pneumothorax (tracheal deviation, shock) requires immediate needle decompression followed by chest tube. VATS bullectomy and pleurodesis for recurrence.

**Q:** What is the diagnosis of OSA based on polysomnography?

**A:** >15 apneic/hypopneic episodes per hour of sleep. Apnea is defined as no breathing for ≥10 seconds from partial or complete upper airway obstruction.

OSA consequences include HTN (2x risk), CAD (3x risk), stroke (4x risk), morning headache from hypercapnia, and cor pulmonale. CPAP is the primary treatment, maintaining patent airway in 95% of cases.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for OSA?

**A:** Male gender, BMI >30, large neck circumference, children with large tonsils/adenoids, aging (decreased muscle tone), alcohol use, sedative use, smoking, and family history.

OSA is an independent risk factor for CAD. Patients must be reported to the Ministry of Transportation if OSA is not controlled by CPAP. Surgical options include UPPP and tonsillectomy.

**Q:** Describe the 5 abnormal respiration patterns and their causes.

**A:** 1. Bradypnea (slow RR): drug-induced respiratory depression, diabetic coma (nonketotic), increased ICP
2. Kussmaul's breathing (fast and deep): metabolic acidosis, exercise, anxiety
3. Biot's/Ataxic (irregular with long apneic periods): drug-induced respiratory depression, increased ICP, brain damage (especially medullary)
4. Cheyne-Stokes (cyclical crescendo-decrescendo between apneas): drug-induced respiratory depression, brain damage (especially cerebral), uremia
5. Apneustic (prolonged inspiratory pause): pontine lesion

Abnormal breathing patterns reflect dysfunction at different levels of the respiratory control system. Cheyne-Stokes is most commonly associated with severe heart failure or cerebral pathology. Biot's/Ataxic breathing implies medullary damage. Apneustic breathing specifically indicates a pontine lesion. Kussmaul's is a physiological compensation for severe metabolic acidosis (e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis).

**Q:** What is obstructive vs. restrictive lung disease pattern on pulmonary function tests (PFTs)?

**A:** Obstructive: Decreased flow rates (especially expiration), air trapping (increased RV/TLC), hyperinflation (increased TLC), REDUCED FEV1/FVC ratio, TLC elevated or normal, RV elevated or normal. DDx: Asthma, COPD, bronchiolitis, bronchiectasis/CF.

Restrictive: Decreased lung compliance, decreased lung volumes, FEV1/FVC ELEVATED or normal, TLC REDUCED, RV reduced/normal/elevated. DDx: ILD, pleural disease, neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease.

The FEV1/FVC ratio is the key discriminator: <0.70 (or <lower limit of normal) indicates obstruction; normal or elevated suggests restriction. In restriction, all volumes are reduced proportionally, so the ratio is preserved or high. In obstruction, air trapping raises RV and may raise TLC, while FEV1 falls more than FVC. DLCO is reduced in emphysema (loss of surface area) and ILD.

**Q:** What is the FEV1/FVC ratio in obstructive vs. restrictive lung disease, and what does each indicate?

**A:** Obstructive: FEV1/FVC REDUCED (<0.70 or below lower limit of normal). Indicates air flow limitation — patient cannot expire quickly because of airway narrowing or loss of elastic recoil.

Restrictive: FEV1/FVC ELEVATED or normal. The ratio is preserved because both FEV1 and FVC are reduced proportionally due to decreased lung compliance or volume.

The FEV1/FVC ratio is the cornerstone of PFT interpretation. Obstruction means airway resistance is increased (asthma, COPD) or elastic recoil is lost (emphysema), limiting expiratory flow. Restriction means the lung or chest wall cannot expand adequately, reducing all volumes but maintaining the proportion of air expelled in the first second.

**Q:** What lung volumes are INCREASED vs. DECREASED in obstructive lung disease (e.g., COPD)?

**A:** INCREASED in obstruction: RV (residual volume due to air trapping), TLC (total lung capacity, due to hyperinflation), RV/TLC ratio, FRC (functional residual capacity).

DECREASED in obstruction: FEV1, FEV1/FVC ratio, FEF (forced expiratory flow rates).

In obstructive disease, incomplete exhalation leads to air trapping, progressively increasing RV. Hyperinflation develops as the lung operates at higher volumes. This is why COPD patients have barrel chests. The flattened diaphragm on CXR reflects the hyperinflated, air-trapped lungs.

**Q:** What conditions cause DLCO (diffusing capacity for CO) to DECREASE vs. INCREASE?

**A:** DLCO DECREASES in: ILD (fibrosis reduces diffusion), pulmonary vascular disease (reduced surface area), anemia (less hemoglobin to bind CO), emphysema (loss of alveolar surface area).

DLCO INCREASES in/with: Asthma, obesity (increased pulmonary blood volume), left-to-right intracardiac shunt (more blood in pulmonary circulation), polycythemia (more hemoglobin), post-exercise physiology.

DLCO measures the lung's ability to transfer gas across the alveolar-capillary membrane. It is reduced when the membrane is thickened (ILD), surface area is lost (emphysema), or the blood's capacity to carry CO is reduced (anemia). It is paradoxically increased in asthma (possibly due to increased pulmonary blood volume) and in conditions that increase pulmonary blood volume (polycythemia, left-to-right shunt).

**Q:** What is Schamroth's sign and what does it indicate?

**A:** Schamroth's sign: When the dorsal surfaces of the terminal phalanges of corresponding fingers are placed together, a small diamond-shaped window (Schamroth's window) is normally visible. In clubbing, this window is obliterated (Schamroth's sign is positive).

Clubbing indicates chronic hypoxia or chronic disease: pulmonary (lung cancer, bronchiectasis, pulmonary fibrosis, CF, TB, lung abscess, A-V fistula), cardiac (cyanotic congenital heart disease, endocarditis), GI (IBD, celiac, cirrhosis), endocrine (Graves'), other malignancy.

Digital clubbing is characterized by increased distal phalangeal finger depth (DPD) relative to interphalangeal depth (IPD) — DPD > IPD. Schamroth's sign is a bedside test. The loss of the diamond window is diagnostic. Importantly, COPD alone does NOT cause clubbing — its presence in a COPD patient should prompt investigation for malignancy.

**Q:** Clubbing is seen in COPD: True or False? What should you think if a COPD patient has clubbing?

**A:** FALSE. Clubbing is NOT seen in COPD. If a COPD patient has clubbing, think MALIGNANCY (lung cancer). Clubbing prompts investigation for an underlying etiology beyond COPD alone.

This is a high-yield MCCQE1 teaching point. COPD causes hypoxia but NOT clubbing. The mechanism of clubbing is not fully understood but is associated with platelet-derived growth factor released from megakaryocytes that bypass the pulmonary circulation (e.g., in cyanotic congenital heart disease) or chronic hypoxia with other mediators. COPD causes airway and parenchymal disease but not the vascular changes that lead to clubbing.

**Q:** What are the signs of respiratory distress on clinical examination?

**A:** 1. Tachypnea (increased RR)
2. Central/peripheral cyanosis
3. Tachycardia
4. Inability to speak in full sentences
5. Nasal flaring
6. Tracheal tug
7. Intercostal indrawing (use of accessory muscles)
8. Tripod position (sitting leaning forward on outstretched arms)
9. Paradoxical breathing (abdominal movement opposite to chest)

These signs reflect the body's physiological response to impaired gas exchange or increased work of breathing. Tripod position optimizes accessory muscle mechanics. Paradoxical breathing suggests diaphragm fatigue or paralysis. Tracheal tug reflects severe upper airway obstruction or lung hyperinflation. Inability to speak in full sentences is a simple bedside marker of severe dyspnea.

**Q:** What are the most common causes of chronic cough (>3 months) in a non-smoking patient with a normal CXR?

**A:** The 'Big Three' causes of chronic cough in non-smokers with normal CXR:
1. Postnasal drip (upper airway cough syndrome)
2. GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease)
3. Asthma

Also: ACEI-induced cough (important medication cause to identify)

These four causes account for the vast majority of chronic cough cases in non-smokers. ACEI-induced cough (dry, persistent, affecting up to 10–15% of ACEI users, more common in women and Asians) resolves within weeks of stopping the medication. Postnasal drip causes cough via posterior pharyngeal stimulation. Asthma may present with cough as the only symptom (cough-variant asthma).

**Q:** What are the most common causes of hemoptysis? What is the most common cause of MASSIVE hemoptysis and which vessels are responsible?

**A:** Most common cause of hemoptysis overall: ACUTE OR CHRONIC BRONCHITIS

Most common cause of massive hemoptysis: Bronchitis (and bronchiectasis)
90% of massive hemoptysis originates from BRONCHIAL ARTERIES (not pulmonary arteries)

Massive hemoptysis is often defined as >600 mL/24 hours or bleeding rate >100 mL/hour.

The bronchial arteries (systemic circulation, high pressure) are responsible for 90% of massive hemoptysis. This is why bronchial artery embolization (BAE) is the primary treatment for massive hemoptysis. Pulmonary arteries (low pressure) contribute to a minority. Other major causes include bronchiectasis, bronchogenic carcinoma, TB (Rasmussen aneurysm), and fungal infections (aspergilloma).

**Q:** What CXR pattern is seen with consolidation vs. reticular (interstitial) vs. nodular patterns, and what are the differential diagnoses for each?

**A:** CONSOLIDATION ('airspace disease'): Air bronchogram, silhouette sign, less visible blood vessels. DDx: Acute — CHF (water), pneumonia (pus), hemorrhage (blood). Chronic — neoplasm (lymphoma, BAC), inflammatory (eosinophilic pneumonia, organizing pneumonia), infection (TB, fungal).

RETICULAR ('interstitial disease'): Increased linear markings, honeycombing (IPF, RA, asbestosis). DDx: ILD (IPF, collagen vascular disease, asbestosis, drugs, HP).

NODULAR: Cavitary — neoplasm (squamous cell CA), infection (anaerobic/GN bacteria, TB, fungal), inflammatory (RA, GPA/Wegener's). Non-cavitary — same plus sarcoidosis, Kaposi's (HIV), silicosis, coal worker's pneumoconiosis.

CXR pattern recognition is fundamental to respirology. Honeycombing (clustered cystic changes) is most characteristic of IPF but also seen in RA and asbestosis. The silhouette sign (loss of normal cardiac or diaphragm border) helps localize consolidation. Air bronchogram confirms that airways remain patent within consolidated lung, distinguishing true consolidation from pleural effusion or mass.

**Q:** How do you approach acid-base interpretation using arterial blood gas (ABG) in 5 steps?

**A:** Step 1: Is pH acidemic (<7.35), alkalemic (>7.45), or normal (7.35–7.45)?
Step 2: What is the primary disturbance? Metabolic = HCO3 and pH move in SAME direction. Respiratory = HCO3 and pH move in OPPOSITE directions.
Step 3: Is there appropriate compensation? (see expected compensation formulas)
Step 4: If metabolic acidosis, calculate anion gap = [Na+] - ([Cl-] + [HCO3-]); normal 5–14 mmol/L. Also osmolar gap = measured - calculated osmolarity (2[Na+] + glucose + urea); normal <10 mmol/L.
Step 5: If elevated anion gap, check if delta-delta ratio is consistent (change in AG should match change in HCO3; if not, consider mixed disorder).

Systematic ABG interpretation prevents errors. A 'normal' pH with abnormal PaCO2 or HCO3 suggests a mixed disorder. Inadequate compensation suggests a second primary disorder. The anion gap identifies unmeasured anions (lactate, ketones, toxins). The delta-delta (or delta ratio) detects superimposed non-anion gap metabolic acidosis or metabolic alkalosis in a high-AG acidosis.

**Q:** What are the expected compensatory changes for the 4 primary acid-base disorders?

**A:** Respiratory Acidosis:
- Acute: for every +10 PaCO2, HCO3 rises +1
- Chronic: for every +10 PaCO2, HCO3 rises +3

Respiratory Alkalosis:
- Acute: for every -10 PaCO2, HCO3 falls -2
- Chronic: for every -10 PaCO2, HCO3 falls -5

Metabolic Acidosis: PaCO2 = (1.5 × HCO3) + 8 ± 2 (Winter's formula), OR for every -1 HCO3, PaCO2 falls -1

Metabolic Alkalosis: for every +10 HCO3, PaCO2 rises +7 (OR for every +1 HCO3, PaCO2 rises +0.7)

Compensation is NEVER complete — it brings the pH toward normal but not to normal. If compensation is absent, inadequate, or excessive, a second primary acid-base disorder should be suspected. Renal compensation (for respiratory disorders) takes 2–3 days; respiratory compensation (for metabolic disorders) occurs immediately via chemoreceptors.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic MUDPILESCAT for causes of high anion gap metabolic acidosis?

**A:** M — Methanol
U — Uremia
D — Diabetic ketoacidosis / starvation ketoacidosis
P — Phenformin / Paraldehyde
I — Isoniazid, Iron, Ibuprofen
L — Lactic acidosis
E — Ethylene glycol
S — Salicylates
C — CO (Carbon monoxide)
A — Alcoholic ketoacidosis
T — Toluene, Theophylline

(Note: SELACEET is an alternative mnemonic)

High anion gap metabolic acidosis (AG >14 mmol/L) is caused by addition of unmeasured acids. The most common in clinical practice are DKA, lactic acidosis (sepsis, shock), uremia, and salicylate poisoning. Methanol and ethylene glycol ingestion also cause an elevated osmolar gap. Recognizing the specific cause directs targeted treatment.

**Q:** What is Kussmaul's breathing and what causes it?

**A:** Kussmaul's breathing is a breathing pattern characterized by rapid, deep respirations (hyperpnea). It is caused by:
- Metabolic acidosis (most classically diabetic ketoacidosis)
- Exercise
- Anxiety

It represents respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis — the increased minute ventilation blows off CO2 to raise pH.

Kussmaul's breathing is a physiological response to severe metabolic acidosis. The increased respiratory drive is triggered by chemoreceptors responding to low pH. It is most classically associated with DKA but can also be seen in any severe metabolic acidosis (lactic acidosis, uremia, salicylate poisoning). The rapid deep breathing can reduce PaCO2 from 40 to as low as 10–15 mmHg in severe acidosis.

**Q:** What is the CADET mnemonic for factors that shift the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the RIGHT?

**A:** CADET, face right! (Right shift = decreased O2 affinity = increased O2 delivery to tissues)
C — CO2 (increased)
A — Acid (increased H+, lower pH)
D — 2,3-DPG (2,3-bisphosphoglycerate) (increased)
E — Exercise
T — Temperature (increased)

A right shift means O2 is released MORE EASILY to tissues (lower O2 affinity of Hb).

The Bohr effect describes how increased CO2 and decreased pH (increased H+) shift the curve right, facilitating O2 unloading in metabolically active tissues. 2,3-BPG (formerly called 2,3-DPG) is produced by red blood cells during glycolysis and competes with O2 for binding to Hb. A LEFT shift (increased O2 affinity, impaired O2 release) occurs with decreased CO2, alkalosis, decreased 2,3-BPG, decreased temperature, and fetal Hb.

**Q:** What are the causes of respiratory ACIDOSIS (increased PaCO2)?

**A:** Respiratory acidosis = hypoventilation leading to CO2 retention:
1. Respiratory Centre Depression (decreased RR): Drugs (anesthesia, sedatives, narcotics), trauma, encephalitis, stroke, central apnea, supplemental O2 in chronic CO2 retainers (e.g., COPD)
2. Neuromuscular Disorders (decreased VC): Myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, botulism, poliomyelitis, muscular dystrophies, ALS, myopathies, chest wall disease (obesity, kyphoscoliosis)
3. Lung Disease: Chronic — COPD, CF; Acute — asthma, pulmonary edema, pneumothorax, pneumonia, ILD (late stage), ARDS
4. Mechanical Hypoventilation (inadequate mechanical ventilation)

Any condition that reduces alveolar ventilation can cause CO2 retention. In chronic COPD patients who are chronic CO2 retainers, administering high-flow O2 can worsen hypoventilation by removing the hypoxic drive to breathe (the Haldane effect also contributes via reduced CO2 carrying capacity). Target SpO2 88–92% in known CO2 retainers.

**Q:** What are the causes of respiratory ALKALOSIS (decreased PaCO2)?

**A:** Respiratory alkalosis = hyperventilation leading to CO2 loss:
1. Systemic diseases: Hypoxic pulmonary disease (pneumonia, edema, PE, acute asthma), severe anemia, heart failure, Gram-negative sepsis, pregnancy
2. Respiratory Centre Stimulation: Drugs (ASA, progesterone, theophylline, catecholamines, psychotropics, nicotine, salicylates), hepatic failure, anxiety, pain
3. Mechanical Hyperventilation (excessive ventilator settings)

Hyperventilation blows off CO2, raising blood pH. In pregnancy, progesterone stimulates the respiratory center causing physiological hyperventilation (PaCO2 normally ~30 mmHg). Salicylate toxicity directly stimulates the respiratory center, causing a primary respiratory alkalosis often combined with metabolic acidosis (mixed picture). Hepatic failure causes respiratory alkalosis through stimulation of the respiratory center by ammonia and other toxins.

**Q:** How do you calculate the alveolar-arterial (A-a) oxygen gradient, and what is normal?

**A:** A-aDO2 = (150 - 1.25 × PaCO2) - PaO2
(At sea level on room air: FiO2 = 0.21, RQ = 0.8)

Normal A-a gradient: <15 mmHg (increases with age)
In pediatric patients: normal <15 mmHg (~5–10 mmHg)

Increased A-a gradient indicates V/Q mismatch, diffusion impairment, or shunt.

The A-a gradient reflects the efficiency of gas exchange across the alveolar-capillary membrane. A normal A-a gradient with low PaO2 suggests hypoventilation (the lungs are working fine, but not enough gas is moving). An increased A-a gradient indicates a lung problem: V/Q mismatch (most common, e.g., PE, pneumonia), diffusion impairment (ILD), or intracardiac/intrapulmonary shunt.

**Q:** What is the concept of V/Q mismatch and how does it cause hypoxemia?

**A:** V/Q mismatch occurs when ventilation (V) and perfusion (Q) are not matched:
- Low V/Q (shunt-like): blood perfuses poorly ventilated areas → venous blood added to arterial circulation → hypoxemia
- High V/Q (dead space-like): ventilated areas not perfused → wasted ventilation, CO2 retention

Pulmonary shunt: blood bypasses alveolar membrane via abnormal circulation (true shunt) or passes through severely consolidated areas (shunt-like physiology). A true shunt does NOT respond to supplemental O2.

V/Q mismatch is the most common cause of hypoxemia in clinical medicine. In pneumonia, consolidated lung has low V/Q (blood perfuses but no O2 exchange). In PE, lung is ventilated but not perfused (high V/Q, dead space). In pulmonary edema or ARDS, fluid-filled alveoli create shunt physiology. The key clinical test: if hypoxemia corrects with supplemental O2, it is V/Q mismatch; if it does NOT correct, it is a true shunt.

**Q:** What are the differential diagnoses of acute dyspnea (onset over minutes to days)?

**A:** CARDIAC:
- Acute coronary syndrome / AMI
- Acute decompensated heart failure / CHF exacerbation
- Cardiac tamponade
- Arrhythmia
- Pericarditis
- Myocarditis

PULMONARY:
- Upper airway obstruction (anaphylaxis, aspiration, croup, EBV)
- Airway disease (asthma, COPD exacerbation, bronchitis)
- Parenchymal disease (ARDS, pneumonia)
- Pulmonary vascular disease (PE, vasculitis)
- Pleural disease (pneumothorax, tension pneumothorax, pleural effusion)

NEUROLOGIC/PSYCHOGENIC:
- Metabolic acidosis
- Anxiety / panic attack

The approach to acute dyspnea requires rapidly identifying life-threatening causes: tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, and acute MI must be excluded first. The history (onset speed, associated symptoms, risk factors) combined with O2 saturation, ECG, and CXR guides initial assessment. Anxiety/panic disorder is a diagnosis of exclusion.

**Q:** What are the common causes of digital clubbing (organized by system)?

**A:** PULMONARY (most common): Lung cancer (CA), bronchiectasis, pulmonary fibrosis, lung abscess, cystic fibrosis (CF), TB, empyema, A-V fistula/malformation. NOTE: NOT COPD.

CARDIAC: Cyanotic congenital heart disease, infective endocarditis.

GI: Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), celiac disease, liver cirrhosis, GI neoplasm.

ENDOCRINE: Graves' disease (thyroid acropachy).

OTHER: Other malignancy, primary hypertrophic osteoarthropathy.

Clubbing results from platelet-derived growth factor and VEGF acting on the periosteum and soft tissues of the digits. In cyanotic heart disease, platelet clumps that normally fragment in the lung reach systemic circulation intact. COPD does not cause clubbing — this distinction is repeatedly tested. When a COPD patient has clubbing, bronchogenic carcinoma must be ruled out urgently.

**Q:** What is the purpose of bronchoscopy and what procedures can be performed through it?

**A:** Purpose: Diagnosis and therapy of airway and pulmonary diseases.

Procedures:
- Bronchial and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL): for culture, cell count analysis, cytology
- Endobronchial or transbronchial tissue biopsies
- Removal of secretions, foreign bodies, blood
- Laser resections
- Airway stenting
- Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS): sampling of mediastinal lymph nodes using bronchoscope with US probe

Flexible bronchoscopy allows direct visualization of airways down to the subsegmental level. EBUS is a major advance allowing real-time ultrasound-guided sampling of mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes without surgery — critical for lung cancer staging (N2/N3 disease). BAL is essential for diagnosing opportunistic infections (PCP, CMV) in immunocompromised patients. Rigid bronchoscopy is preferred for massive hemoptysis management.

**Q:** What is the relationship between electrolyte abnormalities and acid-base disorders?

**A:** Acidosis is associated with HYPERKALEMIA (H+ enters cells in exchange for K+ leaving).
Alkalosis is associated with HYPOKALEMIA (H+ exits cells in exchange for K+ entering).

For every 0.1 unit change in pH, K+ changes by approximately 0.5–0.6 mEq/L in the OPPOSITE direction.

This reciprocal relationship between pH and potassium has critical clinical implications. Treating DKA with insulin and fluids corrects acidosis and drives K+ into cells, causing rapid hypokalemia — aggressive K+ replacement is required. Conversely, metabolic alkalosis (from vomiting, diuretics) causes hypokalemia, and hypokalemia itself perpetuates the alkalosis (K+ exits cells in exchange for H+ entering). Always check K+ with any acid-base disorder.

**Q:** What is the normal anion gap and what formula is used to calculate it? What does an elevated osmolar gap indicate?

**A:** Anion gap (AG) = [Na+] - ([Cl-] + [HCO3-])
Normal: 5–14 mmol/L

Osmolar gap = measured osmolarity - calculated osmolarity
Calculated osmolarity = 2[Na+] + glucose + urea
(Mnemonic: '2 salts and a sticky BUN')
Normal osmolar gap: <10 mmol/L

Abnormal osmolar gap indicates presence of ALCOHOLS (methanol, ethanol, isopropanol, ethylene glycol).

The anion gap represents unmeasured anions (proteins, phosphate, sulfate, organic acids). An elevated AG identifies MUDPILESCAT causes. The osmolar gap is calculated to detect unmeasured osmoles — specifically toxic alcohols that are not sodium, glucose, or urea. An elevated osmolar gap with high AG acidosis strongly suggests methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning.

**Q:** What lung disease condition does bronchiectasis mimic on PFTs and why?

**A:** Bronchiectasis can cause an OBSTRUCTIVE or MIXED pattern on PFTs (unlike most of the other bronchiectasis features which resemble restrictive disease). This is because permanently dilated, damaged airways impair expiratory airflow similar to COPD, while the underlying structural destruction can reduce lung volumes.

Bronchiectasis is a chronic condition of irreversibly dilated bronchi due to chronic infection/inflammation. The PFT pattern depends on severity — early disease may show obstruction only, while severe disease shows a mixed obstructive-restrictive pattern. This distinguishes it from simple COPD (purely obstructive) and ILD (purely restrictive). CF is the most common cause in young adults.

**Q:** What are the 3 components of a bronchodilator response on spirometry, and when is it considered significant?

**A:** A significant bronchodilator response is defined as:
- Traditional/widely used definition: ≥12% AND ≥200 mL increase in FEV1 (or FVC) after bronchodilator
- NEW definition (emerging): >10% of PREDICTED VALUE for FEV1

A significant response suggests reversible airway obstruction (favoring asthma over COPD, which typically shows incomplete or no reversibility).

The bronchodilator response helps differentiate asthma (largely reversible) from COPD (poorly reversible). However, overlap exists — COPD patients can show partial reversibility. The new definition (>10% predicted) is not yet widely implemented but will likely change clinical practice. Full reversibility to normal (post-BD FEV1/FVC ≥0.70) strongly supports asthma.

**Q:** What is the 'Big Three' mnemonic for the most common causes of pleuritic chest pain?

**A:** Common causes of PLEURITIC chest pain (worse with inspiration/movement):
- Pulmonary: Pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax, pleurisy, hemothorax
- Cardiac: Pericarditis, Dressler's syndrome
- MSK: Costochondritis, fractured rib/flail chest, myositis, herpes zoster
- Other: Subphrenic abscess, neoplasm, esophageal rupture

Key distinction from nonpleuritic: Pleuritic pain is sharp, worse with breathing/coughing/movement; nonpleuritic is dull/pressure-like.

Pleuritic chest pain arises from parietal pleural inflammation — the visceral pleura has no pain receptors. PE classically causes pleuritic pain when it causes infarction and pleural involvement. Pericarditis causes pleuritic-type pain relieved by leaning forward (reduces pressure on pericardium). Distinguishing pleuritic from nonpleuritic pain is important for narrowing the DDx.

**Q:** What are the two acute sarcoidosis syndromes and their defining features?

**A:** 1. Lofgren's syndrome: fever, erythema nodosum, bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, arthralgias. 2. Heerfordt-Waldenstrom syndrome: fever, parotid enlargement, anterior uveitis, facial nerve palsy.

These two syndromes represent acute presentations of sarcoidosis. Lofgren's syndrome has a relatively good prognosis with spontaneous resolution in many cases. Heerfordt-Waldenstrom involves cranial nerve involvement including the parotid gland and facial nerve.

**Q:** What are the CXR staging criteria for sarcoidosis (Stages 0–IV)?

**A:** Stage 0: normal radiograph. Stage I: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy + paratracheal lymphadenopathy. Stage II: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy with pulmonary infiltration. Stage III: pulmonary infiltration alone (reticulonodular or nodular pattern). Stage IV: pulmonary fibrosis (upper lobe volume loss, honeycombing uncommon).

Radiographic staging guides prognosis and management decisions in sarcoidosis. Approximately 85% of Stage I and 50% of Stage II cases resolve spontaneously. Stage IV represents end-stage irreversible fibrosis.

**Q:** What are the indications for treating sarcoidosis (rather than observation)?

**A:** Treatment is indicated for symptoms, declining lung function, hypercalcemia, or involvement of the eye, CNS, kidney, or heart. An abnormal CXR alone is NOT an indication for treatment.

Most Stage I sarcoidosis resolves spontaneously without treatment. First-line treatment is prednisone. Methotrexate or other immunosuppressives can be used as adjuncts if steroid response is unsatisfactory or as steroid-sparing agents.

**Q:** What laboratory finding is associated with sarcoidosis and reflects total body granuloma burden?

**A:** Elevated serum ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme). It is non-specific and non-sensitive but reflects total body granuloma load. Hypercalcemia/hypercalciuria also occurs due to vitamin D activation by granulomas.

Sarcoid granulomas produce ACE and also activate vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), leading to hypercalcemia. ACE is not reliable for diagnosis or disease monitoring due to poor sensitivity and specificity.

**Q:** What extrapulmonary manifestations are commonly seen in sarcoidosis?

**A:** Common: anterior or posterior uveitis, skin lesions (papules, erythema nodosum, lupus pernio), peripheral lymphadenopathy, arthralgia, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly. Less common: bone, CNS, kidney, cardiac involvement (arrhythmias, sudden death, CHF).

Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease affecting multiple organs. Uveitis can lead to blindness if untreated, so routine slit-lamp examination is recommended. Cardiac sarcoidosis can cause life-threatening arrhythmias.

**Q:** What are the key differences between asbestosis, silicosis, and coal workers' pneumoconiosis (CWP) in terms of CXR findings?

**A:** Asbestosis: lower lobe reticulonodular pattern, IPF-like honeycombing, pleural plaques (± calcification). Silicosis: upper > lower lobe nodular disease; late progressive massive fibrosis; hilar 'eggshell' calcification. CWP simple: multiple upper lobe nodular opacities; complicated: opacities coalesce (progressive massive fibrosis).

Asbestosis is distinguished by lower lobe involvement and pleural plaques. Silicosis and CWP predominantly affect upper lobes. 'Eggshell' calcification of hilar nodes is characteristic of silicosis. Ferruginous bodies (yellow-brown macrophage-coated fibres) are the microscopic hallmark of asbestosis.

**Q:** What complications are associated with asbestos exposure?

**A:** Increased risk of bronchogenic carcinoma (dramatically increased in smokers), malignant mesothelioma, pleural plaques (± calcification), pleural effusion, round atelectasis, and asbestosis (parenchymal fibrosis).

Asbestos and smoking act synergistically to increase lung cancer risk (but NOT mesothelioma risk — smoking is not a risk factor for mesothelioma). Microscopic examination reveals ferruginous bodies. Latency period for disease development is typically 20–30 years.

**Q:** Which drugs are associated with drug-induced interstitial lung disease?

**A:** Antibiotics (nitrofurantoin, penicillin, sulfonamide); cardiovascular drugs (amiodarone, tocainide); anti-inflammatory agents (methotrexate, penicillamine, etanercept, gold salts); biologics (rituximab, anti-TNF-α agents, immunotherapy); chemotherapy (bleomycin); recreational drugs (heroin, methadone).

Drug-induced ILD should be considered in any patient on these medications who develops new respiratory symptoms or imaging abnormalities. Bleomycin causes pulmonary toxicity in a dose-dependent fashion. Amiodarone causes both acute and chronic pulmonary toxicity.

**Q:** What is the hemodynamic definition of pulmonary hypertension?

**A:** Mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) >20 mmHg with a pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) <15 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) ≥3 Wood units, measured by right heart catheterization in supine position at rest.

Right heart catheterization (Swan-Ganz catheter) is the gold standard for diagnosing pulmonary hypertension. The PAWP distinguishes pre-capillary (Group I, III, IV, V) from post-capillary (Group II — left heart disease) pulmonary hypertension.

**Q:** What are the 5 WHO classifications of pulmonary hypertension and representative examples of each?

**A:** Group I: Pulmonary Arterial HTN (PAH) — idiopathic, hereditary (BMPR2 mutation), collagen vascular disease, Eisenmenger syndrome, HIV, portopulmonary. Group II: Left heart disease — HFpEF, HFrEF, valvular disease. Group III: Lung disease/hypoxia — COPD, ILD, sleep-disordered breathing. Group IV: Chronic thromboembolic PH (CTEPH). Group V: Unclear/multifactorial — sarcoidosis, sickle cell, hematologic disorders.

Classification is critical as treatment differs by group. Group I PAH is treated with advanced vasodilator therapies (prostanoids, ERAs, PDE5 inhibitors, CCBs). Group III is managed by treating underlying lung disease and correcting hypoxia. CTEPH is treated with anticoagulation and pulmonary thromboendarterectomy.

**Q:** What is idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) and what are its histologic features?

**A:** IPAH is pulmonary HTN in the absence of a demonstrable cause (WHO Group I). Histology includes medial arterial hypertrophy, intimal fibrosis, and plexiform arteriopathy. It predominantly affects adults aged 50–65, with female predominance in younger patients.

Most cases are sporadic; familial cases account for <5% and are often linked to BMPR2 mutations. The pathophysiology involves vasoconstriction, vascular proliferation, and obstructive remodeling, increasing pulmonary vascular resistance.

**Q:** What are the signs and symptoms of pulmonary hypertension?

**A:** Symptoms: dyspnea, fatigue, retrosternal chest pain, syncope, palpitations, peripheral edema, symptoms of underlying disease. Signs: loud/palpable P2, RV heave, right-sided S4 (due to RVH), systolic murmur (tricuspid regurgitation). In right ventricular failure: right-sided S3, increased JVP, positive hepatojugular reflux, peripheral edema, TR.

Physical exam may be unremarkable in early disease. Syncope in pulmonary HTN indicates severe disease and carries a poor prognosis. A loud/palpable P2 is a hallmark sign of pulmonary hypertension reflecting elevated RV afterload.

**Q:** What investigations are used to diagnose pulmonary hypertension?

**A:** CXR (enlarged central pulmonary arteries, RV enlargement filling retrosternal space); ECG (RVH/right-sided strain); 2D echo Doppler (RV systolic pressure); right heart catheterization (gold standard — confirms mPAP, PAWP, PVR, cardiac output); PFTs (DLCO usually reduced); CT angiogram (assess parenchyma, rule out PE); V/Q scan (rule out CTEPH); serology (ANA positive in 30% with primary PH).

Right heart catheterization with a Swan-Ganz catheter is required to confirm the diagnosis. Vasoreactivity testing using short-acting agents (IV epoprostenol, IV adenosine, inhaled NO) identifies patients who may benefit from long-term CCB therapy.

**Q:** What is a positive vasodilator response in pulmonary arterial hypertension and what is its clinical implication?

**A:** Positive response: decrease in mPAP ≥10 mmHg to <40 mmHg with stable or increased cardiac output during vasoreactivity testing. Clinical implication: patient should be considered a candidate for trial of oral calcium channel blocker (CCB) therapy.

Vasoreactivity testing uses short-acting agents such as IV epoprostenol, IV adenosine, or inhaled NO. Only a minority of PAH patients respond; those who do have improved survival with high-dose CCBs.

**Q:** What is pulmonary embolism and how is it classified?

**A:** PE is mechanical obstruction of the pulmonary vasculature leading to obstruction of blood flow. Classified temporally as acute, subacute, or chronic. Acute PE further classified as massive (hypotension/clinical right heart failure), sub-massive, or low-risk.

Lower extremity deep vein thrombi are the source of most clinically recognized PEs. Thrombi often start in calf veins and must propagate into proximal veins to produce a sufficiently large thrombus for clinically significant PE. VTE affects approximately 1–2 per 1,000 adults per year.

**Q:** What are the components of Virchow's Triad and how do they relate to PE risk factors?

**A:** Virchow's Triad: 1. Venous stasis (immobilization, paralysis, obesity, CHF, chronic venous insufficiency, prolonged travel). 2. Endothelial cell damage (postoperative injury, trauma). 3. Hypercoagulable states (malignancy, OCP, pregnancy, inherited thrombophilias such as Factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome).

Factor V Leiden is the most common inherited thrombophilia. Oral contraceptive pills and exogenous estrogen increase VTE risk by promoting hypercoagulability. Underlying malignancy (especially adenocarcinoma) is strongly associated with VTE.

**Q:** What are the Wells criteria for pulmonary embolism and how are they interpreted?

**A:** Points: Clinical signs/symptoms of DVT (3.0), PE most likely diagnosis (3.0), HR >100 bpm (1.5), immobilization/surgery in previous 4 weeks (1.5), prior DVT/PE (1.5), hemoptysis (1.0), malignancy (1.0). Probability: Low (<2 points) ~2%, Intermediate (3–6 points) ~17%, High (>6 points) ~65%.

The Wells criteria are a validated clinical decision tool. Low pretest probability plus negative D-dimer effectively excludes PE. High pretest probability warrants direct CT pulmonary angiography without waiting for D-dimer.

**Q:** What is the PERC rule and when is it used?

**A:** PERC (PE Rule Out Criteria) can exclude PE without further testing if ALL criteria met: age <50, HR <100 bpm, O2 sat ≥95%, no hemoptysis, no prior DVT/PE, no unilateral leg swelling, no surgery/trauma requiring hospitalization within 4 weeks, no exogenous estrogen use. Used only in patients with low gestalt clinical probability.

If all 8 PERC criteria are satisfied in a low pretest probability patient, PE can be excluded without D-dimer or imaging. If any criterion is positive, further diagnostic testing (D-dimer) is required.

**Q:** What are the CXR findings in pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Frequently normal. May show: atelectasis (subsegmental), elevation of a hemidiaphragm, pleural effusion (usually small), Hampton's hump (cone-shaped peripheral opacification representing infarction), Westermark's sign (dilated proximal pulmonary artery with distal oligemia/decreased vascular markings), dilatation of proximal pulmonary artery (rare).

CXR is neither sensitive nor specific for PE but helps exclude alternative diagnoses. Hampton's hump is a classic but uncommon finding representing pulmonary infarction. Westermark's sign indicates abrupt cutoff of pulmonary vascularity.

**Q:** What is the classic ECG finding in massive pulmonary embolism?

**A:** S1Q3T3 pattern (deep S in lead I, Q wave in lead III, inverted T wave in lead III). Sinus tachycardia is the most common ECG finding in PE. May also see non-specific ST/T changes, RV strain, RAD, and right bundle branch block (RBBB).

ECG findings in PE are neither sensitive nor specific. Sinus tachycardia is the most common finding. S1Q3T3 occurs in massive PE due to acute right heart strain. RBBB can also develop with acute RV overload.

**Q:** When is a V/Q scan preferred over CT pulmonary angiography for PE diagnosis?

**A:** V/Q scan is preferred when: normal CXR with no COPD, contraindication to CT contrast (allergy or renal dysfunction), or pregnancy. Avoid V/Q scan if: CXR is abnormal or patient has COPD, suspected massive PE, or is an inpatient.

V/Q scan is very sensitive but less specific than CT angiography. A normal V/Q scan excludes PE. A high-probability V/Q scan only confirms PE if clinical suspicion is high. Approximately 60% of V/Q scans are non-diagnostic, requiring additional workup.

**Q:** What is the role and interpretation of D-dimer in the evaluation of PE?

**A:** D-dimer has high sensitivity and high negative predictive value (useful to rule out PE in low probability patients). It has poor specificity and poor positive predictive value. D-dimer is elevated with: increased age, recent surgery, cancer, inflammation, infection, pregnancy, and severe renal dysfunction.

A negative D-dimer combined with low or intermediate pretest probability effectively excludes PE. D-dimer is NOT useful to confirm PE due to its non-specificity. In high pretest probability patients, proceed directly to CT angiography.

**Q:** What is the acute anticoagulation treatment for pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Options: therapeutic-dose SC LMWH (dalteparin 200 units/kg once daily, enoxaparin 1 mg/kg BID, or fondaparinux 5–10 mg once daily), OR IV unfractionated heparin (bolus 75 units/kg then infusion 20 units/kg/h, target aPTT 2–3× control), OR direct oral anticoagulants (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, dabigatran). Start ASAP.

Anticoagulation stops clot propagation, prevents new clots, and allows the endogenous fibrinolytic system to dissolve existing thromboemboli. LMWH requires no lab monitoring but dose should be reduced in renal dysfunction. UFH is preferred for severe renal insufficiency.

**Q:** What is the recommended duration of anticoagulation after pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Reversible cause (surgery, injury, pregnancy): 3–6 months. Unprovoked PE: 6 months to indefinite. Ongoing major risk factor (active cancer, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome): indefinite anticoagulation.

For most non-pregnant patients without renal insufficiency or active cancer, first-line long-term anticoagulation is a DOAC (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, or dabigatran) rather than warfarin. LMWH is preferred in pregnancy, active cancer, or high bleeding risk patients.

**Q:** When is IV thrombolytic therapy indicated in pulmonary embolism?

**A:** Thrombolytics are indicated for massive PE (hemodynamic instability/hypotension or clinical right heart failure) with no contraindications. Thrombolysis hastens resolution of PE but may not improve survival or long-term outcome, and doubles the risk of major bleeding.

Catheter-directed thrombolysis is an alternative for massive PE but is NOT recommended over systemic thrombolysis. IVC filter use is reserved for patients with recent proximal DVT and an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation.

**Q:** What VTE risk categories guide thromboprophylaxis choice in hospital patients?

**A:** Low risk (fully mobile medical patients or surgery <30 min): no specific prophylaxis, frequent ambulation. Moderate risk (most general/gynecologic/urologic surgery, sick medical patients): LMWH, low-dose UFH, or fondaparinux. High risk (arthroplasty, hip fracture, major trauma, spinal cord injury): LMWH, fondaparinux, warfarin (INR 2–3), dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban. High bleeding risk (neurosurgery, intracranial bleed, active bleeding): TED stockings, pneumatic compression devices.

Thromboprophylaxis is cost-effective and mandatory for most hospital patients. It should be started ASAP and continued at least until discharge (extended for 35 days post-operatively after major orthopedic surgery).

**Q:** What are the key features of Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener's Granulomatosis)?

**A:** Definition: systemic vasculitis of medium and small arteries. Pulmonary: necrotizing granulomatous lesions — tracheal/bronchial stenosis, nodules, ILD, alveolar hemorrhage. Extra-pulmonary: nasal/sinus/ear disease, crescentic glomerulonephritis. Investigations: CXR/CT (nodules, cavities, alveolar opacities), c-ANCA positive in 90%, PR3 antibodies. Treatment: corticosteroids + cyclophosphamide or rituximab; PLEX in severe disease.

GPA is the most common pulmonary vasculitis. c-ANCA (anti-PR3) positivity is highly characteristic. With treatment, 65–90% achieve full remission. PLEX (plasma exchange) is used for pulmonary hemorrhage, severe renal failure, or concomitant anti-GBM disease.

**Q:** How does Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA, Churg-Strauss) differ from GPA?

**A:** EGPA: multisystem disorder characterized by allergic rhinitis, asthma (prodromal phase), and prominent peripheral eosinophilia. Involves small- and medium-sized vessels. Extra-pulmonary: life-threatening vasculitis of pericardium, heart, kidneys, skin, PNS (mononeuritis multiplex). p-ANCA/MPO may be positive. Treatment: corticosteroids ± cyclophosphamide (based on Five Factor Score) or rituximab; 90% achieve clinical remission.

Asthma is the hallmark prodromal feature of EGPA distinguishing it from GPA and MPA. Peripheral eosinophilia is the most common finding on lab work. CXR can be normal in 30–60% of cases but may show transient patchy opacities.

**Q:** What is anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's syndrome) and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Disorder characterized by diffuse alveolar hemorrhage and glomerulonephritis caused by anti-GBM antibodies that cross-react with basement membranes of the kidney and lung. Presentations: dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, anemia, acute renal failure. Investigations: ELISA for anti-GBM antibodies, renal biopsy/immunofluorescence showing linear staining. Treatment: corticosteroids, plasmapheresis, cyclophosphamide.

May be triggered by influenza infection or other lung injury. Linear IgG deposition on immunofluorescence of renal biopsy is the hallmark. Plasmapheresis removes circulating anti-GBM antibodies and is a critical component of acute management.

**Q:** How are pleural effusions classified and what is Light's criteria?

**A:** Classified as transudative or exudative using Light's Criteria (98% sensitivity, 83% specificity for exudates). Exudate if ANY of: pleural/serum protein >0.5, pleural/serum LDH >0.6, pleural LDH >2/3 upper limit of normal serum LDH.

Transudates result from alterations in Starling forces (CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome). Exudates result from increased capillary permeability or lymphatic dysfunction (infection, malignancy, inflammatory causes). Modified Light's criteria uses pleural LDH >0.45 upper limit of normal serum LDH.

**Q:** What are common causes of transudative pleural effusions?

**A:** CHF (usually bilateral, right > left), hepatic hydrothorax from cirrhosis (diaphragmatic deficit), nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy, pulmonary embolism (may cause transudate, more often exudate), peritoneal dialysis with transdiaphragmatic flow, hypothyroidism, urinothorax.

CHF is the most common cause of bilateral pleural effusions. Hepatic hydrothorax results from ascitic fluid moving through diaphragmatic defects into the pleural space. An isolated right-sided effusion in CHF is common.

**Q:** What are common causes of exudative pleural effusions?

**A:** Infectious: parapneumonic effusion, empyema, TB pleuritis. Malignancy: lung CA (35%), breast metastases (25%), lymphoma (10%), mesothelioma. Inflammatory: RA, SLE, pancreatitis, benign asbestos effusion, PE, post-CABG. Intra-abdominal: subphrenic abscess, pancreatic pseudocyst. Trauma: hemothorax, chylothorax. Intra-thoracic: esophageal perforation.

Parapneumonic effusions are the most common cause of exudative effusions. The most common malignant cause is lung cancer (35%). Meigs' syndrome (ovarian fibroma with ascites and hydrothorax) can be either transudate or exudate.

**Q:** What are the clinical signs and symptoms of pleural effusion?

**A:** Often asymptomatic. Symptoms: dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, shoulder pain (referred via phrenic nerve C3–C5). Signs: ipsilateral decreased expansion, tracheal deviation away from effusion (if large and rapid), decreased tactile fremitus, dullness to percussion, decreased breath sounds, bronchial breathing and egophony just above the fluid level, pleural friction rub (if inflammatory with minimal fluid).

The minimum fluid required to detect blunting of lateral costophrenic angle on PA CXR is ~200 mL. As little as 50 mL may blunt the posterior costophrenic angle on lateral CXR. Tracheal deviation indicates a large effusion or tension physiology.

**Q:** What pleural fluid analysis findings indicate a complicated parapneumonic effusion requiring drainage?

**A:** Any of: loculation, >1/2 hemithorax of fluid, pH <7.2, glucose <2.2 mmol/L, LDH >1000 IU/L, positive Gram stain or culture, frank pus on tap (empyema). Empyema always requires drainage.

Complicated parapneumonic effusions cannot resolve with antibiotics alone and require tube drainage. tPA/DNase instillation can improve drainage of infected/loculated effusions. Surgical management may be required in refractory cases.

**Q:** What pleural fluid glucose and pH values suggest specific diagnoses?

**A:** Low glucose (pleural:serum <0.5) and very low pH (<7.0): rheumatoid arthritis, TB, empyema, malignancy, esophageal rupture. Normal pleural pH is ~7.6. Esophageal rupture also associated with elevated salivary amylase in pleural fluid.

RA is notorious for causing very low pleural fluid glucose and pH. A pleural fluid pH <7.2 in a parapneumonic effusion indicates complicated effusion requiring drainage. Esophageal rupture is a rare but life-threatening cause of exudative effusion.

**Q:** What is mesothelioma and how does it differ from lung cancer in relation to asbestos and smoking?

**A:** Mesothelioma is a primary malignancy of the pleura occurring decades after asbestos exposure (even with limited exposure). Smoking is NOT a risk factor for mesothelioma (unlike lung cancer). However, asbestos exposure and smoking synergistically increase the risk of lung cancer.

CT thorax and biopsy (pleuroscopic or open) are required for diagnosis. Needle biopsy risks seeding the needle tract with tumour. Treatment involves a multidisciplinary trimodal approach (chemotherapy, radiation, surgical resection), but prognosis is poor and palliation is often the primary goal.

**Q:** What are the non-malignant pleural manifestations of asbestos exposure?

**A:** 1. Benign asbestos pleural effusion (BAPE): exudative effusion, typically ~10 years after exposure, often resolves spontaneously. 2. Round atelectasis: commonly seen peripherally, associated with calcified pleural plaques. 3. Pleural plaques (± calcification): marker of exposure, usually an asymptomatic radiologic finding.

Pleural plaques are the most common manifestation of asbestos exposure and are generally benign. BAPE must be distinguished from mesothelioma or pleural metastases (cytology may be needed). Round atelectasis is a benign condition caused by pleural disease folding adjacent lung tissue.

**Q:** What are the most common mediastinal masses by location (anterior, middle, posterior)?

**A:** Anterior (4 Ts): Thymoma, Teratoma (and other germ cell tumors), Thyroid enlargement (goiter), Terrible lymphoma. Middle: heart, pericardium, trachea, great vessels, lymph nodes. Posterior: esophagus, descending aorta, sympathetic chain, azygous venous system.

The '4 Ts' mnemonic helps recall the anterior mediastinal masses. Thymoma is associated with myasthenia gravis. Lymphoma can occur in both the anterior and middle mediastinum. Neurogenic tumors (schwannoma, neurofibroma) are the most common posterior mediastinal masses.

**Q:** What is acute mediastinitis, its causes, signs/symptoms, and treatment?

**A:** Causes: perforation of esophagus or trachea (Boerhaave's, penetrating trauma, instrumentation, carcinoma erosion), direct extension from dental/lung/pleural/pericardial infections, postoperative sternotomy or mediastinoscopy complications, primary mediastinal infections (e.g. inhalational anthrax). Signs/Symptoms: fever, substernal pain, dramatic acute onset, irritability, restlessness, sense of impending doom, pneumomediastinum, Hamman's sign (auscultatory 'crunch' during cardiac systole). Treatment: IV antibiotics (vancomycin + 3rd gen cephalosporin), drainage, ± surgical closure of perforation.

Boerhaave's syndrome (esophageal perforation due to vomiting) is a life-threatening cause of mediastinitis. Hamman's sign is a classic physical exam finding representing mediastinal air. Early surgical intervention is often required.

**Q:** What is the role of radiation-induced lung disease and when do the two phases occur?

**A:** Two phases: 1. Acute pneumonitis: typically 4–12 weeks post-exposure. 2. Late fibrosis: 6–12 months post-exposure. Infiltrates conform to the shape of the radiation field (distinguishing feature).

Radiation-induced lung disease is an important complication of thoracic radiation therapy. The radiation field-shaped distribution of infiltrates is a key radiographic clue. Risk is increased with larger radiation volumes, higher doses, and concurrent chemotherapy.

**Q:** Which pleural fluid cell count findings suggest specific diagnoses?

**A:** Neutrophil predominance: acute processes (parapneumonic, PE). Lymphocytic effusion: TB, cancer, lymphoma, RA. Eosinophilic: pneumothorax, hemothorax, drug reactions, PE, eosinophilic granulomatosis, asbestos-related, malignancy, parasitic. High RBC: traumatic, malignancy, PE with infarction, TB, hemothorax.

Cell count and differential guide the diagnostic workup. A lymphocytic effusion strongly suggests TB or malignancy, warranting further investigations including pleural biopsy if cytology is non-diagnostic. Eosinophilic effusion has a broad differential.

**Q:** What is the difference between chylothorax and chyliform effusion on pleural fluid analysis?

**A:** Chylothorax: elevated triglycerides in pleural fluid due to thoracic duct leakage (mostly from trauma or lymphoma). Chyliform effusion: elevated cholesterol and chylomicrons but different pathology — seen in chronic inflammation (e.g., TB, RA).

Chylothorax results from injury to the thoracic duct, causing lymphatic fluid rich in chylomicrons to accumulate in the pleural space. It appears milky. Measuring triglycerides and cholesterol/chylomicrons helps distinguish the two entities.

**Q:** What are the defining blood gas criteria for respiratory failure?

**A:** Hypoxemia (PaO2 <60 mmHg) and/or hypercapnia (PaCO2 >50 mmHg), reflecting failure to maintain normal gas exchange.

Respiratory failure is classified as hypoxemic (Type I: low PaO2, normal/low PaCO2) or hypercapnic (Type II: high PaCO2, low PaO2). Chronic respiratory failure activates compensatory mechanisms (e.g., renal bicarbonate retention) that blunt pH changes.

**Q:** A patient has hypoxemia with an elevated A-a O2 gradient and no response to supplemental oxygen. What type of hypoxemia does this represent, and what is the mechanism?

**A:** Anatomic (right-to-left) shunt. Deoxygenated blood bypasses ventilated alveoli entirely, so supplemental O2 cannot correct the hypoxemia because no additional O2 reaches the shunted blood.

In anatomic shunts (e.g., VSD with R→L shunt, intrapulmonary shunts in hepatopulmonary syndrome), the shunted blood never contacts ventilated alveoli. Unlike V/Q mismatch or diffusion impairment, increasing FiO2 has minimal effect. PEEP/BiPAP may worsen shunt by increasing pulmonary vascular resistance.

**Q:** What are the 6 types of hypoxemia, and which have an elevated A-a O2 gradient?

**A:** 1. Low FiO2 (normal A-a), 2. Hypoventilation (normal A-a), 3. Physiologic shunt/V/Q mismatch (elevated A-a), 4. Anatomic R→L shunt (elevated A-a), 5. Low mixed venous O2 content (elevated A-a), 6. Diffusion impairment (elevated A-a). Types 1 and 2 have normal A-a gradient.

The A-a gradient is the difference between alveolar and arterial PO2. A normal gradient with hypoxemia points to low FiO2 or pure hypoventilation. An elevated gradient indicates a lung-based mechanism (V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion problem) or low mixed venous O2 (e.g., in shock).

**Q:** In hypoxemic respiratory failure with a physiologic shunt (e.g., ARDS, pneumonia), what is the expected PaCO2 and how does oxygen therapy respond?

**A:** PaCO2 is normal or decreased (due to hyperventilation), and supplemental O2 is less effective than in other types of hypoxemia because shunted blood cannot be oxygenated.

V/Q mismatch with low V (physiologic shunt) creates areas where blood passes through unventilated lung. Increasing FiO2 helps somewhat by improving O2 delivery to partially ventilated areas, but true shunt units do not respond. PEEP recruits collapsed alveoli and reduces effective shunt fraction.

**Q:** What are the three major pathophysiologic mechanisms causing hypercapnic respiratory failure?

**A:** 1. Increased CO2 production (fever, sepsis, seizure, high carbohydrate load), 2. Increased dead space ventilation (COPD, CF — normal/increased minute ventilation but low alveolar ventilation), 3. Hypoventilation (low minute ventilation from neuromuscular disease, CNS depression, restrictive chest wall disease).

Dead space is ventilation without perfusion. In COPD, emphysematous destruction increases dead space, forcing greater minute ventilation to clear CO2; when reserves are exhausted, PaCO2 rises. Hypoventilation from opiates, benzodiazepines, or neuromuscular disease reduces total alveolar ventilation regardless of dead space.

**Q:** When should noninvasive or mechanical ventilation be initiated for hypercapnic respiratory failure?

**A:** When PaCO2 >50 mmHg and pH <7.35 (i.e., uncompensated respiratory acidosis).

A rising PaCO2 with acidosis indicates the patient cannot maintain adequate alveolar ventilation. NIV (BiPAP) is preferred first-line for COPD exacerbation, neuromuscular disease, and obesity-hypoventilation. Intubation is indicated if NIV fails or the patient cannot protect the airway.

**Q:** What is the concern with giving high-flow oxygen to COPD patients with chronic hypercapnia ('CO2 retainers')?

**A:** High-flow O2 can worsen hypoxemia and hypercapnia via: (1) worsening V/Q mismatch (Haldane effect and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction relief), (2) reduced hypoxic respiratory drive. However, O2 should NOT be denied if the patient is hypoxic — target SpO2 88-92%.

The Haldane effect describes CO2 displacement from hemoglobin by O2, raising PaCO2. Relief of hypoxic vasoconstriction redistributes blood to poorly ventilated areas, worsening V/Q mismatch. The physiologic importance is debated, but controlled O2 titration to SpO2 88-92% is the Canadian/international standard to avoid both hypoxia and CO2 retention.

**Q:** State the Berlin Criteria for ARDS diagnosis.

**A:** All four must be met: (1) Acute onset within 7 days of clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms; (2) Bilateral opacities on CXR or CT not fully explained by effusions, collapse, or nodules; (3) Not fully explained by cardiac failure/fluid overload (objective assessment with echo if no risk factor); (4) PaO2/FiO2 ≤300 mmHg with PEEP ≥5 cmH2O.

The Berlin Definition replaced the 1994 AECC definition and eliminated the ALI category. Severity is stratified by P:F ratio: Mild 200-300 (mortality ~27-30%), Moderate 100-200 (mortality ~32-34%), Severe <100 (mortality ~45-48%). ALI (P:F <300) was removed as a separate entity.

**Q:** What are the direct vs. indirect causes of ARDS?

**A:** Direct (lung injury): aspiration, pneumonia, inhalation injury, pulmonary embolism (fat/amniotic fluid), reperfusion injury. Indirect (systemic): sepsis, shock, polytrauma, massive blood transfusion, pancreatitis, head trauma, drug overdose (opioids, sedatives, TCAs), intracranial hemorrhage.

The common pathophysiology is disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier leading to non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema. Sepsis is the most common indirect cause. Aspiration of gastric contents is a classic direct cause, particularly in patients with decreased consciousness, upper GI disorders, or neurologic conditions.

**Q:** Describe the two phases of ARDS and their clinical characteristics.

**A:** Exudative phase (first 7 days): alveolar-capillary endothelial and type I pneumocyte injury → protein-rich edema, dyspnea, tachypnea, respiratory fatigue/failure. Fibroproliferative phase (after day 7): ongoing dyspnea/hypoxemia but most patients wean from ventilation; some develop fibrosis requiring long-term O2 or ventilation (associated with increased mortality).

During the exudative phase, loss of the tight alveolar barrier allows fluid and protein to flood alveoli, reducing compliance and causing shunting. The fibroproliferative phase involves type II pneumocyte proliferation and fibroblast activation. Most ARDS survivors regain near-normal lung function, though mild diffusion capacity reduction is common.

**Q:** What is the cornerstone of ARDS mechanical ventilation management per the ARDS Network protocol?

**A:** Low tidal volume ventilation: <6 mL/kg of ideal body weight (predicted body weight). This reduces barotrauma/volutrauma. Optimize PEEP to recruit alveoli and allow lower FiO2. ARDS Network trial showed 22% mortality reduction vs. 12 mL/kg.

High tidal volumes cause ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) through cyclic stretch (volutrauma) and alveolar overdistension (barotrauma). PEEP prevents derecruitment atelectrauma. Plateau pressure should be kept <30 cmH2O. Adjuncts for refractory hypoxemia include prone positioning, inhaled nitric oxide, neuromuscular blockade (<48 h), and ECMO.

**Q:** What is the overall mortality of ARDS and what are common causes of death?

**A:** Mortality is 30-40%, and death is usually due to non-pulmonary complications (multi-organ failure, sepsis) rather than refractory hypoxemia. Most survivors regain near-normal lung function with mildly reduced diffusion capacity.

The sequelae of ARDS include residual pulmonary impairment (especially reduced DLCO), severe debilitation, polyneuropathy (ICU-acquired weakness), and psychological difficulties (PTSD, depression). These gradually improve over months to years. The P:F ratio correlates with mortality: severe ARDS (<100) carries ~45-48% mortality.

**Q:** Define obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and its AHI-based severity classification.

**A:** OSA is episodic upper airway obstruction during sleep causing absent/reduced airflow despite persistent respiratory effort. Severity by Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI): Mild 5-15 events/h, Moderate 15-30 events/h, Severe >30 events/h. OSA is present if AHI ≥5 events/h.

The AHI counts apneas (complete cessation of airflow ≥10 s) and hypopneas (≥30% airflow reduction from baseline for ≥10 s with O2 desaturation or arousal) per hour of sleep. OSA is more common in males, obese individuals, and those with craniofacial abnormalities or crowded oropharynx.

**Q:** What are the nocturnal and daytime symptoms of OSA?

**A:** Nocturnal: loud snoring, witnessed apneas, choking/gasping spells, nocturnal awakenings, nocturia, unrefreshing sleep. Daytime: excessive daytime somnolence, irritability, depression, memory loss, morning headaches.

Repetitive arousal from sleep to restore airway patency causes sleep fragmentation and non-restorative sleep. Morning headaches reflect nocturnal CO2 retention and hypoxia. The Epworth Sleepiness Scale quantifies subjective daytime somnolence. Untreated OSA significantly impairs quality of life and increases motor vehicle accident risk.

**Q:** What are the systemic complications of untreated OSA?

**A:** Hypertension (most common), cardiovascular disease (CAD, CHF, arrhythmia), stroke, type 2 diabetes mellitus, pulmonary hypertension, polycythemia, and increased motor vehicle collision risk.

Recurrent hypoxia and sympathetic nervous system activation from arousals drive hypertension and cardiovascular disease. OSA is an independent risk factor for treatment-resistant hypertension. CPAP treatment reduces blood pressure, cardiovascular events, and daytime somnolence, and decreases motor vehicle accident risk.

**Q:** Outline the treatment hierarchy for OSA.

**A:** 1. Modifiable risk factors: weight loss, avoid alcohol/sedatives, treat nasal congestion, positional therapy (avoid supine). 2. CPAP — most effective at reducing AHI but limited by compliance. 3. Oral appliances (mandibular advancement devices) — less effective but better tolerated by some. 4. Surgical (tonsillectomy, UPPP, tongue base/jaw procedures, tracheostomy) for select patients.

CPAP acts as a pneumatic splint maintaining upper airway patency. Systematic reviews confirm CPAP reduces AHI, blood pressure, sleepiness, and motor vehicle accident risk. Oral appliances are appropriate for mild-moderate OSA or CPAP-intolerant patients. Bariatric surgery improves OSA in morbidly obese patients.

**Q:** Differentiate central sleep apnea (CSA) subtypes: hypercapnic vs. hypocapnic (Cheyne-Stokes).

**A:** Hypercapnic CSA: decreased CNS drive to breathe, seen in hypoventilation syndromes (obesity, neuromuscular disease); associated with daytime hypercapnia and somnolence; treated with BiPAP. Hypocapnic CSA (Cheyne-Stokes): crescendo-decrescendo breathing alternating with apnea; seen in CHF, AF, stroke; CO2 swings drive periodic breathing; adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) may cause harm (not recommended in CHF with reduced EF).

Cheyne-Stokes breathing occurs when cardiac output is low, delaying chemoreceptor feedback. During the hyperpneic phase, CO2 is blown off below the apneic threshold, causing central apnea, then CO2 rises again triggering hyperventilation. Treatment focuses on optimizing heart failure. ASV was shown to increase mortality in HFrEF (SERVE-HF trial).

**Q:** What is polysomnography (PSG) and what does it measure?

**A:** PSG is an in-laboratory sleep study attended by a technologist. It measures: sleep stages and arousals (EEG, EOG, EMG), airflow, ribcage and abdominal movement, ECG, SaO2, limb movements, snoring, body position, and video recording.

PSG is the gold standard for diagnosing sleep disorders. Indications include: suspected sleep-disordered breathing, evaluation of non-respiratory causes of excessive daytime sleepiness, selected insomnia and parasomnias, PAP titration, and assessment of response to interventions. Home sleep apnea testing is limited to patients with suspected OSA without significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities.

**Q:** When is home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) appropriate vs. polysomnography?

**A:** HSAT is appropriate for evaluation of suspected OSA in patients without significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities. PSG is required when: non-respiratory causes of sleepiness are suspected, parasomnias or insomnia need evaluation, PAP titration is needed, or significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities exist that may confound HSAT.

HSAT measures a limited number of cardiorespiratory parameters (airflow, oximetry, respiratory effort) without EEG. Because sleep time is estimated rather than measured, AHI from HSAT may underestimate severity. False negatives are more common in patients with complex sleep disorders or low-risk profiles for OSA.

**Q:** What is the obesity-hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian syndrome)?

**A:** OHS is defined as BMI >30 kg/m2 with daytime hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mmHg) not explained by another cause of hypoventilation. Nocturnal hypoventilation (often with superimposed OSA) blunts the central CO2 response over time. Treatment: nocturnal BiPAP, weight loss.

The term 'Pickwickian' comes from the obese, somnolent boy in Dickens' Pickwick Papers. Excess weight reduces chest wall compliance and functional residual capacity, promoting hypoventilation especially during sleep. OHS is distinguished from OSA alone by daytime hypercapnia. BiPAP provides both airway splinting and ventilatory support.

**Q:** What are the goals and monitoring targets of an ICU arterial line?

**A:** Arterial lines provide continuous beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring and easy access for serial ABG sampling. Common sites: radial (preferred) and femoral arteries.

Radial arterial lines are preferred due to collateral ulnar circulation (Allen test). Femoral lines are used when radial access is not available or when the patient is in shock with poor peripheral perfusion. Arterial lines allow real-time waveform analysis for fluid responsiveness (pulse pressure variation).

**Q:** What does pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) reflect and what are the absolute contraindications to pulmonary artery catheter insertion?

**A:** PCWP reflects left atrial and left ventricular diastolic pressure (barring pulmonary venous or mitral valve disease). Absolute contraindications: tricuspid/pulmonary valve mechanical prosthesis, right heart mass (thrombus or tumour), tricuspid/pulmonary valve endocarditis.

The pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) is balloon-tipped; when wedged in a distal pulmonary artery, flow ceases and the catheter 'sees' downstream left atrial pressure. PCWP >18 mmHg suggests cardiogenic pulmonary edema (vs. ARDS). Its use has declined due to lack of mortality benefit and complications (arrhythmia, PA rupture, infection).

**Q:** What is the SOFA score and what SOFA score reflects a >10% overall mortality risk in suspected infection?

**A:** SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) scores dysfunction in 6 systems: respiratory (P:F ratio), coagulation (platelets), liver (bilirubin), cardiovascular (MAP/vasopressors), CNS (GCS), and renal (creatinine/urine output). A SOFA score ≥2 reflects an overall mortality risk of approximately 10% in suspected infection.

The Sepsis-3 definitions (2016) replaced SIRS criteria with SOFA for identifying organ dysfunction in sepsis. Each domain is scored 0-4 based on severity of dysfunction. A change from baseline SOFA ≥2 defines organ dysfunction attributable to infection, supporting sepsis diagnosis. SOFA requires laboratory data, making qSOFA more practical at the bedside.

**Q:** What are the three qSOFA (quick SOFA) criteria used to screen for poor outcomes in suspected infection?

**A:** 1. Respiratory rate ≥22/min, 2. Altered mental status (GCS <15), 3. Systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg. A positive qSOFA (≥2 criteria) should prompt full SOFA assessment.

qSOFA is a bedside screening tool that does not require laboratory tests. Its positive predictive value for poor outcomes (prolonged ICU stay, death) is good, but sensitivity is lower than SOFA. Absence of ≥2 qSOFA criteria should NOT delay investigation or treatment if clinical suspicion for infection remains high.

**Q:** Define septic shock per Sepsis-3 criteria.

**A:** Septic shock = sepsis + persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg AND serum lactate >2 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Sepsis-3 (JAMA 2016) defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction due to dysregulated host response to infection (SOFA ≥2). Septic shock is a subset with circulatory/cellular/metabolic abnormalities that substantially increase mortality (in-hospital mortality >40%). Both vasopressor requirement AND elevated lactate must be present, distinguishing it from simple sepsis.

**Q:** What are the initial resuscitation targets in the first 6 hours for sepsis-induced hypotension or lactate >4 mmol/L?

**A:** CVP 8-12 mmHg (with IV crystalloids/colloids), MAP ≥65 mmHg (vasopressors: first-line norepinephrine), urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h, central venous O2 saturation (SVC) ≥70% or mixed venous ≥65%, and normalize lactate in patients with elevated lactate.

These are the Surviving Sepsis Campaign goals based on early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) principles. Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor for septic shock. Corticosteroids are NOT indicated if hemodynamic stability is achieved with fluids and vasopressors. Empiric antibiotics should be administered within the first hour of sepsis recognition.

**Q:** What is the first-line vasopressor for septic shock, and when should corticosteroids be added?

**A:** Norepinephrine (Levophed) is first-line. Corticosteroid replacement therapy is NOT indicated if adequate hemodynamic stability is achieved with fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy.

Norepinephrine increases MAP primarily through vasoconstriction (alpha-1) with modest inotropy (beta-1). Vasopressin can be added as a second agent to reduce norepinephrine dose. Hydrocortisone is considered in refractory septic shock (vasopressor-dependent despite adequate fluids) to address relative adrenal insufficiency.

**Q:** What are the four categories of shock and their hemodynamic profiles (HR, BP, CO, extremities)?

**A:** Hypovolemic: ↑HR, ↓BP, ↓CO, cold extremities. Cardiogenic: ↑HR, ↓BP, ↓CO, cold extremities + bilateral crackles/signs of LHF. Obstructive: ↑HR, ↓BP, ↓CO, variable (may have pulsus paradoxus, Kussmaul's sign, or tracheal deviation). Distributive: ↑HR, ↓BP, ↑CO (initially), warm extremities.

Distributive shock (sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic) has vasodilation with initially high cardiac output and warm periphery. The diagnostic 'warm shock' of distributive states contrasts with the 'cold shock' of hypovolemic and cardiogenic states. Obstructive shock (PE, tamponade, tension pneumothorax) impedes cardiac filling or outflow.

**Q:** What is SIRS and what are its diagnostic criteria?

**A:** Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome: a generalized inflammatory reaction caused by infectious or non-infectious triggers, defined by ≥2 of: Temperature >38°C or <36°C, Heart rate >90/min, Respiratory rate >20/min or PaCO2 <32 mmHg, WBC >12,000/mL or <4,000/mL or >10% bands.

SIRS is a clinical descriptor, not a diagnosis. While superseded by qSOFA/SOFA for sepsis identification (Sepsis-3), SIRS criteria remain useful for recognizing systemic inflammation from non-infectious causes (e.g., pancreatitis, burns, surgery). Sepsis was previously defined as SIRS due to infection; the newer definition focuses on organ dysfunction.

**Q:** What are the mechanisms by which tension pneumothorax causes obstructive shock, and what is the emergency treatment?

**A:** Tension pneumothorax causes progressive air accumulation in the pleural space, compressing the ipsilateral lung and shifting mediastinum contralaterally, impairing venous return to the right heart and reducing cardiac output. Emergency treatment: immediate needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line) followed by tube thoracostomy.

Obstructive shock impedes cardiac filling (preload) or ejection. Tension pneumothorax is recognized by tracheal deviation away from affected side, absent breath sounds, hypotension, and JVD. It is a clinical diagnosis — do NOT wait for CXR. The obstructive shock table shows increased JVP, absent/reduced breath sounds, and possible pulsus paradoxus.

**Q:** What is the formula for mean arterial pressure (MAP) and what MAP target is used in septic shock?

**A:** MAP = 1/3 sBP + 2/3 dBP = dBP + 1/3 pulse pressure. Target MAP ≥65 mmHg in septic shock (using vasopressors if fluid resuscitation is insufficient).

MAP reflects average perfusion pressure throughout the cardiac cycle. Since diastole is ~2/3 of the cycle, diastolic pressure is weighted more heavily. MAP ≥65 mmHg ensures adequate perfusion to vital organs including the kidneys (urine output target ≥0.5 mL/kg/h) and brain. Higher MAP targets (e.g., ≥80) may benefit patients with chronic hypertension.

**Q:** What is the P:F ratio (P/F ratio) and how is it used in clinical practice?

**A:** P:F ratio = PaO2 / FiO2. It quantifies oxygenation efficiency. Normal: ~400-500 mmHg. Used in ARDS Berlin classification (Mild: 200-300, Moderate: 100-200, Severe: <100) and SOFA respiratory scoring (≥400=0, <400=1, <300=2, <200 with RS=3, <100 with RS=4).

The P:F ratio normalizes PaO2 for the fraction of oxygen delivered. A P:F <300 suggests significant pulmonary dysfunction. In ARDS, all severity categories require PEEP ≥5 cmH2O. P:F ratio is also used in the SOFA score and is a key monitoring parameter in mechanically ventilated patients.

**Q:** What are the indications for a pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) in the ICU?

**A:** Diagnosis and management of: shock (differentiating shock types), primary pulmonary hypertension, valvular disease, intracardiac shunts, cardiac tamponade, PE; assessment of hemodynamic response to therapies; differentiating high-pressure (cardiogenic) vs. low-pressure (ARDS) pulmonary edema; complicated MI; multiorgan failure; severe burns; hemodynamic instability post-cardiac surgery.

Despite these indications, the pulmonary artery catheter is now seldom used due to associated complications (arrhythmias, pulmonary artery rupture, thrombosis, infection) and lack of demonstrated mortality benefit in randomized trials. It has largely been replaced by point-of-care echocardiography and less invasive hemodynamic monitoring.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action and key clinical role of short-acting beta-2 agonists (SABAs) in respiratory disease?

**A:** SABAs (e.g., salbutamol/albuterol) stimulate beta-2 receptors in bronchial smooth muscle, causing bronchodilation. They are used as rescue bronchodilators in acute COPD exacerbations and asthma. Onset: minutes; duration: 4-6 hours.

Beta-2 receptor activation increases cAMP, relaxing airway smooth muscle. Cardiovascular side effects (tachycardia, palpitations, hypokalemia) result from beta-1 crossover and systemic absorption. Salbutamol (Ventolin) is the prototypical SABA in Canada. Overuse of SABAs without ICS in asthma is a marker of poor control.

**Q:** What are the side effects of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and what systemic complication is of particular concern in COPD?

**A:** Local: oropharyngeal candidiasis, dysphonia, throat irritation. Systemic: growth velocity reduction in children/adolescents, HPA axis suppression, and in COPD patients specifically — increased risk of pneumonia.

ICS reduce airway inflammation and are cornerstone therapy in asthma. In COPD, ICS (particularly fluticasone) are associated with increased pneumonia risk without mortality benefit when used in moderate COPD. They are indicated in COPD with frequent exacerbations, eosinophilia, or concomitant asthma features. Rinsing the mouth after use reduces candidiasis.

**Q:** What is the role of roflumilast (Daxas) in COPD and what are its key side effects?

**A:** Roflumilast is a PDE-4 inhibitor indicated for chronic bronchitis with frequent exacerbations (reduces exacerbation rate). Dose: 500 mcg PO once daily. Key side effects: weight loss, suicidal ideation (black box warning).

Phosphodiesterase-4 inhibition increases intracellular cAMP in inflammatory cells, reducing neutrophilic airway inflammation. Roflumilast is not a bronchodilator but reduces exacerbation frequency in the COPD chronic bronchitis phenotype (productive cough ≥3 months/year for 2+ consecutive years). Weight loss is common and limits use in underweight patients.

**Q:** What is the first-line antibiotic for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in Canada, and what are alternatives?

**A:** For outpatient CAP: macrolide (azithromycin, clarithromycin) or doxycycline as first-line. Fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) is an alternative. For inpatient CAP: beta-lactam + macrolide, or respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy.

Macrolides cover Streptococcus pneumoniae, atypical organisms (Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila, Legionella). Doxycycline is an alternative with similar coverage. Fluoroquinolones cover both typical and atypical pathogens and are used when macrolide resistance is a concern or patient has risk factors for resistant organisms. Key macrolide side effect: prolonged QT interval.

**Q:** What antibiotics are used for hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) with suspected Pseudomonas vs. suspected MRSA?

**A:** Suspected Pseudomonas: piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g IV q6-8h), imipenem (500 mg IV q6h), or meropenem (1 g IV q8h), combined with a fluoroquinolone or macrolide. Suspected MRSA: add vancomycin (1 g IV BID x7-10 days). 4th-gen cephalosporin (cefepime) covers MSSA.

HAP (occurring ≥48h after hospital admission) involves more resistant organisms. Risk factors for Pseudomonas: structural lung disease, recent broad-spectrum antibiotics, ICU admission. MRSA risk: prior MRSA colonization, dialysis, skin/soft tissue infection. Vancomycin's 'red man syndrome' (flushing, hypotension) is rate-related, not allergic — slow the infusion.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action and key toxicity of theophylline?

**A:** Theophylline is a methylxanthine that inhibits phosphodiesterase, increases cAMP, and blocks adenosine receptors, causing bronchodilation and mild anti-inflammatory effects. Used for reversible airway obstruction in COPD. Toxicity (narrow therapeutic window): nausea/vomiting, seizures, arrhythmias. Toxic levels cause persistent vomiting and seizures.

Theophylline has a narrow therapeutic index (target serum level 5-15 mg/L). Many drug interactions alter its metabolism (erythromycin, ciprofloxacin increase levels; rifampin, phenytoin decrease levels). Seizures from theophylline toxicity can be refractory. GI upset is a common dose-related side effect even at therapeutic levels.

**Q:** What is the role of omalizumab (Xolair) in asthma and how is it administered?

**A:** Omalizumab is an anti-IgE biologic monoclonal antibody indicated for moderate-to-severe persistent allergic asthma not controlled with standard therapy. Dose: 150-375 mg SC every 2-4 weeks (weight- and IgE-dependent). Key side effects: anaphylaxis, HA, sinusitis, URTI, thrombocytopenia.

Omalizumab binds free IgE, preventing it from binding to mast cell and basophil receptors and inhibiting the allergic cascade. It reduces exacerbations, hospitalizations, and oral corticosteroid use in IgE-mediated asthma. Anaphylaxis risk mandates 30-min observation after each injection. Other biologics (dupilumab, mepolizumab) target eosinophilic asthma.

**Q:** What are the indications for systemic corticosteroids in respiratory disease and their major side effects?

**A:** Indications: acute COPD exacerbation, severe/persistent asthma, status asthmaticus, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP). Typical dose: prednisone 40-60 mg/d PO or methylprednisolone 125 mg q8h IV. Major side effects: Cushing's syndrome, DM/glucose intolerance, HPA axis suppression, avascular necrosis, osteoporosis, cataracts, glaucoma, psychiatric effects, easy bruising.

Short courses (5-7 days) for COPD exacerbation reduce treatment failure and hospitalization duration. PCP adjunctive corticosteroids are indicated when PaO2 <70 mmHg or A-a gradient >35 mmHg, reducing mortality. Long-term systemic corticosteroids require monitoring for metabolic, bone, and ophthalmologic complications.

**Q:** What is montelukast's mechanism and indication, and what are its neuropsychiatric side effects?

**A:** Montelukast is a leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA) that blocks cysteinyl leukotriene receptors, reducing bronchoconstriction and inflammation. Indicated for chronic asthma and prophylaxis of exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. Dose: 10 mg PO once daily. Neuropsychiatric side effects: anxiety, depression, agitation, attention/memory impairment, insomnia — prompted an FDA/Health Canada black box warning.

Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) are potent bronchoconstrictors produced via the 5-lipoxygenase pathway. Montelukast is particularly useful in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (aspirin/NSAID-sensitive asthma) and allergic rhinitis comorbidity. It is less effective than ICS for asthma control but has a role as an add-on therapy.

**Q:** Compare CPAP vs. BiPAP: mechanism, indications in sleep/respiratory disorders.

**A:** CPAP delivers a single constant positive pressure throughout inspiration and expiration — splints upper airway open. Indicated for OSA. BiPAP delivers higher inspiratory pressure (IPAP) and lower expiratory pressure (EPAP) — provides ventilatory support in addition to airway splinting. Indicated for: hypoventilation syndromes (obesity-hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease), hypercapnic respiratory failure, hypercapnic CSA, acute COPD exacerbation with hypercapnia.

CPAP is sufficient when the problem is airway obstruction (OSA) without hypoventilation. BiPAP augments tidal volume by cycling between IPAP and EPAP, reducing work of breathing and improving alveolar ventilation. The pressure support (IPAP-EPAP difference) drives tidal volume. BiPAP is the mode of choice for acute hypercapnic exacerbations of COPD (reduces intubation rate and mortality).

**Q:** A patient presents with hypoxemia and septic shock. Their mixed venous O2 is very low. How does this type of hypoxemia differ mechanistically from other types?

**A:** Low mixed venous O2 content (Type 5 hypoxemia) occurs when peripheral tissues extract more O2 (e.g., in shock with reduced cardiac output). Even normal lungs cannot fully reoxygenate blood that arrives severely desaturated. The A-a gradient is elevated because venous blood is so desaturated that even matched V/Q units return reduced PaO2. Treatment: improve cardiac output/O2 delivery.

In shock, reduced CO forces higher O2 extraction (widened a-vO2 difference, low SvO2). This mixed venous desaturation 'loads' pulmonary capillaries with more deoxygenated blood. Supplemental O2 and BiPAP may help modestly, but the key intervention is increasing cardiac output (fluids, vasopressors, inotropes). This explains why SvO2 monitoring guides resuscitation in sepsis.

**Q:** What are the sepsis bundle management priorities (Surviving Sepsis Campaign) in the first hour?

**A:** Within 1 hour: (1) Measure lactate (if >2 mmol/L or septic shock suspected, initiate full bundle); (2) Blood cultures x2 before antibiotics; (3) Administer broad-spectrum IV antibiotics; (4) 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥4 mmol/L; (5) Start vasopressors (norepinephrine) if MAP <65 mmHg despite fluids.

Early source identification (blood cultures, imaging) guides de-escalation. Antibiotics within 1 hour of sepsis recognition is associated with improved survival. The 1-hour bundle replaced the previous 3-hour and 6-hour bundles (2018 update). Lactate normalization serves as a resuscitation endpoint when initial lactate is elevated.

**Q:** What are the ICU sedative options for mechanically ventilated patients and their key side effects?

**A:** Fentanyl (opioid): 50-100 mcg bolus then infusion — sedation/analgesia; bradycardia, respiratory depression, hypotension, apnea. Propofol (anesthetic): 1-3 mg/kg then 0.3-5 mg/kg/h IV — sedation (good for ventilator sedation, allows rapid awakening); bradycardia, hypotension, propofol infusion syndrome (rare).

Propofol's short context-sensitive half-life makes it ideal for daily sedation interruption ('awakening trials') and neurological assessment. Fentanyl provides analgesia and sedation, addressing pain which is a major driver of agitation in ventilated patients. Benzodiazepines (lorazepam, midazolam) are now avoided in favor of analgesia-first sedation protocols to reduce ICU delirium.

**Q:** What are the side effects of vancomycin, and what is 'red man syndrome'?

**A:** Vancomycin side effects: ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity (interstitial nephritis, renal failure), red man syndrome, CNS effects (drug fever, chills), hematologic (eosinophilia). Red man syndrome: flushing, erythema, and hypotension due to histamine release from rapid infusion — NOT an allergic reaction; managed by slowing the infusion rate.

Vancomycin requires therapeutic drug monitoring (trough levels or AUC/MIC ratio). It is used for MRSA, serious Gram-positive infections, and C. difficile colitis (oral route). Red man syndrome occurs with infusion rates >10 mg/min. Premedication with antihistamines can prevent it. True vancomycin allergy (urticaria, anaphylaxis) is rare but precludes use.

**Q:** A patient in the ICU has septic shock with MAP 55 mmHg after 2L IV crystalloid. What vasopressor is first-line and what is the target MAP?

**A:** First-line vasopressor: norepinephrine (Levophed). Target MAP: ≥65 mmHg. Norepinephrine primarily acts on alpha-1 receptors (vasoconstriction) with some beta-1 activity (inotropy), effectively raising MAP in distributive shock.

Dopamine was previously used but is now avoided as first-line due to higher rates of arrhythmia and increased mortality in cardiogenic shock subgroups (SOAP II trial). Vasopressin (0.03 units/min) can be added as a norepinephrine-sparing agent. Phenylephrine (pure alpha-1) is used in anesthesia-related hypotension or when tachycardia is a concern.

**Q:** What did the ARDS Network trial (NEJM 2000) demonstrate about tidal volume strategy in ARDS?

**A:** Low tidal volume ventilation (6 mL/kg) reduced mortality compared to traditional tidal volume (12 mL/kg): 31.0% vs. 39.8% mortality (P=0.007), and increased ventilator-free days.

Lung-protective ventilation using low tidal volumes (6 mL/kg of ideal body weight) is now standard of care for ARDS in Canadian ICUs, reducing barotrauma and volutrauma-related mortality. This is a cornerstone trial that established the low-tidal-volume strategy for all mechanically ventilated ARDS patients.

**Q:** What was the key finding of the ACURASYS trial regarding neuromuscular blockade in early severe ARDS?

**A:** Early cisatracurium besylate for 48 hours in severe ARDS reduced 90-day mortality (31.6% vs. 40.7%) and decreased ventilator time, without increasing ICU-acquired paresis.

ACURASYS (NEJM 2010) demonstrated that early neuromuscular blockade improves survival in severe ARDS. This is relevant to Canadian ICU practice for patients with P/F ratio <150 mmHg, though subsequent ROSE trial showed no benefit in all ARDS — context of severe ARDS is key.

**Q:** What did the OSCILLATE trial (NEJM 2013) show about high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) in moderate-to-severe ARDS?

**A:** HFOV increased in-hospital mortality compared to conventional ventilation: 47% vs. 35% (RR 1.33; P=0.005). Early HFOV may be harmful in moderate-to-severe ARDS.

OSCILLATE showed that HFOV, despite theoretical advantages (sustained lung recruitment, reduced volutrauma), actually worsened outcomes in ARDS, likely due to increased sedation and vasopressor requirements. This trial led to abandonment of routine HFOV use in ARDS in Canadian ICUs.

**Q:** What were the main results of the PROSEVA trial on prone positioning in severe ARDS?

**A:** Prone positioning sessions >16 hours/day in severe ARDS reduced 28-day mortality from 32.8% to 16.0% (HR 0.39; P<0.001) and 90-day mortality from 41.0% to 23.6% (HR 0.44; P<0.001).

PROSEVA (NEJM 2013) established prolonged prone positioning (>16 hrs/day) as a life-saving intervention in severe ARDS (P/F ratio <150 with FiO2 ≥0.6 and PEEP ≥5). This is now standard care in Canadian ICUs for severe ARDS, markedly reducing mortality without significant increase in complications.

**Q:** What safety concern did the SMART trial (Chest 2006) identify about salmeterol in asthma?

**A:** Salmeterol added to usual asthma care increased the risk of respiratory-related and asthma-related deaths, particularly among African American patients not using baseline ICS.

The SMART trial led to a black box warning for LABAs as monotherapy in asthma. In Canadian asthma guidelines (CTS), LABAs must always be combined with ICS in asthma management. LABAs should never be used as monotherapy for asthma.

**Q:** What did the EXTRA trial (Ann Intern Med 2011) demonstrate about omalizumab in severe allergic asthma?

**A:** Omalizumab added to high-dose ICS plus LABA in inadequately controlled severe allergic asthma reduced exacerbation rates (0.66 vs. 0.88; P=0.006) without increasing serious adverse events.

Omalizumab is a monoclonal anti-IgE antibody indicated for severe allergic asthma inadequately controlled on maximum inhaler therapy. In Canada, it is approved for adults and children ≥6 years with severe persistent allergic asthma confirmed by elevated IgE and positive skin test/RAST.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of tezepelumab, and what did the PATHWAY trial show?

**A:** Tezepelumab is an anti-TSLP (thymic stromal lymphopoietin) monoclonal antibody. In PATHWAY (NEJM 2017), it reduced asthma exacerbation rates by 62–71% vs. placebo in patients uncontrolled on LABA and medium-to-high dose ICS.

Tezepelumab targets TSLP, an upstream epithelial cytokine that drives type 2 and non-type 2 inflammation. It is unique among biologics in that it works regardless of eosinophil count, making it appropriate for a broader asthma population with uncontrolled disease despite LABA+ICS therapy.

**Q:** What did the Novel START trial (NEJM 2019) show about as-needed budesonide-formoterol vs. SABA alone in mild asthma?

**A:** As-needed budesonide-formoterol reduced annualized exacerbation rate vs. albuterol alone (RR 0.49; P<0.01) and was non-inferior to regular budesonide maintenance therapy.

This trial established that as-needed ICS-formoterol (rather than SABA alone) is a safe and effective strategy in mild asthma. Canadian CTS guidelines now recommend as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol as a preferred option for mild asthma, reducing cumulative ICS dose while maintaining exacerbation protection.

**Q:** What did the PrimoTinA-asthma trials show about adding tiotropium to LABA+ICS in uncontrolled asthma?

**A:** Adding tiotropium to LABA+ICS improved peak FEV1 (86–154 mL above placebo) and delayed time to first severe exacerbation by 56 days in patients with asthma uncontrolled on dual therapy.

Tiotropium (a long-acting muscarinic antagonist, LAMA) added as a third controller agent (triple therapy) in asthma uncontrolled on LABA+ICS improves lung function and reduces exacerbations. This is recommended in Canadian guidelines as a step-up option before escalating to biologics.

**Q:** What were the key findings of the TORCH trial regarding LABA+ICS combination therapy in COPD?

**A:** Salmeterol+fluticasone combination showed a trend toward reduced all-cause mortality vs. placebo (12.6% vs. 15.2%; HR 0.825; P=0.052, not significant) and significantly reduced exacerbations. Neither salmeterol nor fluticasone alone reduced mortality.

TORCH (NEJM 2007) showed that ICS+LABA combination improves symptoms and reduces exacerbations in COPD, though the mortality benefit did not reach statistical significance. It established ICS+LABA as superior to monotherapy for exacerbation-prone COPD, which is reflected in Canadian CTS COPD guidelines.

**Q:** What did the UPLIFT trial (NEJM 2008) demonstrate about tiotropium's effect on FEV1 decline in COPD?

**A:** Tiotropium maintained better FEV1 (87–103 mL better before bronchodilation) and reduced exacerbations compared to placebo over 4 years, but did not significantly alter the rate of FEV1 decline.

UPLIFT established that tiotropium (LAMA) improves symptoms and reduces exacerbations in COPD but does not modify disease progression as measured by FEV1 decline rate. This distinction is important: LAMAs are preferred for symptom control and exacerbation prevention, not disease modification.

**Q:** In the POET-COPD trial (NEJM 2011), which agent was superior for preventing COPD exacerbations: tiotropium or salmeterol?

**A:** Tiotropium was superior to salmeterol: it increased time to first exacerbation with a 17% risk reduction (HR 0.83; P<0.001) and reduced moderate and severe exacerbations (0.64 vs. 0.72; P=0.002).

POET-COPD demonstrated that LAMAs (tiotropium) are preferred over LABAs (salmeterol) for preventing exacerbations in moderate-to-severe COPD. Canadian CTS guidelines recommend LAMAs as first-line bronchodilator therapy for COPD patients with exacerbations.

**Q:** What did the REDUCE trial (JAMA 2013) establish about corticosteroid duration for acute COPD exacerbations?

**A:** A 5-day course of prednisone 40 mg was non-inferior to a 14-day course for acute COPD exacerbations, with no difference in time to next exacerbation, mortality, or recovery of lung function.

REDUCE established that shorter (5-day) oral corticosteroid courses are as effective as conventional 14-day courses for acute COPD exacerbations. Current Canadian guidelines recommend 5 days of prednisone 40 mg for COPD exacerbations, minimizing corticosteroid side effects.

**Q:** What did the FLAME trial (NEJM 2016) show about LABA+LAMA vs. LABA+ICS in COPD?

**A:** Indacaterol-glycopyrronium (LABA+LAMA) reduced COPD exacerbation rate by 11% compared to salmeterol-fluticasone (LABA+ICS): rate ratio 0.89 (P=0.003), with lower moderate-severe exacerbations.

FLAME demonstrated that dual bronchodilation (LABA+LAMA) is superior to LABA+ICS for preventing exacerbations in COPD patients at high risk (≥1 exacerbation in prior year). Canadian CTS guidelines now favor LABA+LAMA over LABA+ICS as dual therapy in most COPD patients, reserving ICS for those with concomitant asthma features or eosinophilia.

**Q:** What did the IMPACT trial (NEJM 2018) demonstrate about triple therapy (ICS+LAMA+LABA) in COPD?

**A:** Triple therapy (fluticasone+umeclidinium+vilanterol) reduced moderate-to-severe exacerbations vs. dual therapy: rate 0.91/yr vs. 1.07/yr (fluticasone+vilanterol) and 1.21/yr (umeclidinium+vilanterol). Hospitalization rate was also lower.

IMPACT established triple inhaler therapy (ICS+LAMA+LABA) as the most effective regimen for reducing exacerbations and hospitalizations in high-risk COPD. In Canadian practice, triple therapy is recommended for patients with frequent exacerbations not controlled on dual therapy.

**Q:** What critical safety finding did the PANTHER trial (NEJM 2012) reveal about combination therapy for IPF?

**A:** Triple therapy (prednisone + azathioprine + N-acetylcysteine) for IPF increased mortality (8 vs. 1 deaths) and hospitalization (23 vs. 7) compared to placebo. The trial was terminated early.

PANTHER overturned prior assumptions that immunosuppression might benefit IPF. It confirmed IPF is not primarily an inflammatory disease and that immunosuppression is harmful. Canadian guidelines now contraindicate triple therapy (prednisone+azathioprine+NAC) in IPF.

**Q:** What did the INPULSIS trials show about nintedanib in IPF?

**A:** Nintedanib 150 mg BID significantly slowed FVC decline in IPF compared to placebo: difference of ~125 mL/year (INPULSIS-1) and ~94 mL/year (INPULSIS-2), with a reduction in acute IPF exacerbations but no mortality difference.

Nintedanib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that slows fibrosis progression in IPF. Along with pirfenidone, it is a first-line antifibrotic agent for IPF in Canada. The main side effect is GI toxicity (diarrhea, nausea). It also showed benefit in progressive fibrosing ILDs (INBUILD trial).

**Q:** What did the ASCEND trial (NEJM 2014) demonstrate about pirfenidone in IPF?

**A:** Pirfenidone 2403 mg/day reduced the proportion of patients with ≥10% FVC decline by 47.9% compared to placebo (P<0.001) and improved progression-free survival, with no significant difference in mortality in this trial alone.

Pirfenidone is an antifibrotic agent (mechanism not fully understood, likely anti-TGF-β effects) that slows disease progression in IPF. It is approved in Canada alongside nintedanib as first-line antifibrotic therapy. Main side effects include photosensitivity and GI upset.

**Q:** What did the INBUILD trial (NEJM 2019) show about nintedanib in progressive fibrosing ILDs beyond IPF?

**A:** Nintedanib reduced annual FVC decline in progressive fibrosing ILDs of any cause: -80.8 mL/year vs. -187.8 mL/year with placebo; benefit was most pronounced in patients with UIP pattern on imaging.

INBUILD established that nintedanib's antifibrotic benefit extends to non-IPF progressive fibrosing ILDs (e.g., connective tissue disease-associated ILD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis with progressive fibrosis). This broadened its approval indications in Canada.

**Q:** What did the EINSTEIN-PE trial (NEJM 2012) demonstrate about rivaroxaban for pulmonary embolism treatment?

**A:** Rivaroxaban was non-inferior to standard therapy (enoxaparin then vitamin K antagonist) for symptomatic PE: 2.1% vs. 1.8% recurrent VTE (HR 1.12; non-inferiority P=0.003), with similar major bleeding rates.

EINSTEIN-PE established rivaroxaban as an effective oral DOAC for both initial and long-term treatment of PE, replacing the need for parenteral bridging therapy. Canadian Thrombosis guidelines now recommend DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran) as preferred agents over warfarin for VTE treatment.

**Q:** What did the PEITHO trial (NEJM 2014) show about thrombolysis (tenecteplase) for intermediate-risk PE?

**A:** Tenecteplase reduced death or hemodynamic decompensation within 7 days (2.6% vs. 5.6%; OR 0.44; P=0.02) but significantly increased extracranial bleeding (6.3% vs. 1.2%) and stroke (2.4% vs. 0.2%; P=0.003).

PEITHO demonstrated that systemic thrombolysis prevents hemodynamic deterioration in intermediate-risk (submassive) PE but at significant hemorrhagic cost. Canadian guidelines reserve systemic thrombolysis for massive PE with hemodynamic instability; for intermediate-risk PE, anticoagulation alone is standard, with rescue thrombolysis if deterioration occurs.

**Q:** What did the SAVE trial (NEJM 2016) show about CPAP in obstructive sleep apnea and cardiovascular outcomes?

**A:** CPAP with standard care did not reduce cardiovascular events (death, MI, stroke, hospitalization for CHF/ACS/TIA) compared to standard care alone in patients with OSA (HR 1.10; P=0.34).

The SAVE trial showed CPAP does not prevent cardiovascular events in OSA patients who already have established cardiovascular disease. However, CPAP remains indicated for symptomatic OSA (excessive daytime somnolence, impaired quality of life). Canadian guidelines recommend CPAP for symptom relief, not solely for cardiovascular risk reduction.

**Q:** What did the NICE-SUGAR trial (NEJM 2009) show about intensive glucose control in critically ill patients?

**A:** Intensive glucose control (target 81–108 mg/dL) increased 90-day mortality vs. conventional control (target ≤180 mg/dL): OR for death 1.14 (95% CI 1.02–1.28; P=0.02).

NICE-SUGAR established that tight glycemic control (targeting normoglycemia) is harmful in ICU patients, primarily due to hypoglycemic events. Current Canadian Critical Care guidelines recommend targeting blood glucose ≤10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL) in critically ill patients, avoiding both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia.

**Q:** What did the ARISE trial (NEJM 2014) demonstrate about early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) in septic shock?

**A:** EGDT did not reduce all-cause mortality compared to usual care in patients with early septic shock: absolute risk difference -0.3% (95% CI -4.1% to 3.6%; P=0.90).

ARISE (along with ProCESS and ProMISe trials) showed that the rigid EGDT protocol from the original Rivers trial did not improve outcomes over current usual care. This led to simplification of sepsis bundles; modern Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines focus on early antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and vasopressors as needed rather than rigid hemodynamic targets.

## Pulmonology

**Q:** What is the approach to a solitary pulmonary nodule?

**A:** Compare with prior CXR. CT characteristics favoring benign: <6mm, smooth borders, calcified (diffuse/central/laminated/popcorn), stable >2 years. Favoring malignant: >8mm (especially >3cm), spiculated borders, doubling time 20-400 days. PET scan to narrow differential. Biopsy if indeterminate.

70% of solitary pulmonary nodules are benign. Most common benign cause is infectious granuloma. Most common malignant cause is bronchogenic carcinoma. Hamartomas show pathognomonic 'popcorn' calcification.

## Resp Bronchiectasis

**Q:** What is the definition of bronchiectasis and what are its most common sputum pathogens?

**A:** Irreversible dilatation of airways due to inflammatory destruction of airway walls resulting from persistently impaired mucous clearance and/or infected mucus; usually affects medium-sized airways. Most common pathogens in non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis: Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Moraxella catarrhalis, and nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM).

Distinguishing bronchiectasis from chronic bronchitis can be difficult clinically. HRCT (high-resolution CT) is the gold standard: 'signet ring sign' (bronchus diameter >1.5× accompanying artery) and 'tram tracking' (parallel lines radiating from hilum) are classic findings. CXR shows tram-tracking and cystic spaces.

**Q:** What are the causes of bronchiectasis?

**A:** Obstruction: tumour, foreign body. Post-infectious (dilation of bronchial walls): pneumonia, nontuberculous mycobacterium, measles, pertussis. Impaired defenses/drainage: hypogammaglobulinemia, defective leukocyte function, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), ciliary dysfunction (Kartagener's syndrome: bronchiectasis + sinusitis + situs inversus).

Kartagener's syndrome is a form of primary ciliary dyskinesia with autosomal recessive inheritance causing bronchiectasis, chronic sinusitis, and situs inversus. ABPA (hypersensitivity reaction to Aspergillus) causes proximal central bronchiectasis and is associated with asthma and CF. CF is the most common cause of bronchiectasis in young patients.

**Q:** What are the radiological findings in bronchiectasis?

**A:** CXR: nonspecific (increased markings, linear atelectasis, volume loss) or specific ('tram tracking' — parallel narrow lines radiating from hilum, cystic spaces). HRCT (gold standard, 87–97% sensitivity, 93–100% specificity): 'signet ring sign' — dilated bronchi with thickened walls where diameter of bronchus is >1.5× diameter of accompanying pulmonary artery.

HRCT has replaced bronchography as the gold standard for diagnosing and characterizing bronchiectasis. The signet ring appearance is pathognomonic. Distribution helps identify etiology: upper lobe predominance suggests CF or ABPA; lower lobe predominance suggests traction from ILD; middle lobe/lingula suggests NTM.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for bronchiectasis?

**A:** Vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal). Chest physiotherapy, breathing exercises, physical exercise. Antibiotics: inhaled (chronically to decrease bacterial load in frequent exacerbators, especially if Pseudomonas in sputum); oral/IV for exacerbations driven by sputum sensitivity; macrolides for anti-inflammatory effect and exacerbation reduction. Mucolytics (hypertonic saline). ICS only if concurrent asthma or other indication. Pulmonary resection for focal bronchiectasis. Transplant for end-stage diffuse causes (e.g., primary ciliary dyskinesia, CF).

Oral corticosteroids generally have no role unless ABPA is present. Airway clearance (physiotherapy, oscillating devices) is fundamental. Inhaled antibiotics (tobramycin, colistin) are used for chronic suppression in Pseudomonas colonization. Regular sputum cultures guide antibiotic selection.

**Q:** What is Kartagener's syndrome and how does it cause bronchiectasis?

**A:** Kartagener's syndrome is a form of primary ciliary dyskinesia (autosomal recessive) consisting of the triad: bronchiectasis + sinusitis + situs inversus. Ciliary dysfunction leads to impaired mucociliary clearance, resulting in chronic infections and inflammatory destruction of airway walls → bronchiectasis. Ciliary dysfunction also causes abnormal left-right asymmetry determination during embryogenesis → situs inversus.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) causes bronchiectasis, chronic sinusitis, and infertility (immotile sperm). Half of PCD patients have situs inversus (Kartagener's syndrome). Diagnosis: nasal nitric oxide (↓ in PCD) and nasal ciliary biopsy. These patients may require lung transplantation for end-stage disease.

**Q:** A 28-year-old woman presents with recurrent pneumonia, chronic productive cough with copious purulent sputum, hemoptysis, and chronic sinusitis. HRCT chest shows dilated bronchi with thickened walls and a bronchus-to-artery ratio >1.5. What is the diagnosis, and what initial workup should be performed?

**A:** Bronchiectasis. Workup: PFTs (typically obstructive), CBC, LFTs, immunoglobulin panel (serum Ig levels), alpha-1 antitrypsin level, immunology panel (ANA, ENAs, RF, HIV), sweat chloride (if CF suspected given upper zone predominance or concomitant features), nasal nitric oxide and ciliary biopsy (if primary ciliary dyskinesia suspected), sputum cultures including AFB (routine and acid-fast bacillus).

The 'signet ring sign' on HRCT (bronchus diameter >1.5× pulmonary artery) is diagnostic. Workup aims to identify treatable underlying causes: hypogammaglobulinemia, CF, PCD, ABPA. Young female + sinusitis raises primary ciliary dyskinesia. Sweat chloride and genetic testing exclude CF. The most common pathogens are H. influenzae, P. aeruginosa, M. catarrhalis, NTM.

## Resp Cf

**Q:** What is the genetic basis of cystic fibrosis and what does sweat chloride testing show?

**A:** Autosomal recessive disorder with >2100 mutations in CFTR gene described (not all disease-causing). Sweat chloride test: increased concentrations of NaCl and K+; chloride >60 mmol/L on two occasions supports diagnosis. Single mutation carriers have normal sweat tests and no disease.

CFTR encodes a chloride channel; dysfunction leads to thick secretions from exocrine glands (lung, pancreas, reproductive tract) and blockage of secretory ducts. The most common mutation in Caucasians is ΔF508. Genetic testing can identify specific mutations, which guide CFTR modulator therapy selection.

**Q:** What are the multisystem clinical features of cystic fibrosis?

**A:** Pulmonary: sinusitis, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic lung infections. GI: pancreatic insufficiency, meconium ileus (children), distal ileal obstruction, liver disease, malnutrition. Other: salt loss syndrome, azoospermia, CF-related DM, bone disease. Chronic lung infections progress: S. aureus and H. influenzae (early) → P. aeruginosa (most common in adulthood) → Burkholderia cepacia complex (worse prognosis).

Burkholderia cepacia complex has some subtypes with much worse prognosis; infection control is critical (patient cohort separation). Adults increasingly colonized with resistant bacteria (PsA, B. cepacia, Stenotrophomonas, Achromobacter, MRSA, NTM). CF-related DM differs from type 1/2 DM and worsens pulmonary outcomes.

**Q:** What are the PFT findings in cystic fibrosis and how do they progress?

**A:** Early: airflow limitation in small airways. Late: severe airflow obstruction, hyperinflation, gas trapping. Very late: hypoxemia and hypercapnia → respiratory failure and cor pulmonale. CXR: hyperinflation and increased pulmonary markings (especially upper lobes).

CF primarily causes obstructive pattern on PFTs, unlike ILD which causes restriction. FEV1 decline rate is the strongest predictor of prognosis. Worse prognosis factors include: frequent pulmonary exacerbations, rapid FEV1 decline, supplemental O2 requirement, worsening malnutrition, infection with difficult-to-manage organisms, CF-DM, pneumothorax, massive hemoptysis, female sex, low SES.

**Q:** What are the CFTR modulator therapies for cystic fibrosis?

**A:** CFTR modulators include: Ivacaftor (potentiator for Class III mutations), Orkambi® (lumacaftor-ivacaftor for ΔF508 homozygous), Symdeko (tezacaftor-ivacaftor), Trikafta (elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor, most effective, for ΔF508 and other mutations). These are correctors/potentiators that restore CFTR protein function.

Lumacaftor-ivacaftor and tezacaftor-ivacaftor combination therapy improves QoL (respiratory domain) and lung function in Class II CFTR mutations versus placebo. Lumacaftor-ivacaftor caused more early breathlessness (OR 2.05). Tezacaftor-ivacaftor has fewer adverse effects. These drugs are mutation-class specific and expensive.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for cystic fibrosis?

**A:** Chest physiotherapy. Pancreatic enzyme replacements + high fat/high calorie diet. Bronchodilators (salbutamol + ipratropium bromide). Inhaled mucolytics: hypertonic saline, DNase (reduces mucus viscosity). Inhaled antibiotics (tobramycin, colistin, aztreonam, levofloxacin, vancomycin). Anti-inflammatory medications (azithromycin, ICS if concurrent asthma). Oral/IV antibiotics targeted to sputum cultures (e.g. ciprofloxacin for Pseudomonas). CFTR modulators (Ivacaftor, Orkambi, Symdeko, Trikafta). Lung transplant.

DNase (dornase alfa) cleaves extracellular DNA from neutrophils in mucus, reducing viscosity. Azithromycin has anti-inflammatory properties beyond antibacterial. Lung transplant is bilateral sequential and improves survival in CF with advanced disease. Infection control between CF patients is essential (Burkholderia cepacia).

## Resp Ild

**Q:** What is interstitial lung disease (ILD) and what are its typical PFT findings?

**A:** ILD is a group of disorders causing diffuse parenchymal lung disease with progressive scarring. Pathophysiology: inflammatory and/or fibrosing process in alveolar walls → distortion and destruction of normal alveoli and microvasculature. PFTs: restrictive pattern (↓TLC, ↓VC), decreased lung compliance, normal or increased FEV1/FVC (flow rates normal/high when corrected for absolute lung volume), decreased DLCO.

The FASST/RAAIDS mnemonic captures upper and lower lobe ILD causes: Upper (FASST): Fibrosis (extrinsic HP), Ankylosing spondylitis, Sarcoidosis, Silicosis, TB. Lower (RAAIDS): Rheumatoid arthritis, Asbestosis, Amiodarone, IPF, Drugs, Scleroderma. Hypoxemia occurs due to V/Q mismatch (without hypercapnia until end stage). Pulmonary HTN and cor pulmonale develop in advanced disease.

**Q:** What are the causes of upper vs. lower lobe predominant interstitial lung disease (ILD)?

**A:** Upper lobe predominant ILD (FASST): Fibrosis (extrinsic HP/farmer's lung), Ankylosing spondylitis, Sarcoidosis, Silicosis, TB. Lower lobe predominant ILD (RAAIDS): Rheumatoid arthritis, Asbestosis, Amiodarone, IPF, Drugs, Scleroderma.

Distribution on CXR/CT helps narrow the differential diagnosis. Asbestosis causes lower lobe predominant fibrosis with pleural plaques. Silicosis causes upper lobe nodules and 'eggshell' calcification of hilar nodes. Sarcoidosis typically has bilateral hilar adenopathy with upper lobe predominance.

**Q:** What are the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs)?

**A:** IPF (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis), NSIP (non-specific interstitial pneumonia), RB-ILD (respiratory bronchiolitis-related ILD), DIP (desquamative interstitial pneumonia), COP (cryptogenic organizing pneumonia), AIP (acute interstitial pneumonia), LIP (lymphocytic organizing pneumonia), IPPFE (idiopathic pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis), AFOP (acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia).

IPF is the most common and worst-prognosis IIP. It requires UIP (usual interstitial pneumonia) pattern on CT or biopsy. DIP and RB-ILD are smoking-related. COP was formerly called BOOP and responds well to corticosteroids. AIP (Hamman-Rich syndrome) has very high mortality. Classification guides prognosis and treatment.

**Q:** What investigations are performed for ILD?

**A:** CXR: decreased lung volumes, reticular/nodular/reticulonodular pattern, hilar/mediastinal adenopathy, honeycombing. HRCT: UIP, probable UIP, indeterminate for UIP, or alternative diagnosis patterns. PFTs: restrictive pattern, ↓DLCO. ABGs: hypoxemia ± respiratory acidosis late. Labs: ANA, RF, anti-CCP, ANCA, myositis antibodies, serum precipitins for HP. Bronchoscopy with BAL in select cases. Surgical lung biopsy or transbronchial cryobiopsy for indeterminate/alternative diagnosis CT patterns.

CXR can be normal in up to 10% of patients with ILD. Surgical biopsy is the gold standard when diagnosis is uncertain; transbronchial cryobiopsy is an emerging less invasive alternative. BAL showing lymphocytic predominance suggests HP or sarcoidosis; eosinophilic predominance suggests eosinophilic pneumonia.

**Q:** What is IPF, who is typically affected, and what are the key diagnostic findings?

**A:** IPF is pulmonary fibrosis of unknown cause with UIP histology. Progressive and irreversible. Commonly presents in males over age 50; smoking is a risk factor. Symptoms: dyspnea on exertion, nonproductive cough, late inspiratory fine crackles at lung bases, clubbing. CXR: reticular/reticulonodular pattern with lower lobe predominance; honeycombing in advanced disease. HRCT: UIP pattern (reticulation, subpleural and basal predominant, honeycombing ± traction bronchiectasis). Autoimmune serology usually negative.

IPF prevalence increases with age (35–44 years: 27/100,000; >75 years: 175/100,000). Biopsy is only performed if CT pattern is indeterminate for UIP or suggests alternative diagnosis to IPF. DDx: CTD-associated ILD, chronic HP, asbestosis, NSIP. Ground glass, consolidation, or nodules should NOT be prominent in IPF on CT.

**Q:** What are the four CT patterns for IPF interpretation and what does each indicate?

**A:** 1) UIP pattern: reticulation, subpleural and basal predominant, honeycombing + traction bronchiectasis — diagnostic of IPF without biopsy. 2) Probable UIP: reticulation, subpleural and basal predominant, traction bronchiectasis (no honeycombing). 3) Indeterminate for UIP: subtle reticulation, subpleural/basal predominant ('early UIP'), CT features not suggesting specific etiology. 4) Alternative diagnosis to IPF: cysts, mosaic attenuation, predominant ground-glass opacity, profuse micronodules, centrilobular nodules, consolidation, mid or upper lung zone predominance, peribronchovascular distribution.

This classification guides the need for surgical lung biopsy. If UIP pattern: no biopsy needed. If indeterminate or alternative diagnosis pattern: biopsy considered (transbronchial cryobiopsy emerging). Ground glass opacity as the predominant finding suggests alternative diagnoses such as HP, NSIP, or DIP.

**Q:** What is the treatment of IPF?

**A:** Antifibrotic therapy: pirfenidone or nintedanib (slow disease progression). Smoking cessation. Pulmonary rehabilitation. Supplemental O2. PPI if patient has GERD/reflux. Lung transplantation for advanced disease. Palliative care. Acute exacerbation: prednisone (minimal evidence but frequently used) ± antibiotics ± diuretics ± mechanical ventilation on individualized basis.

Pirfenidone reduces fibroblast proliferation and TGF-β-mediated fibrosis. Nintedanib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting PDGFR, FGFR, VEGFR. Both slow FVC decline by approximately 50%. Neither cures IPF. Lung transplant is bilateral in IPF. Median survival without treatment is 2–5 years from diagnosis.

**Q:** What is cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP) and how does it differ from other ILDs?

**A:** COP: organizing pneumonia without identifiable cause. OP: inflammatory process involving bronchioles and alveolar space, caused by CTD-associated ILD, medications, or other triggers. Key distinction: COP has no history of connective tissue disease. Symptoms: nonspecific cough, dyspnea, recurrent pneumonia, fever, fatigue, often acute flu-like illness onset. Symptoms present <2 months in most patients. May be misdiagnosed as CAP (lack of response to empiric antibiotics suggests OP).

CXR/CT: ground-glass opacities and patchy consolidation (bilateral, can be unilateral, migratory on serial X-rays). 'Reverse halo sign' (consolidation surrounding ground glass) is classic on CT. Treatment: immunosuppression with steroids for ~6 months; often recurrent requiring long taper. Drug-induced OP may improve with drug removal alone.

**Q:** What is the 'reverse halo sign' on CT and what condition is it associated with?

**A:** The reverse halo sign (also called 'atoll sign') consists of a central area of ground-glass opacity surrounded by a ring of consolidation on CT. It is a classic sign of cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP)/organizing pneumonia.

The more common 'halo sign' (ground glass surrounding a nodule) is associated with invasive aspergillosis and other angioinvasive infections. Distinguishing these two signs is important: reverse halo = organizing pneumonia context; halo sign = infection/angioinvasion context. CT can also show patchy consolidation that is bilateral and migratory on serial X-rays in COP.

**Q:** What is hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and what are common causative antigens?

**A:** HP (extrinsic allergic alveolitis): spectrum of immune-mediated lung disorders occurring in response to inhaled organic antigens or low molecular weight compounds (drugs/chemicals). Common examples: Farmer's lung (Thermophilic actinomycetes), Bird breeder's/fancier's lung (bird antigens), Humidifier lung (Aureobasidium pullulans), Sauna taker's lung (Vibrio spp.), Metalworking fluid lung.

Two-hit hypothesis: genetic susceptibility + antigen exposure. Acute/subacute HP: immune complex formation (Type III hypersensitivity). Chronic HP: Type IV hypersensitivity (T-cell mediated granulomatous inflammatory response). May have no identified antigen (likely home environment). Exposure often occupational or hobby-related.

**Q:** What are the presentations of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and how do PFTs vary?

**A:** Acute: 4–6 hours after exposure; dyspnea, cough, fever, chills, malaise lasting 18–24 hours. Subacute: more insidious onset than acute. Chronic: insidious onset over years; dyspnea, cough, malaise, anorexia, weight loss. PFTs: acute HP often obstructive, subacute is obstructive/mixed, chronic is progressively restrictive. Serum precipitins may be detectable in acute and chronic HP (neither sensitive nor specific).

Histopathology: acute HP triad = poorly formed granulomas + cellular bronchiolitis + interstitial lymphocytic infiltrate. BAL shows lymphocytic predominance. Chronic HP may be difficult to distinguish from IPF on biopsy (poorly formed granulomas + multinucleated giant cells ± features mimicking UIP). Early diagnosis and antigen avoidance are critical as chronic changes are irreversible.

**Q:** What is the treatment of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)?

**A:** Early diagnosis and antigen avoidance (critical — chronic changes are irreversible). Systemic corticosteroids: relieve symptoms and speed resolution; more effective in acute/subacute HP; uncertain benefit in chronic fibrosing HP. Steroid-sparing agents (mycophenolate, azathioprine): used in progressive disease despite steroids or to prevent steroid side effects.

CXR patterns: acute HP shows diffuse infiltrates predominantly in upper lobes; chronic shows predominantly upper lobe reticulonodular pattern. Contrast with fibrotic ILD caused by asbestosis (lower lobes) and silicosis (upper lobes). Upper lobe fibrosis in HP can mimic silicosis or sarcoidosis.

**Q:** What ILD conditions are caused by connective tissue diseases, and which connective tissue diseases are associated?

**A:** CTD-associated ILD: Scleroderma (most commonly NSIP pattern), Rheumatoid arthritis (UIP or NSIP), SLE, Polymyositis/dermatomyositis (NSIP or OP), Anti-synthetase syndromes, Mixed connective tissue disease, ANCA-associated vasculitis, Sjögren's syndrome. NSIP is the most common pattern in CTD-ILD, unlike IPF which shows UIP.

CTD-ILD is an important diagnostic consideration before labeling someone as IPF—workup includes ANA, RF, anti-CCP, ANCA, myositis antibodies. Anti-Jo-1 is the most common myositis antibody in anti-synthetase syndrome (ILD + myositis + arthritis + mechanic's hands + Raynaud's). Scleroderma has highest prevalence of ILD among CTDs.

**Q:** What are drug-induced causes of ILD and what are examples of each drug class?

**A:** Antibiotics: nitrofurantoin. Anti-inflammatory agents: methotrexate. Cardiovascular drugs: amiodarone. Antineoplastic agents: chemotherapy (bleomycin, cyclophosphamide). Recreational drugs: crack lung, talc granulomatosis. Radiation therapy. (Mnemonic RAAIDS: lower lobe ILD causes include amiodarone and drugs.)

Amiodarone causes a phospholipidosis pattern (foamy macrophages) and is associated with NSIP or OP patterns on CT. Bleomycin causes pulmonary toxicity that is dose-dependent. Methotrexate causes hypersensitivity pneumonitis. Drug-induced OP may improve with drug discontinuation alone. A careful medication history is essential in all ILD evaluations.

## Resp Sarcoidosis

**Q:** What is sarcoidosis and what are its key epidemiological features?

**A:** Idiopathic non-infectious granulomatous multi-system disease with lung involvement in 90%. Pathologically characterized by non-necrotizing (non-caseating) granulomas (occasionally necrosis is present). Typically affects young and middle-aged patients. Higher incidence among people of African descent (USA) and northern latitudes (Scandinavia, Canada). Familial sarcoidosis recognized; HLA markers play a role.

Sarcoidosis is the great imitator—it can affect virtually every organ. Lung is most commonly involved (90%), but also affects lymph nodes, skin (erythema nodosum, lupus pernio), eyes (uveitis), heart (arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy), liver, kidney, and nervous system. Non-necrotizing granulomas help distinguish from TB (necrotizing/caseating) and fungal infections.

**Q:** What does bilateral hilar adenopathy on CXR suggest in a young patient?

**A:** Bilateral hilar adenopathy in a young or middle-aged patient most commonly suggests sarcoidosis or lymphoma. Unilateral hilar adenopathy suggests neoplasia (primary or metastatic), TB, or sarcoid. Bilateral hilar adenopathy can also be seen in other conditions but sarcoid is the classic teaching association.

Hilar adenopathy refers to enlargement of mediastinal lymph nodes seen on CXR as spherical/ellipsoidal and/or calcified nodes. In sarcoidosis, bilateral hilar adenopathy (BHL) is classic and may be asymptomatic. Löfgren syndrome = BHL + erythema nodosum + arthritis (good prognosis). CXR staging: Stage 0 (normal), I (BHL alone), II (BHL + parenchymal), III (parenchymal alone), IV (fibrosis).

**Q:** What are the clinical signs and symptoms of ILD, and how does sarcoidosis differ from IPF in its presentation?

**A:** General ILD: dyspnea on exertion, nonproductive cough, dry fine end-inspiratory crackles, clubbing, cor pulmonale features. IPF: crackles and clubbing prominent. Sarcoidosis: seldom associated with crackles and clubbing. Often presents as asymptomatic CXR finding (bilateral hilar adenopathy).

The absence of crackles in sarcoidosis despite radiographic findings is a classic MCCQE1 discriminating point. Clubbing in ILD suggests IPF or asbestosis. Sarcoidosis may also present with Löfgren's syndrome (bilateral hilar adenopathy + erythema nodosum + polyarthritis) which has a good prognosis. Uveoparotid fever (Heerfordt syndrome) is another sarcoid presentation.
