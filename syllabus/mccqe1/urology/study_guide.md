# Urology

## UTI & Urinary Tract Disorders

**Q:** What is the definition of asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) and in which populations should it be treated?

**A:** ≥10⁵ CFU/mL in one (women) or two (men) clean-catch midstream specimens without UTI symptoms; treat only in pregnant women and before urological procedures

IDSA/AMMI guidelines: routine treatment of ASB in elderly, diabetics, catheterized patients, or before non-urological surgery is not recommended and increases antibiotic resistance. Screen and treat in pregnancy (risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth).

**Q:** A 25-year-old non-pregnant woman presents with 2 days of dysuria, frequency, and urgency without fever or flank pain. Urinalysis shows pyuria and bacteriuria. What is first-line treatment?

**A:** Nitrofurantoin macrocrystals 100 mg BID × 5 days, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole 160/800 mg BID × 3 days (if local resistance <20%), or fosfomycin 3 g single dose

Uncomplicated UTI in premenopausal non-pregnant women. Fluoroquinolones should be reserved for more serious infections to preserve effectiveness. Check local antibiogram for TMP-SMX resistance rates.

**Q:** What clinical and laboratory features distinguish uncomplicated cystitis from pyelonephritis?

**A:** Pyelonephritis: fever >38°C, rigors, costovertebral angle tenderness/pain, nausea/vomiting, elevated WBC and CRP — cystitis lacks these systemic features

Distinction matters because pyelonephritis requires longer antibiotic course (7–14 days) and may require IV antibiotics if severe. Blood cultures if systemically ill. Imaging (CT/US) if no improvement at 48–72 hours.

**Q:** What is the antibiotic of choice for acute uncomplicated pyelonephritis in an outpatient?

**A:** Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID × 7 days (if susceptible) or TMP-SMX 160/800 mg BID × 14 days

Fluoroquinolones are first-line for pyelonephritis because they achieve high urinary and tissue concentrations. Beta-lactams are less effective for pyelonephritis. If local fluoroquinolone resistance >10%, obtain culture and use based on susceptibility.

**Q:** What is the most common organism causing UTI overall?

**A:** Escherichia coli (accounts for 75–90% of community-acquired uncomplicated UTIs)

E. coli produces fimbriae (type 1 and P) that allow adhesion to uroepithelium. Other common organisms: Klebsiella pneumoniae, S. saprophyticus (young women), Proteus mirabilis (struvite stones), Enterococcus faecalis.

**Q:** A 75-year-old man with a Foley catheter develops fever, rigors, and pyuria. Urine culture grows >10⁵ CFU/mL of P. aeruginosa. What is the management?

**A:** Remove or replace catheter, treat with anti-pseudomonal antibiotic (piperacillin-tazobactam or cefepime or ciprofloxacin based on sensitivity) for 7 days

Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI): catheter removal is the most important intervention. Use smallest bore catheter for shortest time. Pseudomonas is an intrinsically resistant organism — susceptibility testing is essential. Prevention: bladder bundles, closed drainage systems.

**Q:** What is recurrent UTI and what prophylactic strategies are available for women?

**A:** ≥2 UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months; strategies: post-coital antibiotic prophylaxis, continuous low-dose prophylaxis (nitrofurantoin or TMP), patient-initiated therapy, cranberry products (modest evidence)

Recurrent UTIs affect 25% of women after first episode. Topical estrogen for postmenopausal women reduces recurrences by restoring vaginal lactobacillus colonization. Cranberry (proanthocyanidins) may prevent E. coli adhesion.

**Q:** What is the appropriate investigation for a man with his first UTI?

**A:** Urine culture, urinalysis; consider urology referral and renal/bladder ultrasound to exclude structural abnormality or obstruction

UTIs in men are considered complicated. Causes include prostatitis, BPH, urethral stricture, or calculi. Blood cultures if febrile. Treat for 7–14 days (vs. 3 days for women). Ciprofloxacin or TMP-SMX depending on culture.

**Q:** What is the CAUTI prevention bundle per Safer Healthcare Now!/IPAC Canada?

**A:** Appropriate catheter indication, insertion using aseptic technique, closed drainage system maintenance, daily assessment for removal (ABCDE bundle), and nurse-driven protocol for early catheter removal

Catheter-associated UTIs are the most common healthcare-associated infections. Every day with an unnecessary catheter increases CAUTI risk by 3–7%. Regular reassessment of catheter necessity is the most impactful intervention.

**Q:** What is urethritis and which organisms cause it in sexually active young adults?

**A:** Inflammation of the urethra caused by STIs; most common: Chlamydia trachomatis (non-gonococcal urethritis) and Neisseria gonorrhoeae

Symptoms: dysuria, urethral discharge, meatal erythema. NAAT (urine or urethral swab) for gonorrhea and chlamydia. Treat empirically for both: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM (gonorrhea) + azithromycin 1 g PO or doxycycline 100 mg BID × 7 days (chlamydia). Notify and treat partners.

**Q:** How do you distinguish uncomplicated from complicated UTI?

**A:** Uncomplicated: non-pregnant woman, no structural abnormalities, no catheter. Complicated: male, pregnant, structural abnormality, catheter, immunocompromised, upper tract involvement

Uncomplicated cystitis: nitrofurantoin 100mg BID 5d or TMP-SMX 3d. Complicated: fluoroquinolone or broader spectrum, longer course (7-14d). Pyelonephritis: fluoroquinolone or ceftriaxone.

**Q:** How is uncomplicated vs complicated UTI defined and what empiric antibiotics are used?

**A:** Uncomplicated: cystitis in non-pregnant premenopausal woman. Rx: nitrofurantoin 100mg BID x5d (first-line) or TMP-SMX x3d. Complicated: pregnancy, male, catheter, structural abnormality, immunosuppression, upper tract - broader coverage needed

AMMI Canada/Bugs & Drugs guidelines. Avoid fluoroquinolones for uncomplicated UTI (collateral damage, resistance). Complicated: requires culture, imaging if recurrent. Pyelonephritis: TMP-SMX or ciprofloxacin PO x7d if mild, IV ceftriaxone if requires admission.

**Q:** What is the management of acute pyelonephritis?

**A:** Outpatient (mild, can tolerate PO): ciprofloxacin x7d or TMP-SMX x14d. Inpatient: IV ceftriaxone or fluoroquinolone. Admit if: sepsis, unable to tolerate PO, obstruction, pregnancy, immunosuppressed

Urine culture + sensitivities essential. Blood cultures if septic. CT with contrast (or US) if no improvement at 48-72h (rule out abscess, obstruction). Pregnant: admit for IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone). Urologic obstruction with infection = EMERGENCY drainage.

**Q:** When should asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) be treated?

**A:** Treat ONLY in: pregnancy (risk pyelo/preterm labor) and before urologic procedures. Do NOT treat: elderly, diabetics, catheterized, spinal cord injury, nursing home residents

Screening for ASB recommended only in pregnancy (first trimester). Treatment of ASB in other populations increases resistance and C. difficile risk without benefit. A common source of inappropriate antibiotic use in Canada.

**Q:** What is the approach to recurrent UTIs in women?

**A:** >=3 UTIs/year or >=2 in 6 months. Behavioral: post-coital voiding, hydration, cranberry (weak evidence). Prophylaxis options: post-coital antibiotic (nitrofurantoin 100mg or TMP-SMX), continuous low-dose prophylaxis, self-start therapy

Urine culture to confirm each episode. Rule out structural abnormality (US, CT). Consider: vaginal estrogen for postmenopausal women (restores lactobacilli). Imaging/cystoscopy if atypical features. D-mannose has some evidence for prevention.

**Q:** How is UTI managed in pregnancy?

**A:** ASB: screen in first trimester, treat with cephalexin, amoxicillin, or nitrofurantoin (avoid at term). Cystitis: same as ASB treatment x7d. Pyelonephritis: admit for IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone)

Avoid: TMP-SMX (first trimester: folate antagonist; third: kernicterus), fluoroquinolones (cartilage), nitrofurantoin near term (hemolytic anemia). Monthly urine cultures after treatment. Untreated ASB in pregnancy: 20-40% develop pyelo.

**Q:** How is catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) managed?

**A:** Diagnosis requires symptoms (NOT just positive culture). Remove or replace catheter. Treat x7d (10-14d if delayed response). Do NOT treat asymptomatic catheterized patients even with positive culture

CAUTI prevention: avoid unnecessary catheters, remove early, closed drainage, daily reassessment of need. Nurse-driven removal protocols reduce CAUTI rates. Candida in urine with catheter: usually colonization, only treat if symptomatic or high-risk (neutropenic, renal transplant).

**Q:** How is UTI diagnosed in elderly patients?

**A:** Symptoms required: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, new incontinence, gross hematuria. Do NOT rely on pyuria alone (common asymptomatic finding). Confusion alone is NOT an indication to treat UTI

Over-diagnosis of UTI in elderly is extremely common and harmful. Positive UA/culture without urinary symptoms should not be treated. Delirium workup should include UTI in differential but also other causes. Urine dip sensitivity/specificity poor in elderly.

**Q:** What is the management of acute prostatitis?

**A:** Presentation: fever, perinatal pain, dysuria, urinary retention, boggy tender prostate on DRE. Treatment: fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin) x4-6wk or TMP-SMX x4-6wk. If septic: IV ceftriaxone + aminoglycoside

Prostatic abscess: suspect if no improvement at 48h, CT confirms, may need drainage. Chronic bacterial prostatitis: recurrent UTI with same organism, treat x4-6wk. Chronic prostatitis/CPPS (most common): no infection, multimodal therapy (alpha-blocker, physio, CBT).

**Q:** What is the approach to urinary retention?

**A:** Acute: catheterize (Foley or intermittent). Measure PVR. If >1L: monitor for post-obstructive diuresis. Causes: BPH (most common in men), medications (anticholinergics, opioids), neurogenic (cauda equina - emergency), stricture

Cauda equina syndrome: saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness, urinary retention - MRI STAT and surgical decompression. Medications causing retention: anticholinergics, opioids, alpha-agonists (pseudoephedrine), antihistamines. Post-catheterization trial of void with alpha-blocker.

**Q:** What is the approach to hematuria?

**A:** Microscopic (>3 RBC/HPF) or gross. Workup: UA+culture, cytology, CT urogram + cystoscopy for patients >35 or risk factors (smoking, occupational). Transient causes: UTI, exercise, menstruation - repeat UA before full workup

Red flags: gross, painless, >35yo, smoking history. Bladder cancer is most important diagnosis to rule out. Glomerular hematuria (dysmorphic RBCs, casts, proteinuria): nephrology referral. IgA nephropathy most common glomerulonephritis worldwide.

**Q:** What is testicular torsion and how is it managed?

**A:** Sudden severe testicular pain, high-riding testis, absent cremasteric reflex, abnormal lie. <6h: surgical exploration (90% salvage). >24h: <10% salvage. Manual detorsion: medial to lateral ('open the book')

Peak: neonatal and adolescent. Bell clapper deformity predisposes. Doppler US if diagnosis uncertain but do NOT delay surgery for imaging if clinical suspicion high. Always fix contralateral testis (bilateral bell clapper).

**Q:** A 14-year-old boy presents with sudden-onset severe left testicular pain and nausea. The affected testis is high-riding with an absent cremasteric reflex. What is the diagnosis and management?

**A:** Testicular torsion — a surgical EMERGENCY. Must detorse within 6 hours for >90% salvage rate (drops to <10% after 24h). Clinical: sudden severe unilateral scrotal pain, nausea/vomiting, high-riding testis with horizontal lie, absent cremasteric reflex (most sensitive sign), negative Prehn sign (elevation does not relieve pain). Diagnosis: clinical — do NOT delay for imaging if high suspicion; Doppler ultrasound only if diagnosis uncertain. Management: 1) Attempt manual detorsion at bedside ('open the book' — medial to lateral rotation); 2) URGENT surgical exploration and bilateral orchidopexy (fix both testes) regardless of manual detorsion success; 3) If delayed >6h: intraoperative assessment of viability — orchiectomy if non-viable.

Bell clapper deformity (high attachment of tunica vaginalis allowing free rotation) is the predisposing anatomical variant — it is bilateral, which is why BOTH testes are fixed during surgery. The cremasteric reflex has ~99% negative predictive value — if present, torsion is very unlikely. Canadian surgical guidelines mandate that torsion should be treated as 'door-to-OR' within 1-2 hours.

**Q:** What are the types of kidney stones, their presentation, diagnosis, and management?

**A:** Types: Calcium oxalate (80% — most common), calcium phosphate, uric acid (radiolucent), struvite (infection/'staghorn'), cystine (hereditary). Presentation: acute colicky flank pain radiating to groin, hematuria (90%), nausea/vomiting, restlessness. Diagnosis: CT KUB without contrast (gold standard — 98% sensitivity); ultrasound in pregnancy/children. Management: <5mm: medical expulsive therapy (tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily — relaxes ureter, increases passage rate by 30%), NSAIDs for pain (ketorolac), hydration. 5-10mm: MET with close follow-up, intervention if not passing. >10mm or complicated (obstruction with infection, AKI, intractable pain, solitary kidney): urgent urology — ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy) for <2cm renal/upper ureteral stones; ureteroscopy for ureteral stones; PCNL for large stones >2cm.

CUA guidelines recommend CT KUB as first-line imaging. Tamsulosin for MET has strongest evidence for distal ureteral stones 5-10mm. Obstructing stone with fever/UTI is a urologic emergency requiring urgent decompression (ureteral stent or nephrostomy) — antibiotics alone are insufficient. Prevention: increase fluid intake to >2.5L/day urine output, dietary modifications based on stone type.

**Q:** What is the investigation of choice for suspected renal colic (nephrolithiasis)?

**A:** Non-contrast CT of abdomen/pelvis (CT KUB) — best sensitivity and specificity. Abdominal ultrasound is preferred in females of childbearing age, children, or if CT is contraindicated.

Non-contrast CT can detect stones of any composition, size, and location, and identify hydronephrosis and alternative diagnoses. AXR can identify large radiopaque stones (calcium, struvite, cystine) but misses uric acid stones and small stones. Uric acid stones are radiolucent.

**Q:** What percentage of renal stones pass spontaneously, and what size determines this?

**A:** 75% of stones <4 mm pass spontaneously within 2 weeks. Larger stones (>5 mm) are less likely to pass and may require urological intervention.

Stone size and location determine spontaneous passage rates. Distal ureteral stones pass more readily than proximal ones. Alpha-blockers (e.g., tamsulosin) relax ureteral smooth muscle and can increase stone passage rates in select cases with stones 5-10 mm.

**Q:** What are the indications for urgent urology consult in nephrolithiasis?

**A:** Stone >5 mm, bilateral obstructing stones, obstruction + infection (urological emergency), fever (septic stone), renal impairment, solitary kidney, intractable vomiting, single kidney with ureteral obstruction.

Obstruction + infection is the most urgent indication—it represents obstructive pyelonephritis requiring immediate decompression (ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) plus IV antibiotics and fluids. This cannot be managed with antibiotics alone.

**Q:** What is the preferred analgesic for renal colic and why?

**A:** NSAIDs (e.g., ketorolac/Toradol) are preferred over opioids. NSAIDs reduce ureteral prostaglandin-mediated spasm, treat both pain and ureteral obstruction. Opioids provide pain relief but do not address ureteral spasm.

Prostaglandins cause ureteral smooth muscle contraction and promote inflammation around the stone. NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis, reducing ureteral spasm and edema. Ketorolac IV is highly effective. Opioids are used as adjuncts or when NSAIDs are contraindicated (renal impairment).

**Q:** What composition are the majority of kidney stones?

**A:** Calcium oxalate: ~75-80%. Also: struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate), uric acid (~10%), cystine, calcium phosphate.

Calcium oxalate stones are most common and are radiopaque on AXR. Struvite (infection/triple phosphate) stones form staghorn calculi associated with urease-producing bacteria (Proteus, Klebsiella). Uric acid stones are radiolucent and occur in gout/hyperuricemia. Cystine stones are rare (cystinuria).

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for uncomplicated UTI in women, per Canadian guidelines?

**A:** TMP/SMX (cotrimoxazole) 160/800 mg BID × 3 days OR nitrofurantoin 100 mg BID × 5 days.

Short-course therapy with TMP/SMX or nitrofurantoin is effective for uncomplicated cystitis in non-pregnant women. Nitrofurantoin should be avoided in the third trimester of pregnancy. Culture is not required if dipstick is positive for nitrites + leukocytes with ≥2 clinical criteria.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for a complicated UTI?

**A:** Male sex; pregnancy; recent urinary tract instrumentation; functional or anatomic abnormality of the urinary tract; chronic renal disease; immunosuppression; indwelling catheter.

Complicated UTIs require longer courses (7-14 days) of broader-spectrum antibiotics such as TMP/SMX or ciprofloxacin. Complicated UTIs are at higher risk of treatment failure and systemic spread.

**Q:** How should bacteriuria in pregnancy be managed?

**A:** Pregnant women with bacteriuria (2-7%) MUST be treated even if asymptomatic. First-line: amoxicillin, nitrofurantoin (avoid in third trimester), cephalexin. Follow with monthly urine cultures and repeat treatment if still infected. Risk of untreated: pyelonephritis, preterm labour, low birth weight, perinatal mortality.

Asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy is an important exception to the general rule of not treating asymptomatic bacteriuria. Untreated ASB in pregnancy has significant maternal and fetal consequences.

**Q:** What are the common causative organisms for UTI/cystitis (KEEPS mnemonic)?

**A:** KEEPS: Klebsiella, E. coli, Enterobacter spp., P. mirabilis, Pseudomonas spp., S. saprophyticus.

E. coli is by far the most common cause of uncomplicated UTI (80%). S. saprophyticus is classically seen in sexually active young women. KEEPS helps recall the spectrum of common uropathogens.

**Q:** What are the signs and symptoms of pyelonephritis compared to cystitis?

**A:** Cystitis: internal dysuria, frequency, urgency, incontinence, hematuria, nocturia, back pain, suprapubic discomfort, low-grade fever (rare). Pyelonephritis: internal dysuria, FEVER, chills, flank pain radiating to groin, CVA tenderness, nausea/vomiting.

Pyelonephritis indicates upper urinary tract infection and requires more aggressive treatment. CVA (costovertebral angle) tenderness on percussion is a key physical exam finding. CBC and differential should be ordered if pyelonephritis is suspected.

**Q:** How does the UTI Clinical Decision Aid work?

**A:** If the patient has: (1) Dysuria + (2) Leukocytes on dipstick + (3) Nitrites on dipstick — if ≥2 criteria are met, treat empirically without culture. If <2 criteria met, culture is required before treatment.

This clinical decision aid avoids unnecessary cultures and reduces antibiotic overuse. It applies to uncomplicated UTI in non-pregnant women without risk factors for complicated UTI.

## Prostate Disease & Urologic Cancer

**Q:** What is benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and what are the typical lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)?

**A:** Non-malignant enlargement of the prostate causing bladder outlet obstruction; LUTS: urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia (storage symptoms), weak stream, hesitancy, straining, incomplete emptying (voiding symptoms)

BPH affects >50% of men >60 years. IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) quantifies severity. DRE: smooth, rubbery enlargement. First-line treatment: lifestyle modifications, then alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) or 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride).

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of alpha-1 blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin) in treating BPH?

**A:** Relax smooth muscle in prostate and bladder neck by blocking alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, reducing outlet resistance

Alpha-1 blockers provide faster symptom relief (1–2 weeks) than 5-ARIs. Side effects: orthostatic hypotension, retrograde ejaculation, rhinitis. Tamsulosin is more uroselective with fewer systemic effects than terazosin/doxazosin.

**Q:** A 70-year-old man with BPH cannot void and has a palpable bladder. What is the acute management?

**A:** Urinary catheterization (Foley catheter) to relieve acute urinary retention

Acute urinary retention is a urological emergency. Post-void residual on ultrasound or catheterization >300 mL confirms significant retention. Start alpha-blocker before catheter removal (trial without catheter in 24–72 h). Urology referral for TURP if recurrent or fails trial.

**Q:** What PSA level and DRE finding warrant urology referral for possible prostate cancer in a 55-year-old man?

**A:** PSA >3–4 ng/mL or abnormal DRE (firm, irregular, asymmetric nodule or induration) regardless of PSA

Canadian Cancer Society: offer PSA screening discussion to men 50–74 (or 40+ with risk factors: African ancestry, family history). Abnormal DRE + any PSA, or PSA >3–4 → transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy or MRI-targeted biopsy.

**Q:** What is the gleason grading system for prostate cancer?

**A:** Histological grading based on differentiation; Gleason grade 1–5 (most to least differentiated); combined Gleason score (primary + secondary pattern) 6–10; Grade group 1–5 maps to Gleason scores 6, 3+4=7, 4+3=7, 8, 9–10

Gleason ≤6 (Grade group 1) = low-risk, favorable prognosis. Gleason ≥8 (Grade group 4–5) = high-risk, aggressive. The Grade Group system (1–5) is now preferred for clarity. Used in risk stratification and treatment decisions.

**Q:** What is active surveillance in prostate cancer and when is it appropriate?

**A:** Regular monitoring (PSA every 3–6 months, DRE, repeat biopsy ±MRI) without immediate treatment; appropriate for low-risk, localized prostate cancer (Gleason 6, PSA <10, T1c-T2a, <3 cores positive)

Active surveillance avoids overtreatment of indolent cancers. Approximately 30% of patients on AS will eventually require treatment (progression). It is endorsed by the Canadian Urological Association (CUA) for Grade Group 1 disease.

**Q:** What are the most common side effects of radical prostatectomy vs. external beam radiotherapy for prostate cancer?

**A:** Prostatectomy: erectile dysfunction (~60–80%) and urinary incontinence (5–15%); radiotherapy: rectal toxicity (bleeding, urgency), cystitis, and later development of ED

Both are equivalent in cancer control for localized disease. Choice depends on patient preference, anatomy, comorbidities, and urinary/sexual function baseline. Brachytherapy and HIFU are alternatives for appropriate candidates.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC)?

**A:** Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) + docetaxel or abiraterone acetate (intensification therapy)

ADT alone (surgical or medical castration with LHRH agonist/antagonist) is no longer standard for mCSPC. Intensification with docetaxel (CHAARTED trial) or abiraterone (LATITUDE trial) significantly improves overall survival.

**Q:** A 65-year-old man on long-term ADT for prostate cancer develops osteoporosis. What should be prescribed?

**A:** Calcium + vitamin D + bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid annually) or denosumab

ADT causes castrate testosterone levels → bone loss and increased fracture risk. DEXA scan should be performed at baseline and every 1–2 years. Zoledronic acid or denosumab reduce fracture risk. Prevent falls (exercise, medication review).

**Q:** What is the purpose of the IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) in BPH management?

**A:** Quantifies symptom severity: 0–7 mild, 8–19 moderate, 20–35 severe; guides treatment decisions

IPSS includes 7 voiding/storage symptom questions plus quality-of-life item. Mild symptoms (≤7): watchful waiting. Moderate (8–19): medical therapy. Severe (≥20) or failed medical therapy: surgical intervention (TURP, laser prostatectomy).

**Q:** What is PSA screening approach in Canada?

**A:** CTFPHC recommends AGAINST routine PSA screening. If requested: shared decision-making discussing benefits (small mortality reduction) vs harms (overdiagnosis, overtreatment, biopsy complications)

CUA more supportive of informed discussion in men 55-69. PSA >4: repeat, free/total ratio, consider MRI before biopsy. High-grade PCa management: radical prostatectomy or radiation +/- ADT.

**Q:** What is the IPSS score and how does it guide BPH management?

**A:** International Prostate Symptom Score: 7 questions on urinary symptoms + 1 QoL. Mild 0-7: watchful waiting. Moderate 8-19: medical therapy. Severe 20-35: consider surgery

BPH evaluation: DRE, PSA (baseline), urinalysis, creatinine, IPSS. Uroflowmetry and PVR for moderate-severe. BPH does NOT increase prostate cancer risk. Prostate size does not correlate well with symptom severity.

**Q:** What are the medical treatments for BPH?

**A:** Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin): rapid symptom relief. 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride): reduce prostate size over 6mo, for large prostates. Combination if large prostate. Tadalafil 5mg daily also effective

Alpha-blockers SE: orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, retrograde ejaculation, intraoperative floppy iris syndrome. 5-ARI SE: decreased libido, ED, gynecomastia, reduces PSA by ~50% (double PSA value for cancer screening). 5-ARI reduces risk of acute retention and surgery.

**Q:** When is surgical intervention indicated for BPH?

**A:** Indications: refractory urinary retention, recurrent UTIs, bladder stones, renal insufficiency from obstruction, failed medical therapy, recurrent gross hematuria. TURP is gold standard. Alternatives: HoLEP, UroLift, Rezum

TURP complications: retrograde ejaculation (75%), TUR syndrome (hyponatremia from irrigation), bleeding, urethral stricture. HoLEP: size-independent, less bleeding. Minimally invasive (UroLift, Rezum): preserve ejaculation, for moderate symptoms.

**Q:** What is the approach to PSA screening and an elevated PSA?

**A:** CTFPHC: against routine screening. CUA: shared decision-making 50-70 (45 if high-risk). Elevated PSA: repeat in 4-6wk. If persistently elevated: consider MRI then targeted biopsy. PSA >100 suggests metastatic disease

PSA elevations: BPH, prostatitis, recent ejaculation, cycling, UTI, cancer. PSA velocity >0.75ng/mL/yr concerning. Free PSA%: <10% more likely cancer, >25% more likely benign. mpMRI with PI-RADS scoring reduces unnecessary biopsies.

**Q:** How is localized prostate cancer managed?

**A:** Low-risk (Gleason 6, PSA<10, T1-T2a): active surveillance preferred (PSA q3-6mo, DRE q12mo, repeat biopsy). Intermediate/high-risk: radical prostatectomy or radiation + ADT. Metastatic: ADT + docetaxel or novel hormonal agents

Active surveillance avoids overtreatment. Gleason score: 6=low, 7=intermediate (3+4 better than 4+3), 8-10=high. Side effects of treatment: erectile dysfunction, incontinence (surgery), bowel/bladder toxicity (radiation). 10-year cancer-specific survival >95% for localized.

**Q:** What is the presentation and management of acute urinary retention in BPH?

**A:** Presentation: inability to void, suprapubic pain, distended bladder. Management: Foley catheter, start tamsulosin, trial of void in 3-5 days. If fails: long-term catheter or surgery referral

Precipitants: medications (anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, opioids), alcohol, cold, constipation. Measure residual volume. If >1L: slow decompression (controversial) and monitor for post-obstructive diuresis (replace fluids if significant). TWOC success ~60% with alpha-blocker.

**Q:** What is the approach to an incidental prostate nodule on DRE?

**A:** Firm, irregular, asymmetric nodule on DRE requires investigation regardless of PSA. Order PSA. Refer urology for biopsy (TRUS-guided or MRI-targeted). 50% of palpable nodules are cancer

DRE detects primarily peripheral zone tumors (70% of cancers arise here). Normal DRE does not exclude cancer. DRE + PSA together are more informative than either alone. BPH causes smooth symmetric enlargement.

**Q:** What is androgen deprivation therapy and its side effects?

**A:** ADT: GnRH agonists (leuprolide) or antagonists (degarelix), or surgical orchidectomy. For metastatic or locally advanced prostate cancer. Side effects: hot flashes, osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome, CV risk, sarcopenia, cognitive decline

Initial GnRH agonist causes testosterone flare (can worsen bone pain/cord compression) - give with antiandrogen flare protection x2wk. GnRH antagonists avoid flare. Monitor: DXA, metabolic panel, CV risk. Exercise program essential. Intermittent ADT may reduce side effects.

**Q:** How is erectile dysfunction evaluated and managed?

**A:** Evaluation: history (psychogenic vs organic), medications review, CV risk assessment, testosterone level, glucose/lipids. Management: lifestyle (exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation), PDE5 inhibitor first-line (sildenafil, tadalafil). Second: vacuum device, ICI, penile prosthesis

ED is a marker of CV disease (shares risk factors and endothelial dysfunction). Assess CV fitness for sexual activity. PDE5i contraindicated with nitrates (severe hypotension). Testosterone replacement if confirmed hypogonadism. Psychogenic: CBT + PDE5i.

**Q:** What is the approach to a scrotal mass?

**A:** Painless: testicular cancer until proven otherwise (US + tumor markers AFP/bHCG/LDH). Transilluminates: hydrocele. Does not: solid mass (cancer concern). Painful: torsion (emergency, <6h), epididymitis. Testicular cancer: radical inguinal orchiectomy (never transscrotal)

Testicular torsion: no cremasteric reflex, high-riding testis, <6h for salvage. US if diagnosis unclear but do not delay surgery. Testicular cancer: peak 15-35yr. Seminoma (radiosensitive) vs non-seminoma (chemo). Staging CT + markers guide treatment. 95% cure rate overall.

**Q:** How is BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) evaluated and managed?

**A:** Evaluation: IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score): mild 0-7, moderate 8-19, severe 20-35; DRE (smooth, firm, symmetrically enlarged prostate); PSA (to rule out cancer — discuss with patient); urinalysis, creatinine, post-void residual (PVR). Management by severity: Mild (IPSS <8): watchful waiting, lifestyle (limit fluids before bed, avoid caffeine/alcohol, timed voiding). Moderate-severe: Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin — rapid symptom relief in days-weeks; SE: orthostatic hypotension, retrograde ejaculation). 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride — reduce prostate size over 6-12 months; for large prostates >30-40g; SE: decreased libido, ED, gynecomastia; reduce PSA by ~50%). Combination: alpha-blocker + 5ARI for large prostates. Surgery: TURP (gold standard) for refractory symptoms, urinary retention, recurrent UTI, bladder stones, renal insufficiency.

CUA (Canadian Urological Association) guidelines recommend alpha-blockers as first-line pharmacotherapy. 5ARIs take 6-12 months to show effect but reduce progression to urinary retention and need for surgery. Tamsulosin is more uroselective than older alpha-blockers (less orthostatic hypotension). TURP carries risks of retrograde ejaculation (~75%), TUR syndrome (hyponatremia from irrigation fluid absorption), and bleeding.

**Q:** How is erectile dysfunction evaluated and managed, including cardiovascular risk assessment?

**A:** Evaluation: history (onset, duration, situational vs global, libido, relationship, medications, psychosocial); risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, obesity); medication review (beta-blockers, SSRIs, spironolactone, 5ARIs); labs: fasting glucose/A1C, lipid panel, testosterone (morning), TSH, prolactin if low T. Cardiovascular risk: ED is an independent predictor of CVD events — ED often precedes MI by 3-5 years (shared endothelial dysfunction). Stratify cardiac risk before prescribing PDE5 inhibitors. Treatment: 1st line: PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil); CI: nitrates (absolute — fatal hypotension), alpha-blockers (relative — use with caution). 2nd line: intracavernosal injections (alprostadil), vacuum erection devices. 3rd line: penile prosthesis. Address modifiable risk factors (exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation, optimize diabetes/HTN).

CUA guidelines recommend treating ED as a cardiovascular risk marker — all men with new ED should have CV risk assessment. PDE5 inhibitors require sexual stimulation to work and should be tried on 6-8 occasions before deeming them ineffective. Tadalafil has the longest duration (36h) and is available as daily dosing (5 mg). Low testosterone should be confirmed with two morning levels before initiating testosterone replacement.

**Q:** What is the epidemiology of erectile dysfunction (ED)?

**A:** Approximately 20% of men aged 40 and 50% of men aged 70 are affected. F:M ratio not applicable — affects males.

ED prevalence increases dramatically with age, largely due to increasing vascular disease, comorbidities, and medication use. It is an important marker of cardiovascular disease, as endothelial dysfunction is a shared mechanism.

**Q:** How do you differentiate organic vs. psychogenic erectile dysfunction?

**A:** Organic: gradual onset, global (all circumstances), constant course, poor non-coital erections, absent morning erections, secondary psychosexual problems. Psychogenic: acute onset, situational, variable course, rigid non-coital erections, present morning erections, primary anxiety.

The presence or absence of nocturnal/morning erections is the most useful clinical differentiator. Preserved nocturnal erections suggest intact neurovascular function and a psychogenic etiology.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of organic erectile dysfunction and what are the pharmacologic contraindications?

**A:** Most common cause: vascular (90%) — arterial insufficiency, atherosclerosis. First-line pharmacologic treatment: PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil). Absolute contraindication: concurrent use of nitrates (risk of fatal hypotension).

PDE5 inhibitors cause smooth muscle relaxation in penile vasculature via cGMP accumulation. Nitrates also act through cGMP pathways — combined use causes profound, potentially fatal hypotension.

**Q:** What are the three PDE5 inhibitors used for erectile dysfunction, and how do they differ?

**A:** Sildenafil (Viagra): 25-100 mg, take 0.5-4h before, lasts ~24h. Tadalafil (Cialis): 5-20 mg, effects last 36h. Vardenafil (Levitra): 2.5-20 mg, take 1h before. All share side effects: flushing, headache, indigestion. All contraindicated with nitrates.

Tadalafil's longer duration of action (36h) allows for more spontaneous sexual activity ('weekend pill'). All three are equally effective. Choice depends on patient preference, frequency of sexual activity, and cost.

**Q:** What testosterone-related investigations are indicated in erectile dysfunction workup?

**A:** Hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis: free and total testosterone, prolactin, LH. Risk factor evaluation: fasting glucose, HbA1C, lipid profile. Others: TSH, CBC, urinalysis. Specialized: nocturnal penile tumescence and rigidity (NPTR) assessment, vascular diagnostics (Doppler, angiography), psychological/psychiatric consultation.

Testosterone deficiency with hypogonadism is an indication for testosterone replacement in ED. Note: breast and prostate cancer are absolute contraindications to testosterone therapy.

**Q:** What are the non-pharmacologic management options for erectile dysfunction?

**A:** Lifestyle changes (reduce alcohol, smoking cessation, exercise); relationship/sexual counselling; vacuum erection devices; referral to urology for: significant penile anatomic disease, young patient with pelvic/perineal trauma history, need for vascular/neurosurgical intervention, complicated endocrinopathies, complicated psychiatric/psychosocial problems.

Lifestyle modification and CV risk factor reduction are both associated with significant improvements in IIEF-5 (erectile function) scores. Treating underlying vascular risk factors can improve ED without pharmacotherapy.

## General

**Q:** What is the management of major bladder rupture?

**A:** Surgical repair

Major extraperitoneal or intraperitoneal ruptures require operative intervention.

**Q:** What is the management of posterior urethral injury?

**A:** Suprapubic cystostomy (avoid catheterization) + surgical repair

Catheterization is contraindicated in posterior urethral injuries.

**Q:** What is ureteroureterostomy?

**A:** Surgical connection of one ureter to another (ureter to ureter anastomosis)

A surgical option for ureteral injury repair.

**Q:** What is the management of intraperitoneal bladder rupture?

**A:** Surgical repair

Intraperitoneal bladder ruptures require urgent surgical exploration and repair.

**Q:** What is the recommended PSA screening approach for men ages 55-69 according to 2014 Canadian Task Force guidelines?

**A:** Recommend NOT screening for prostate cancer with PSA test (weak recommendation); clinicians should discuss risks/benefits with each patient

For ages 55-69, PSA screening is not routinely recommended; clinicians should discuss the potential small benefit against significant harms (bleeding, infection, incontinence, false positives, overdiagnosis).

**Q:** What are the layers of the scrotum from superficial to deep?

**A:** Skin, dartos muscle and fascia, external spermatic fascia, cremasteric fascia, internal spermatic fascia, tunica vaginalis

The scrotum has 7 layers that must be understood for orchiectomy and hernia repairs. The tunica vaginalis is the deepest layer covering the testicle.

**Q:** What are risk factors for penile cancer?

**A:** Chronic inflammatory disease, STI, phimosis, uncircumcised penis

Phimosis and poor hygiene lead to chronic inflammation and increased cancer risk. HPV also plays a role in many cases.

**Q:** What is the lymphatic spread pattern of penile cancer?

**A:** Superficial/deep inguinal nodes -> iliac nodes. Hematogenous spread is rare.

Penile cancer spreads lymphatically first. Inguinal lymphadenectomy is part of definitive treatment.

**Q:** What is the grading system for varicoceles?

**A:** Grade 1: palpable only with Valsalva. Grade 2: palpable without Valsalva. Grade 3: visible through scrotal skin.

Varicoceles are graded by physical exam findings. Grade 3 is most severe and often associated with infertility.

**Q:** What are indications for varicocele treatment?

**A:** Impaired sperm quality/quantity, pain affecting quality of life, adolescent with affected testis failing to grow, cosmetic reasons

Varicocelectomy can improve fertility outcomes. Treatment is not always required if asymptomatic with normal semen analysis.

**Q:** What is the time window for testicular torsion salvage?

**A:** Testicular viability decreases significantly after 5-6 hours. Surgery within 6 hours has best outcomes.

Torsion causes arterial ischemia. After 6 hours, necrosis of testicular tissue occurs. Delayed presentation often leads to orchiectomy.

**Q:** What is Peyronie disease?

**A:** Acquired curvature of the penile shaft due to fibrous thickening of the tunica albuginea

Associated with DM, vascular disease, Dupuytren contracture, trauma, and erectile dysfunction. Can cause pain with erection.

**Q:** What is the difference between paraphimosis and phimosis?

**A:** Paraphimosis: foreskin retracted behind glans and cannot be reduced. Phimosis: inability to retract foreskin over glans.

Paraphimosis is an emergency due to vascular compromise. Phimosis can be physiologic (90% resolve by age 3) or pathologic.

**Q:** What is the definition of premature ejaculation?

**A:** Ejaculation occurring before one or both partners desire, with inability to control or delay. Primary: never had control. Secondary: loss of control after period of normal function.

IELT <1 minute is suggestive. Psychological factors are common in primary; medical conditions in secondary.

**Q:** What is the treatment for torsion of the testicular appendix?

**A:** Analgesia; most resolve in 5-7 days. Surgical exploration and excision if refractory pain.

Unlike testicular torsion, this is not a surgical emergency but exploration may be needed for diagnosis if uncertain.

**Q:** What is Bowen disease vs erythroplasia of Queyrat?

**A:** Both are penile carcinoma in situ. Bowen: crusted red plaques on shaft. Erythroplasia: velvet red, ulcerated plaques on glans.

Both are pre-invasive SCC of the penis. Erythroplasia on the glans has higher risk of progression to invasive cancer.

**Q:** What is the investigation of choice for varicocele?

**A:** Ultrasound Doppler with Valsalva maneuver to assess reversal of blood flow

US confirms diagnosis and rules out secondary causes (e.g., renal tumor causing compression). Valsalva increases sensitivity.

**Q:** What percentage of varicoceles are left-sided and why?

**A:** 90% left-sided due to incompetent valves in the left testicular vein which drains into the left renal vein (higher pressure)

The left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a perpendicular angle, making valvular incompetence more common.

## Urology

**Q:** What are the four types of kidney stones, their key features, and which is radiolucent on KUB x-ray?

**A:** Calcium (75-85%): radiopaque, most common. Uric acid (5-10%): RADIOLUCENT on KUB, seen on CT, acidic urine pH <5.5. Struvite (5-10%): associated with UTI from urease-producing organisms, staghorn calculi. Cystine (1%): autosomal recessive, aggressive disease in young patients.

Uric acid stones are the only radiolucent stones on KUB but are visible on CT. Struvite stones are caused by urease-splitting organisms (Proteus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella) and require complete clearance. E. coli does NOT cause struvite stones. Calcium stone prevention includes increased fluids, citrate, reduced salt, and thiazide diuretics for hypercalciuria.

**Q:** A 25-year-old male presents with a painless testicular mass. Serum AFP is elevated but β-hCG is normal. What is the most likely tumor type and what is the initial management?

**A:** Non-seminomatous germ cell tumor (AFP is NEVER elevated in pure seminoma). Initial management is radical inguinal orchiectomy.

Testicular tumors are divided into seminoma (50%) and non-seminoma (50%). AFP is never elevated in pure seminoma, making it a key distinguishing marker. β-hCG can be elevated in both (7% of pure seminomas). All stages require radical orchiectomy via an inguinal approach - a trans-scrotal approach is contraindicated due to risk of lymphatic seeding.

**Q:** What is the most common congenital obstructive urethral lesion in male infants and how does it present on prenatal ultrasound?

**A:** Posterior urethral valves (PUV). Prenatal U/S shows bilateral hydronephrosis, thickened trabeculated bladder, dilated posterior urethra ('keyhole sign'), and oligohydramnios in a male fetus.

PUV is caused by abnormal mucosal folds at the distal prostatic urethra. Treatment involves immediate catheterization followed by cystoscopic resection. If resection is not possible, vesicostomy is indicated. PUV can lead to renal dysplasia and secondary vesicoureteral reflux.

**Q:** What is the Bosniak classification system and at what class is surgical resection recommended?

**A:** Bosniak classifies renal cysts by CT features and malignancy risk. Class I-II: simple cysts, near zero risk, no follow-up. Class IIF: minimally complex, ≤20% risk, surveillance. Class III: complex cyst, >50% risk, surgical resection. Class IV: likely malignant, >90% risk, surgical resection.

The Bosniak classification uses contrast CT features including septal thickness, calcification, and enhancement to stratify renal cyst malignancy risk. Simple cysts (Class I-II) are extremely common (up to 50% at age 50) and require no intervention. Class III and IV cysts require surgical resection due to significant malignancy risk.

**Q:** A 65-year-old smoker presents with painless gross hematuria. What is the most important diagnosis to rule out and what is the initial investigation of choice?

**A:** Urothelial (bladder) carcinoma must be ruled out. Initial investigation: cystoscopy combined with CT urography.

Unexplained hematuria in any individual >40 years must be investigated to rule out malignancy. Cystoscopy is the initial procedure of choice for diagnosis and staging of urothelial malignancy. TURBT is both diagnostic and often therapeutic, establishing diagnosis and determining depth of invasion. The 'field defect' theory explains why urothelial carcinoma has a high recurrence rate.

**Q:** What is post-obstructive diuresis and how should it be managed?

**A:** Polyuria (>3L/24h or >200 cc/h for 2 consecutive hours) after relief of urinary obstruction. Management: admit, monitor hemodynamics/electrolytes, replace every 1 mL of urine output with 0.5 mL half-normal saline IV.

Physiologic POD is due to excretion of retained urea, sodium, and water (high osmotic load) and is self-limiting within 48 hours. Pathologic POD is a sodium-wasting nephropathy from impaired tubular concentrating ability. Avoid glucose-containing fluids to prevent iatrogenic diuresis. Monitor Na and K every 6-12 hours.

**Q:** What is the classic triad of renal cell carcinoma and how often does it present?

**A:** Gross hematuria, flank pain, palpable mass — only found in 10-15% of patients ('too late triad').

Most RCC is diagnosed incidentally by US or CT (>50%). RCC originates from proximal convoluted tubule cells, with clear cell being the most common subtype (75-85%). Top 3 risk factors are smoking, HTN, and obesity. Paraneoplastic syndromes occur in 10-40% of patients.

**Q:** What are the paraneoplastic syndromes associated with renal cell carcinoma?

**A:** Hematopoietic (anemia, polycythemia, elevated ESR), endocrinopathies (hypercalcemia, erythrocytosis, HTN from renin), Stauffer syndrome (abnormal LFTs without metastases), hemodynamic (systolic HTN from AV shunting).

Stauffer syndrome is hepatic cell dysfunction (abnormal LFTs, decreased WBC, fever, hepatic necrosis) that is reversible following removal of the primary tumor. RCC may invade renal veins and IVC, resulting in varicocele, ascites, and pulmonary emboli.

**Q:** What is the most common histological type of bladder cancer and its key presenting symptom?

**A:** Urothelial carcinoma (>90%) presenting with painless hematuria (85-90% at diagnosis).

Smoking is implicated in 60% of new bladder cancer cases. Non-muscle invasive disease (75%) has >80% overall survival but high recurrence rates. Muscle-invasive disease (25%) has 50-60% 5-year survival. TURBT is the gold standard for diagnosis and often therapeutic. BCG intravesical therapy prevents progression.

**Q:** What is the Gleason score in prostate cancer?

**A:** A grading system based on cellular differentiation and pattern of prostate adenocarcinoma, used to determine mortality risk: <7 = low risk, 7 = intermediate, 8-10 = high risk.

Prostate cancer is the most prevalent cancer in males and 3rd leading cause of male cancer death. 60-70% of nodules arise in the peripheral zone. DRE and PSA are primary screening tools. Active surveillance is safe for low-risk, small volume Gleason <7 disease.

**Q:** What is the management of testicular torsion?

**A:** Emergency surgical exploration and bilateral orchiopexy. Definitive diagnosis is NOT necessary before proceeding to the OR. Necrosis of testicular tissue occurs in 5-6 hours.

Testicular torsion presents with acute severe scrotal pain, absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn's sign, and horizontal lie of the testicle. US Doppler shows decreased uptake. Any acute scrotal swelling/pain in a young boy should be considered torsion until proven otherwise.

**Q:** What tumour markers are used for testicular cancer staging?

**A:** Beta-hCG, AFP, and LDH. Beta-hCG and AFP are positive in 85% of non-seminomatous tumours. Beta-hCG is positive in 7% of pure seminomas. AFP is NEVER elevated in pure seminoma.

Testicular cancer is the most common solid malignancy in young males (15-35 years). 95% are germ cell tumours (50% seminoma, 50% non-seminoma). Radical inguinal orchiectomy is the standard approach — trans-scrotal approach is contraindicated to avoid disrupting lymphatic drainage.

**Q:** What are the risk factors and complications of cryptorchidism?

**A:** Risk factors: prematurity. Complications: reduced fertility (bilateral untreated = ~100% infertility), increased malignancy risk (10-40x), increased testicular torsion risk.

Cryptorchidism occurs in 1-4.6% of full-term newborns. Most undescended testes complete descent within the first 4 months of life. Orchiopexy is the standard treatment and facilitates testicular monitoring, may reduce malignancy risk, and reduces torsion risk.

**Q:** What investigation must be performed before attempting urethral catheterization in suspected urethral injury?

**A:** Retrograde urethrogram (RUG).

Signs of urethral injury include blood at the urethral meatus, high-riding prostate on DRE, butterfly perineal hematoma, and sensation of voiding without urine output. Posterior urethral injuries are associated with pelvic fractures; anterior injuries are from straddle injuries. Stricture is the most common complication.

**Q:** What is the Bosniak classification for renal cysts?

**A:** Class I: simple cyst, near zero malignancy risk. Class II: thin septum, minimal risk. Class IIF: minimally complex, ≤20% risk, needs follow-up. Class III: complex with thickened septa, >50% risk, surgical resection. Class IV: likely malignant with enhancing components, >90% risk, surgical resection.

Simple renal cysts are very common (up to 50% at age 50) and are usually incidental findings. The Bosniak system stratifies cysts by CT features to guide management decisions from observation to surgical intervention.

**Q:** What is the approach to managing a small solid renal mass?

**A:** Initial workup: kidney function, CXR, contrast CT/MRI ± biopsy. If limited life expectancy: watchful waiting. <2 cm: active surveillance with imaging q3-6 months. 2-4 cm: active surveillance or definitive therapy (biopsy + ablation or partial nephrectomy).

There is controversy over optimal management of small renal masses. Partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing) is preferred for tumors roughly <4 cm or in solitary kidney/bilateral tumors. Radical nephrectomy involves en bloc removal of kidney, tumor, ipsilateral adrenal, and Gerota's fascia.

**Q:** What conditions are associated with congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens?

**A:** Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene mutations.

CFTR mutations cause congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens and epididymal obstruction, leading to obstructive azoospermia. Before intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), both the patient and partner should be screened for CF. Kartagener syndrome (ciliary dyskinesia) also causes infertility through impaired sperm motility.

**Q:** What is Wilms' tumour and how does it present?

**A:** Embryonal kidney tumour in children (average age 3.5 years), presenting as a large, firm, unilateral abdominal mass (80%), with flank pain (30-40%) and microscopic hematuria (12-25%).

Wilms' tumour is the most common primary malignant renal tumour of childhood, comprising 5% of all childhood cancers. Associated syndromes include WAGR (Wilms', aniridia, genital anomaly, retardation), Beckwith-Wiedemann, and Denys-Drash. Treatment combines surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy with 90% 5-year survival.

**Q:** What is hypospadias and when should it be repaired?

**A:** A congenital condition where the urethral meatus opens on the ventral side of the penis, proximal to normal. Optimal surgical repair is before age 2. Neonatal circumcision should be deferred because the foreskin may be needed for repair.

Hypospadias occurs in 1 in 300 live male births, with distal forms being more common. It may be associated with ventral penile curvature, disorders of sexual differentiation, undescended testicles, or inguinal hernia. Prevalence is higher in white males.

**Q:** What is the most common congenital obstructive urethral lesion in male infants?

**A:** Posterior urethral valves (PUV).

PUV are abnormal mucosal folds at the distal prostatic urethra causing varying obstruction. Antenatal features include bilateral hydronephrosis, distended bladder, and oligohydramnios. The 'keyhole sign' on prenatal US is characteristic. Treatment is immediate catheterization followed by cystoscopic valve resection.

**Q:** What is the PSA and what causes it to be elevated?

**A:** PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a glycoprotein produced by prostate epithelial cells. Elevated by: BPH, prostatitis, prostate biopsy/surgery, prostatic massage, urinary retention, catheterization, cystoscopy, exercise, ejaculation, and prostate cancer.

PSA is specific to the prostate but NOT to prostate cancer. No single cut-off is universally justifiable, though <4 ng/mL is traditional. Decreased free:total PSA ratio and elevated PSA velocity/density are associated with increased cancer risk. Canadian guidelines recommend shared decision-making about screening starting at age 50.

**Q:** A 28-year-old sexually active woman presents with 2 days of dysuria and urinary frequency without vaginal discharge. Urine dipstick shows positive leukocytes and nitrites. What is the first-line treatment?

**A:** TMP/SMX (cotrimoxazole) 160/800 mg BID for 3 days, OR nitrofurantoin 100 mg BID for 5 days.

Uncomplicated UTI in women can be treated empirically when history, exam, and dipstick are consistent. The UTI clinical decision aid uses dysuria + positive leukocytes + positive nitrites (2 or more criteria) to justify treatment without culture.

**Q:** What are the risk factors that classify a UTI as 'complicated,' requiring longer antibiotic courses?

**A:** Male sex, pregnancy, recent urinary tract instrumentation, functional/anatomic abnormality of urinary tract, chronic renal disease, immunosuppression, indwelling catheter.

Complicated UTIs require broader spectrum antibiotics for 7-14 days (e.g., TMP/SMX 160/800 BID for 7-14 days or ciprofloxacin 500 BID for 7 days) because the risk of treatment failure and complications is higher.

**Q:** A 55-year-old man reports gradual onset of erectile dysfunction over 6 months with absent morning erections. What is the most likely etiology, and what distinguishes this from psychogenic ED?

**A:** Organic (vascular) ED. Organic ED has gradual onset, global (not situational), constant course, poor non-coital erections, and absent morning erections. Psychogenic ED has acute onset, situational, varying course, and preserved morning erections.

Vascular disease accounts for 90% of organic ED. The presence or absence of morning erections is a key differentiator. Initial workup includes testosterone, prolactin, LH, HbA1c, and lipid profile. First-line treatment is PDE5 inhibitors.

**Q:** What are the contraindications to prescribing phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil) for erectile dysfunction?

**A:** Concurrent use of nitrates (absolute contraindication due to risk of severe hypotension).

PDE5 inhibitors potentiate the hypotensive effects of nitrates by enhancing nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation. This combination can cause life-threatening hypotension. Patients on any form of nitrate therapy must not use PDE5 inhibitors.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for uncomplicated UTI in women?

**A:** TMP/SMX 160/800 mg BID x 3 days OR nitrofurantoin 100 mg BID x 5 days.

If dysuria + leukocytes + nitrites on dipstick, treat empirically without further investigations. Complicated UTIs (male, immunocompromised, catheter) require longer broader-spectrum courses (7-14 days).

**Q:** What differentiates organic from psychogenic erectile dysfunction?

**A:** Organic: gradual onset, global (all circumstances), constant course, poor morning erections, absent non-coital erections. Psychogenic: acute onset, situational, varying course, morning erections present.

Vascular causes account for 90% of organic ED. Key investigations include testosterone, prolactin, LH, HbA1C, and lipid profile. PDE5 inhibitors are contraindicated with nitrates.

**Q:** What is the most common histological subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and from which cells does it originate?

**A:** Clear cell subtype (75-85%), originating from proximal convoluted tubule epithelial cells.

Other subtypes include papillary (10-15%), chromophobe (5-10%), collecting duct (<1%), and other (<1%). Sarcomatoid or rhabdoid elements in any subtype are markers of poor prognosis. Hereditary forms are seen with von Hippel-Lindau syndrome.

**Q:** What is the classic 'too late triad' of renal cell carcinoma?

**A:** Gross hematuria, flank pain, and palpable mass. Found in only 10-15% of patients.

RCC is usually asymptomatic and frequently diagnosed incidentally by U/S or CT in over 50% of cases. The classic triad indicates advanced disease. Most patients now present before developing all three features due to widespread use of abdominal imaging.

**Q:** What are the top 3 risk factors for renal cell carcinoma?

**A:** Smoking, hypertension, and obesity.

Other risk factors include end-stage renal disease with acquired renal cystic disease and environmental exposures such as aromatic hydrocarbons. Hereditary syndromes (von Hippel-Lindau, hereditary papillary RCC, Birt-Hogg-Dube) also increase risk significantly.

**Q:** What paraneoplastic syndromes are associated with RCC?

**A:** Hematopoietic disturbances (anemia, polycythemia, elevated ESR), endocrinopathies (hypercalcemia from increased vitamin D hydroxylation, erythrocytosis from increased erythropoietin, HTN from increased renin), Stauffer syndrome (abnormal LFTs, decreased WBC, fever, hepatic necrosis without metastases), and hemodynamic alterations (systolic HTN, peripheral edema).

Paraneoplastic syndromes occur in 10-40% of RCC patients. Stauffer syndrome is particularly notable as it mimics hepatic metastases but is reversible following removal of the primary tumor. These syndromes can be the presenting features of RCC.

**Q:** What is the difference between radical nephrectomy and partial nephrectomy for RCC?

**A:** Radical nephrectomy involves en bloc removal of the kidney, tumour, ipsilateral adrenal gland (in upper pole tumours), and intact Gerota's fascia. Partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing) is used for small tumours (roughly <4 cm) or in cases of solitary kidney/bilateral tumours.

Both approaches provide excellent oncologic results for early RCC. The EORTC-30904 trial showed no significant difference in 10-year survival between NSS and radical nephrectomy for small, solitary, low-stage RCC. Partial nephrectomy preserves renal function and is preferred when feasible.

**Q:** What is the 5-year survival for RCC by T stage?

**A:** T1: 90-100%, T2-T3: 60%, Metastatic disease: <5% 10-year survival.

Stage at diagnosis is the most important prognostic factor for RCC. Predictors of relapse include tumour grade, local extent of the primary tumour, presence of local metastases, and histological subtype. Most common metastatic sites are bone, brain, lung, and liver.

**Q:** What is the Bosniak classification used for, and what are the management recommendations for each class?

**A:** It stratifies renal cysts for risk of malignancy based on contrast CT features. Class I-II (simple/minimally complex): no follow-up. Class IIF: follow-up imaging 6-12 months, surgery if evolves. Class III (>50% malignancy risk): surgical resection. Class IV (>90% malignancy risk): surgical resection.

The Bosniak classification helps determine whether a renal cyst requires intervention. Key features that increase malignancy risk include thickened/irregular septa, calcifications, and enhancement after contrast administration. Growing literature suggests surveillance may be safe for some Class III cysts.

**Q:** What is the most common type of bladder carcinoma and what is the main risk factor?

**A:** Urothelial carcinoma (UC) accounts for >90% of cases. Smoking is the main risk factor, implicated in 60% of new cases.

Other risk factors include aromatic amines, cyclophosphamide, pelvic radiation, Schistosoma hematobium (associated with SCC), and chronic irritation. Bladder cancer is the 2nd most common urological malignancy with M:F ratio of 3:1 and mean age at diagnosis of 65 years.

**Q:** What is the key presenting symptom of bladder carcinoma and what is the gold standard investigation?

**A:** Hematuria is the key symptom (85-90% at diagnosis). TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) is the gold standard — it is both diagnostic and often therapeutic, establishing diagnosis and determining depth of penetration.

Unexplained hematuria in any individual over 40 years old must be investigated to rule out malignancy. The entire urinary tract must be evaluated unless there is clear evidence of glomerular bleeding. Cystoscopy is the initial procedure of choice for diagnosis and staging.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) vs muscle invasive bladder cancer?

**A:** NMIBC: TURBT + intravesical chemotherapy, with BCG for high-risk (T1, CIS, high-grade Ta). Muscle invasive (T2+): radical cystectomy + pelvic lymph node dissection, with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine-platinum). Chemoradiation is an alternative for select patients.

75% of bladder cancers are non-muscle invasive at diagnosis with >80% overall survival, though 15% progress to invasive disease. For muscle invasive disease, neoadjuvant chemotherapy before cystectomy improves survival compared to cystectomy alone, as shown in landmark trials.

**Q:** What is the 'field defect' theory in urothelial carcinoma?

**A:** The entire urothelium (from renal pelvis to bladder) is bathed in carcinogens, which explains why UC has multiple lesions and a high recurrence rate.

This theory has important clinical implications: the entire urinary tract must be evaluated in patients with UC, and lifelong surveillance with cystoscopy is required. UC is frequently multifocal, and recurrence rates are high even after complete resection of visible tumours.

**Q:** What is the tumour grade's significance in bladder cancer prognosis?

**A:** Tumour grade is the single most important prognostic factor for progression. Low grade: 10% invasive, 60% recur locally. High grade: 50-80% are invasive or expected to progress to invasive.

Grading determines aggressiveness of treatment. High-grade tumours require more intensive surveillance and often intravesical BCG therapy. Carcinoma in situ (CIS) is a high-grade flat lesion with aggressive behavior and higher recurrence rates following radical cystectomy.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for prostate cancer?

**A:** Age >50 years (risk increases 1%/yr after 65), African descent, high dietary fat (2x risk), family history (1st degree relative = 2x, 1st and 2nd degree = 9x risk), and positive BRCA mutation.

Prostate cancer is the most prevalent cancer in males and the 3rd leading cause of male cancer deaths. Up to 50% risk at age 50, with 75% diagnosed between ages 60-85. The mean age at diagnosis is 65 years, and lifetime risk of death from prostate cancer is 3%.

**Q:** Where do most prostate cancer nodules arise anatomically?

**A:** 60-70% arise in the peripheral zone. 10-20% in the transition zone. 5-10% in the central zone.

The peripheral zone location explains why DRE can detect many prostate cancers, as the peripheral zone is accessible to digital examination posteriorly. In contrast, BPH primarily affects the transition zone. The zonal anatomy is important for understanding cancer detection and treatment.

**Q:** How is prostate cancer risk stratified and what are the categories?

**A:** Low-risk: PSA <10, Gleason <7 (Grade Group 1), stage T1-T2a. Intermediate-risk: PSA 10-20, Gleason 7 (Grade Group 2-3), stage T2b-T2c. High-risk: PSA >20, Gleason 8-10 (Grade Group 4-5), stage T3/T4.

Risk stratification guides treatment decisions. Any single criterion meeting a higher risk category places the patient in that category. Low-risk patients may be candidates for active surveillance, while high-risk patients require definitive therapy with or without adjuvant treatments.

**Q:** What is the role of active surveillance in prostate cancer management?

**A:** Active surveillance (serial PSA, DRE, and biopsies) is safe for low-risk, small volume, Gleason <7 prostate cancer. It avoids the side effects of definitive treatment while monitoring for disease progression.

The ProtecT trial showed that at 10 years, prostate-cancer-specific mortality was low regardless of treatment (monitoring vs. surgery vs. radiotherapy). However, surgery and radiotherapy had lower rates of disease progression and metastasis compared to active monitoring.

**Q:** What is castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and how is it managed?

**A:** CRPC is prostate cancer that progresses despite castrate levels of testosterone. ADT should be maintained. Non-metastatic CRPC: observation vs. apalutamide/enzalutamide/darolutamide. Metastatic CRPC: abiraterone, enzalutamide, or docetaxel-based chemotherapy. Post-docetaxel: cabazitaxel. Bone mets: denosumab/zoledronic acid + palliative radiation.

CRPC represents a lethal phenotype of prostate cancer. Multiple new agents have improved survival in recent years, including androgen receptor signaling inhibitors and PARP inhibitors (olaparib for HRR mutations). Lutetium-177 PSMA is a newer option post-docetaxel and ARPI failure.

**Q:** What are causes of elevated PSA besides prostate cancer?

**A:** BPH, prostatitis, prostatic ischemia/infarction, prostate biopsy/surgery, prostatic massage, acute urinary retention, urethral catheterization, cystoscopy, TRUS, strenuous exercise, ejaculation, acute renal failure, cardiac surgery-related ischemia, and radiation therapy.

PSA is specific to the prostate but NOT to prostate cancer. This is why PSA screening has high sensitivity but lower specificity. Free:total PSA ratio, PSA velocity, and PSA density can help improve specificity. No single justifiable PSA cut-off point exists.

**Q:** What is the most common solid malignancy in young males aged 15-35?

**A:** Testicular cancer.

Any solid testicular mass or acute hydrocele in a young patient must rule out malignancy. 95% are germ cell tumours (50% seminoma, 50% non-seminoma). Risk factors include cryptorchidism (10-40x increased risk), testicular atrophy, HIV, infertility, and family history.

**Q:** What tumour markers are used in testicular cancer and how do they distinguish seminoma from non-seminoma?

**A:** Beta-hCG, AFP, and LDH. Beta-hCG and AFP are positive in 85% of non-seminomatous tumours. Beta-hCG is positive in only 7% of pure seminomas. AFP is NEVER elevated with seminoma.

If AFP is elevated, the tumor is non-seminomatous regardless of histology, which is critical for treatment planning. Elevated markers return to normal post-orchiectomy if no metastasis. These markers are also used for staging and monitoring treatment response.

**Q:** Why must orchiectomy for testicular cancer be performed through an inguinal incision rather than a scrotal approach?

**A:** The testicle drains via inguinal lymphatics. A trans-scrotal approach risks tumour seeding along scrotal lymphatics, which would alter the pattern of metastatic spread and complicate treatment.

Radical inguinal orchiectomy involves ligating the spermatic cord inside the inguinal canal to prevent proximal tumour spread. This approach preserves the normal lymphatic drainage pattern, which is important for subsequent staging and treatment decisions including retroperitoneal lymph node dissection.

**Q:** What is the clinical presentation and management of testicular torsion?

**A:** Acute onset severe scrotal pain and swelling, retracted and transverse-lying testicle (horizontal lie), negative Prehn's sign, and absent cremasteric reflex. Management: emergency surgical exploration and bilateral orchiopexy. Testicular necrosis occurs in 5-6 hours. Definitive diagnosis is NOT necessary to take to OR.

Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency. The 'bell clapper deformity' predisposes to torsion. In young boys, any acute scrotal swelling/pain is torsion until proven otherwise. Bilateral orchiopexy is performed because the contralateral testis often has the same predisposing anatomy.

**Q:** How do you differentiate testicular torsion from epididymitis on physical exam?

**A:** Torsion: diffuse tenderness, absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn's sign (no pain relief with testicular elevation), horizontal lie. Epididymitis: epididymal tenderness, present cremasteric reflex, positive Prehn's sign (pain relief with elevation).

The cremasteric reflex is elicited by stroking the inner thigh and observing ipsilateral testicular elevation. Its absence is highly suggestive of torsion. Doppler ultrasound showing decreased blood flow to the testicular artery confirms torsion, but surgery should not be delayed for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

**Q:** What is the transurethral resection (TUR) syndrome and how does it present?

**A:** Caused by absorption of large volume of hypotonic irrigation solution (glycine) through perforated venous sinusoids during TURP, leading to hypervolemic hyponatremic state. Presents with dilutional hyponatremia, confusion, nausea, vomiting, HTN, bradycardia, visual disturbances, CHF, and pulmonary edema.

Treatment involves diuresis and, if severe, hypertonic saline administration. This complication does not occur with bipolar TURP because saline is used as irrigation instead of glycine. Recognition of early symptoms (confusion, visual changes) is important to prevent progression.

**Q:** What are the medical treatment options for BPH and their mechanisms?

**A:** Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, terazosin, doxazosin): reduce stromal smooth muscle tone (dynamic component). 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride): block testosterone to DHT conversion, reduce prostate volume (static component). Combination therapy is more effective than either alone.

The MTOPS trial showed combination therapy with doxazosin and finasteride reduced BPH clinical progression by 66% vs placebo, significantly more than either agent alone. Alpha-blockers provide faster symptom relief, while 5-ARIs take months to reduce prostate volume.

**Q:** What are the indications for TURP in BPH?

**A:** Obstructive uropathy (large bladder diverticula, renal insufficiency), two or more episodes of urinary retention, recurrent UTIs, recurrent gross hematuria, bladder stones, and intolerance/failure of medical therapy.

TURP is the traditional gold standard surgical treatment for BPH. Newer options include green-light laser photovaporization, laser enucleation, and minimally invasive surgical therapies (Rezum water-vapour therapy, UroLift, iTind). Retrograde ejaculation occurs in >75% post-TURP.

**Q:** What are the indications and contraindications for extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL)?

**A:** Indications: renal calculi <1.5 cm and ureteral calculi; must be radio-opaque (not for uric acid stones). Contraindications: acute UTI/urosepsis, bleeding disorder/coagulopathy, pregnancy, uncontrolled HTN, aortic aneurysm, and obstruction distal to stone.

ESWL is the least invasive treatment option but also least successful. It uses fluoroscopy-guided shockwaves to fragment stones. Common complications include hematuria, bacteremia, ureteric obstruction by fragments, and perinephric hematoma. Patient preference and wait times play a large role in stone management.

**Q:** What is von Hippel-Lindau syndrome and its urological significance?

**A:** An autosomal dominant syndrome featuring multiple bilateral renal cysts and/or renal cell carcinomas (50% incidence of RCC), along with cerebellar/spinal/retinal hemangioblastomas, pancreatic and epididymal cysts, and pheochromocytomas.

VHL is caused by a mutation in the VHL tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 3p. Patients require lifelong surveillance for RCC and other tumours. Genetic testing should be considered in patients with bilateral/multifocal renal tumours, onset <45 years, or family history of renal tumours.

**Q:** What is angiomyolipoma and what CT finding is pathognomonic?

**A:** A benign renal neoplasm consisting of blood vessels (angio-), smooth muscle (-myo-), and fat (-lipoma). Negative attenuation (-20 HU) on CT (representing fat) is pathognomonic.

Angiomyolipomas account for <10% of adult renal tumours, with 20% associated with tuberous sclerosis (especially if multiple/recurrent). They may extend into regional lymphatics. Management includes surgical excision or embolization if symptomatic, with potential role for mTOR inhibitors in unresectable disease.

**Q:** What is Wilms' tumour and what are its associated syndromes?

**A:** An embryonal tumour (nephroblastoma) from immature kidney cells, the most common primary malignant renal tumour of childhood (average age 3.5 years). Presents as a large, firm, unilateral abdominal mass. Associated syndromes: WAGR (Wilms', aniridia, genital anomaly, retardation), Beckwith-Wiedemann, and Denys-Drash.

Treatment involves radical nephrectomy or nephron-sparing surgery plus chemotherapy and radiation. Bilateral disease (5%) is treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by nephron-sparing surgery. Prognosis is excellent with 90% 5-year survival and 15% recurrence rate.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of urethral injury and what investigation should all patients with suspected urethral injury undergo?

**A:** Blood at urethral meatus, high-riding prostate on DRE, butterfly perineal hematoma, penile/scrotal hematoma, sensation of voiding without urine output, distended bladder. All patients should undergo retrograde urethrogram (RUG).

Urethral injury is a contraindication to blind catheterization as it may convert a partial tear to a complete disruption. RUG or cystoscopy should be performed before attempting catheterization. If catheterization is not possible, a suprapubic cystostomy is placed.

**Q:** What are the contraindications to urethral catheterization?

**A:** Blood at the urinary meatus, scrotal hematoma, pelvic fracture, and/or high-riding prostate.

These signs suggest urethral injury, and blind catheterization could worsen the injury. A retrograde urethrogram should be performed first. If urethral injury is confirmed or catheterization is not possible, suprapubic catheterization is the alternative.

**Q:** What percentage of blunt renal traumas are minor, and what are the indications for surgical intervention?

**A:** 90% are minor (contusions and superficial lacerations). Absolute indication for surgery: hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability. Relative indications: non-viable tissue, major laceration, urinary extravasation, vascular injury, expanding/pulsating perirenal mass.

The majority of renal trauma is now managed conservatively and nonoperatively. CT with contrast (triphasic) is the imaging modality of choice for stable patients. The degree of hematuria does not correlate with severity of injury. HTN develops in 5% as a late complication.

**Q:** What is ischemic (low-flow) priapism and how is it managed?

**A:** A prolonged erection lasting >4 hours in the absence of sexual excitement/desire, most commonly ischemic (thromboembolic, e.g., sickle cell disease). Management: needle aspiration and decompression of corpora cavernosa, phenylephrine intracorporeal injection q3-5 min, and surgical shunt if no response.

Ischemic priapism is a urological emergency because prolonged ischemia leads to corporal smooth muscle necrosis and permanent erectile dysfunction. Cavernosal blood gas analysis and Doppler U/S help distinguish ischemic from non-ischemic priapism. Non-ischemic (high-flow) priapism from trauma is generally self-limited.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of PDE5 inhibitors in erectile dysfunction and what is an absolute contraindication?

**A:** PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) selectively inhibit phosphodiesterase-5, which degrades cGMP, leading to sinusoidal smooth muscle relaxation, increased blood flow, and erection. Absolute contraindication: concurrent use of nitrates (risk of severe hypotension).

All four PDE5 inhibitors have similar effectiveness. Tadalafil has a longer half-life, no cyanopsia, and can be taken with food. They are also contraindicated in patients with history of priapism or conditions predisposing to priapism (leukemia, sickle cell disease, polycythemia).

**Q:** What is the CHAARTED trial's main finding regarding metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer?

**A:** Combination of docetaxel and ADT resulted in significantly longer overall survival (13.6 months longer median OS) compared to ADT alone in hormone-sensitive metastatic prostate cancer.

This landmark trial established the role of early chemotherapy in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer. The median time to progression was also significantly longer in the combination group (20.2 vs 11.7 months). This changed the standard of care for newly diagnosed metastatic prostate cancer.

**Q:** What is the GnRH agonist 'flare' phenomenon in prostate cancer treatment?

**A:** GnRH agonists (leuprolide, goserelin) initially stimulate LH, causing a transient increase in testosterone ('flare') that can worsen bone pain in patients with bone metastases. After continued use, they suppress LH and testosterone levels.

The flare phenomenon occurs in the first 1-2 weeks of GnRH agonist therapy. To prevent clinical consequences, an antiandrogen (e.g., bicalutamide) is given for the first few weeks. Alternatively, GnRH antagonists (e.g., degarelix) can be used as they do not cause a flare.

**Q:** What is cryptorchidism and what are its long-term complications?

**A:** Abnormal location of testes along the normal path of descent (1-4.6% of full-term newborns). Complications: reduced fertility (untreated bilateral = ~100% infertility), increased malignancy risk (10-40x, intra-abdominal > inguinal), and increased risk of testicular torsion.

Most undescended testes complete descent within the first 4 months of life (0.7-1.0% remain at 1 year). Treatment is orchiopexy, which does not eliminate malignancy risk but allows easier monitoring and may reduce risk. Hormonal therapy has not been proven beneficial over surgery.

**Q:** What is a varicocele, how common is it, and what are the indications for treatment?

**A:** Dilation and tortuosity of the pampiniform plexus due to incompetent valves in testicular veins. Found in 15% of men, 90% left-sided. Feels like a 'bag of worms.' Treatment indications: impaired sperm quality/quantity, pain/ache affecting QOL, affected testis fails to grow in adolescents, and cosmetic concerns.

Varicoceles are present in 35-40% of infertile males, making them the most common correctable cause of male infertility. The left-sided predominance is due to the left testicular vein draining into the left renal vein at a right angle. A new right-sided varicocele should raise suspicion for retroperitoneal mass.

**Q:** What is hydronephrosis and what are the two main categories of its etiology?

**A:** Hydronephrosis is dilation of the renal pelvis, calyces, and ureters generally caused by obstruction of antegrade urine flow (pelvicaliectasis). The two main etiologic categories are: (1) Mechanical — intrinsic (calculi, BPH, strictures, trauma, neoplasms, previous urologic surgery) or extrinsic (pregnancy, uterine fibroids, colorectal/uterine/cervical malignancies, aortic aneurysm); and (2) Functional — neuropathic (neurogenic bladder, diabetic neuropathy, spinal cord disease) or hormonal (pregnancy, as progesterone decreases ureteral tone).

Hydronephrosis is a common urology presentation on the MCCQE1. Distinguishing mechanical from functional causes guides management. Pregnancy is a physiologic cause (both mechanical from gravid uterus and hormonal from progesterone). Imaging with U/S (>90% sensitive and specific) is first-line; MAG3 renogram adds functional data.

**Q:** What imaging modality is >90% sensitive and specific for hydronephrosis, and what does a MAG3 diuretic renogram add?

**A:** Ultrasound (U/S) is >90% sensitive and specific for hydronephrosis. MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) diuretic renogram provides little anatomic/structural information but evaluates differential renal function and demonstrates whether functional obstruction exists (i.e., delayed drainage of radiotracer). CT better delineates anatomy and potential causes (e.g., obstructing stone) but provides less functional information.

On the MCCQE1, knowing which modality gives anatomic vs. functional information is high-yield. U/S is the go-to first-line imaging. MAG3 is key for evaluating UPJ obstruction and differential renal function. Retrograde pyelogram can both delineate anatomy and allow stent insertion.

**Q:** Define post-obstructive diuresis (POD) and distinguish physiologic from pathologic POD.

**A:** Post-obstructive diuresis (POD) is polyuria (>3 L/24 h or >200 cc/h for 2 consecutive hours) resulting from relief of obstructive uropathy with elevated creatinine. Physiologic POD: excretion of retained urea, Na+, and H2O (high osmotic load) after obstruction relief — self-limiting, usually resolves within 48 h with PO fluids. Pathologic POD: Na+-wasting nephropathy from impaired concentrating ability of renal tubules due to decreased reabsorption of NaCl in the thick ascending limb and urea in the collecting tubule, increased medullary blood flow (solute washout), and increased flow/solute in distal nephrons.

Recognizing the transition from physiologic to pathologic POD is clinically critical. Pathologic POD requires careful IV replacement. The key management rule is replacing every 1 mL of urine output with 0.5 mL of 1/2 normal saline IV to avoid perpetuating the diuresis. Glucose-containing fluids must be avoided (cause iatrogenic osmotic diuresis).

**Q:** How is post-obstructive diuresis managed?

**A:** Management of POD: (1) Admit patient; (2) Monitor hemodynamics and electrolytes (Na+ and K+ q6–12h, replace PRN); (3) Follow creatinine and BUN to baseline; (4) Monitor urine output q2h; (5) Ensure total fluid intake < urine output by replacing every 1 mL of urine output with 0.5 mL of 1/2 NS IV (PO fluids if physiologic POD); (6) Avoid glucose-containing fluids (cause iatrogenic osmotic diuresis).

The '0.5 mL replacement per 1 mL urine output' rule is a key MCCQE1 testable fact. Over-replacement perpetuates the diuresis; under-replacement risks hemodynamic instability. Electrolyte monitoring every 6–12 hours reflects the rapid shifts that can occur.

**Q:** Define overactive bladder (OAB) and its diagnostic criteria.

**A:** Overactive bladder is a symptom complex including: urinary urgency (with or without incontinence), urinary frequency (voiding ≥8 times in 24 hours), and nocturia (awakening ≥1 time per night to void). Diagnosis requires: (1) Focused history (past GU disorders, LUTS questionnaire, 3-day micturition diary); (2) Physical exam (genitourinary, pelvic, and rectal examination); (3) Urinalysis to rule out hematuria and infection. Additional tests if indicated: post-void residual, cystoscopy (to rule out carcinoma in situ, recurrent infections), urodynamics (in older men to rule out obstruction).

OAB is thought to result from involuntary detrusor contractions. The MCCQE1 may test the distinction from stress urinary incontinence (SUI) — OAB involves urgency, while SUI involves leakage with increased abdominal pressure. The diagnostic minimum requires history, exam, and urinalysis.

**Q:** What are the first-line pharmacological treatments for overactive bladder?

**A:** First-line pharmacological options for OAB: (1) Antimuscarinics — oxybutynin hydrochloride, tolterodine, solifenacin, fesoterodine, darifenacin, propiverine, or trospium; (2) β3-adrenoceptor agonist — mirabegron. For refractory OAB: botulinum toxin bladder injection, sacral neuromodulation, or percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (not commonly used in Canada).

Non-pharmacological therapy (bladder training, pelvic floor exercises, fluid management, caffeine/alcohol avoidance, weight loss) is tried first. Antimuscarinics are the most established pharmacotherapy; mirabegron is an alternative with a different mechanism and side effect profile (avoids dry mouth). Botulinum toxin is third-line.

**Q:** What conditions can contribute to symptoms of overactive bladder (OAB)?

**A:** Conditions contributing to OAB symptoms: Lower urinary tract (UTI, obstruction, impaired bladder contractility); Neurological (stroke, MS, dementia, diabetic neuropathy); Systemic diseases (CHF, sleep disorders — primarily causing nocturia); Functional and behavioural (excessive caffeine and alcohol, constipation, impaired mobility); Medications (diuretics, cholinergic agents, narcotics, calcium-channel blockers, cholinesterase inhibitors).

The MCCQE1 may present a case with apparent OAB symptoms requiring you to exclude secondary causes. Cholinesterase inhibitors (used in dementia) can worsen bladder overactivity — this is a common drug-induced cause. Treating the underlying condition (e.g., UTI, CHF) may resolve OAB symptoms.

**Q:** What is the antibiotic treatment for simple uncomplicated UTI in Canada?

**A:** First-line options for simple, uncomplicated UTI: (1) TMP-SMX 160/800 mg PO BID × 3 days; (2) Nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO BID × 5 days; (3) Fosfomycin 3g PO × single dose. Choice should be guided by local resistance patterns. Fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin) are reserved for complicated UTIs due to resistance concerns.

The MCCQE1 tests antibiotic selection for UTI. TMP-SMX is cheap and effective but local resistance rates must be considered (avoid if local resistance >20%). Nitrofurantoin is safe in pregnancy (avoid near term). Fosfomycin's single-dose convenience improves adherence. Fluoroquinolones should be preserved for complicated UTIs.

**Q:** What antibiotic regimens are used for gonococcal vs. non-gonococcal urethritis?

**A:** Gonococcal urethritis: Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM × 1 dose PLUS treat for Chlamydia trachomatis co-infection. Non-gonococcal urethritis (usually Chlamydia): Azithromycin 1g PO × 1 day OR doxycycline 100 mg PO BID × 7 days. Antibiotic therapy should always be based on local resistance patterns and adjusted according to culture and sensitivity results.

Co-treatment for chlamydia when treating gonorrhea is standard practice due to high co-infection rates. Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome — urethritis, uveitis/conjunctivitis, arthritis — 'can't pee, can't see, can't climb a tree') can follow Chlamydia infection. If culture-negative or unresponsive to treatment, consider Mycoplasma/Ureaplasma.

**Q:** What are the defining features and antibiotic duration for acute vs. chronic bacterial prostatitis?

**A:** Acute bacterial prostatitis (Category I): LUTS + pelvic pain + systemic signs (fever, chills, malaise) + leukocytosis in prostatic fluid + positive bacterial cultures (SEEK PP, 80% E. coli); treat with ciprofloxacin or TMP-SMX × 2–4 weeks. Chronic bacterial prostatitis (Category II): LUTS + pelvic pain without systemic signs + recurrent UTIs + leukocytosis + positive cultures; treat with ciprofloxacin × 4–6 weeks (plus α-blockers, anti-inflammatories). Chronic pelvic pain syndrome (Category III): LUTS + pelvic pain without evidence of infection; multimodal therapy.

The four NIH categories of prostatitis are high-yield for MCCQE1. The key distinction between Categories I and II is systemic signs (fever, malaise) only in acute. Category III (CPPS) is the most common — 90% of prostatitis cases — and does not require antibiotics unless culture-naive. Category IV is asymptomatic and found incidentally.

**Q:** What is the 4-glass test used for in prostatitis diagnosis?

**A:** The 4-glass (Meares-Stamey) test involves culturing: VB1 (first voided urine = urethral specimen), VB2 (midstream urine = bladder specimen), EPS (expressed prostatic secretions obtained after prostate massage), and VB3 (urine voided after massage = post-massage specimen). It is used in chronic bacterial prostatitis (Category II) to localize infection to the prostate versus the urethra or bladder. Leukocytosis in EPS/VB3 with positive cultures confirms Category II; leukocytosis without positive cultures suggests Category IIIA.

The 4-glass test is the gold standard for differentiating prostatitis categories, particularly separating Category I (acute — cultures not needed due to obvious systemic presentation) from Categories II and III. In chronic prostatitis, the NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI) is also used to quantify symptom burden.

**Q:** What are the clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment of epididymitis?

**A:** Epididymitis clinical features: sudden onset scrotal pain and swelling (± radiation along cord to flank), scrotal erythema and tenderness, Prehn's sign (pain relief with testicle elevation), fever, storage symptoms, purulent discharge, reactive hydrocele. Diagnosis: U/A, urine C&S, urethral Gram stain/culture; if uncertain, MUST rule out testicular torsion with Doppler U/S; U/S confirms with increased vascularity. Treatment: <35 yr (presumed STI) — ceftriaxone 200 mg IM × 1 PLUS doxycycline 100 mg PO BID × 10 days; ≥35 yr (presumed urinary source) — ofloxacin 300 mg PO BID × 10 days. Plus scrotal support, bed rest, ice, and analgesia.

Differentiating epididymitis from testicular torsion is critical — torsion is a surgical emergency. Prehn's sign (pain relief with elevation) suggests epididymitis, but is not reliable. Any diagnostic uncertainty mandates urgent Doppler U/S. In <35 yr, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis are the dominant pathogens; in ≥35 yr, GI organisms (E. coli) predominate.

**Q:** What are the typical organisms causing epididymitis by age group?

**A:** <35 years: Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis (sexually transmitted). ≥35 years or penetrative anal intercourse: GI organisms, especially E. coli. Other causes: mumps orchitis (post-parotitis), TB, syphilis, granulomatous (autoimmune) in elderly men, amiodarone (involves only the head of the epididymis), and chemical epididymitis (urine reflux into ejaculatory ducts — due to increased prostatic urethral pressure from straining, heavy lifting).

Age is the key determinant for empiric antibiotic choice in epididymitis. Amiodarone is a unique drug-induced cause affecting only the epididymal head. Chemical epididymitis from urinary reflux is sterile and treated symptomatically. Mumps orchitis can cause infertility in post-pubertal males.

**Q:** What is the definition of UTI and how is it classified?

**A:** UTI definition: symptoms suggestive of UTI plus evidence of pyuria and bacteriuria on U/A or urine C&S. Asymptomatic bacteriuria = asymptomatic + ≥100,000 CFU/mL (treatment only in pregnancy, immunosuppressed, or prior to urologic surgery). Classifications: (1) Uncomplicated — lower UTI in a functionally and structurally normal urinary tract; (2) Complicated — structural/functional abnormality, male patients, immunocompromised, diabetic, iatrogenic complication, pregnancy, pyelonephritis, catheter-associated; (3) Recurrent — ≥3 UTIs/year or ≥2 UTIs/6 months.

The MCCQE1 frequently tests when NOT to treat bacteriuria — asymptomatic bacteriuria should be left untreated except in pregnancy and before urologic procedures. Complicated UTI requires longer antibiotic courses (up to 2–3 weeks) and imaging. Male UTI is always considered complicated.

**Q:** What organisms most commonly cause UTIs and what are the atypical organisms?

**A:** Typical UTI organisms (mnemonic SEEK PP): E. coli (75–95% of cases), plus Enterococcus, Klebsiella, Proteus, Pseudomonas, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus. Atypical organisms: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB), Chlamydia trachomatis, Mycoplasma (Ureaplasma urealyticum), and fungi (Candida).

E. coli dominates UTI microbiology due to its ability to produce adhesins that bind uroepithelium. S. saprophyticus is the second most common in young sexually active women. Atypical organisms require specific testing (PCR, AFB culture). Sterile pyuria should raise suspicion for TB, Chlamydia, or inadequately treated UTI.

**Q:** What is recurrent/chronic cystitis and how is it defined?

**A:** Recurrent/chronic cystitis is defined as ≥3 UTIs per year or ≥2 UTIs per 6 months. Etiology: (1) Bacterial reinfection (80%) — recurrence with a different organism, the same organism if cultured >2 weeks after therapy, or any organism with an intermittent sterile culture; (2) Bacterial persistence (relapse) — same organism cultured within 2 weeks of sensitivity-based therapy. Treatment: lifestyle changes (↑ fluids, ↓ caffeine), antibiotics (continuous low-dose daily suppression vs. post-coital only vs. self-start therapy), vaginal estrogen for post-menopausal women.

Distinguishing reinfection from persistence guides workup — persistence implies a persistent anatomic nidus (e.g., stone, biofilm, fistula) requiring investigation. Continuous prophylaxis with TMP-SMX or nitrofurantoin is recommended for 6–12 months. Topical vaginal estrogen restores atrophic mucosa that predisposes to UTI in post-menopausal women.

**Q:** What is interstitial cystitis (IC/PBS) and how is it diagnosed and treated?

**A:** Interstitial cystitis (painful bladder/bladder pain syndrome): bladder pain, chronic urgency, and frequency without other identifiable cause. 90% in females; prevalence 20/100,000; median age 40 years. Types: non-ulcerative (more common, younger) and ulcerative (Hunner's lesions, older). Features: pelvic pain (suprapubic tenderness), storage symptoms (frequency > urgency > nocturia), negative U/A + urine C&S + cytology. Cystoscopy: glomerulations (submucosal petechiae) ± Hunner's lesions (not required for diagnosis). Treatment: 1st line — education, dietary modification, bladder retraining, stress management, pelvic floor physio; 2nd line — amitriptyline, cimetidine, hydroxyzine, pentosan polysulfate (oral/intravesical), gabapentin; 3rd line — hydrodistension, botulinum toxin, sacral neuromodulation; 4th line — radical surgery.

IC is a diagnosis of exclusion. The classic triad — pelvic pain, urgency/frequency, negative cultures — in a middle-aged white woman is the MCCQE1 profile. Cystoscopic evaluation is not necessary to diagnose IC but can identify Hunner's lesions which respond to endoscopic treatment (cauterization, triamcinolone injection). Ketamine and tiaprofenic acid are drug-induced causes.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of acute pyelonephritis and how is it investigated?

**A:** Acute pyelonephritis: infection of renal parenchyma with local and systemic manifestations. Clinical features: rapid onset (<24 h), LUTS (frequency, urgency, hematuria — NOT dysuria unless concurrent cystitis), fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, myalgia, malaise, CVA tenderness, and/or flank pain. Investigations: U/A + urine C&S, CBC with differential (leukocytosis, left shift), imaging only if complicated pyelonephritis or no improvement after 48–72 h (U/S or CT), DMSA scan (photopenic defect = active infection or scar).

The absence of dysuria in pyelonephritis (unless concurrent cystitis) is a high-yield distinction. Gram-positive organisms (Enterococcus, S. aureus) require ampicillin treatment. Emphysematous pyelonephritis (gas-forming infection, typically in DM patients) usually requires nephrectomy. If febrile with suspected obstruction, emergent decompression (stent or nephrostomy) is needed.

**Q:** What are the causative organisms in acute pyelonephritis?

**A:** Ascending infection (most common, from lower UTI): Gram-negative bacilli — E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus, Pseudomonas, Enterobacter. Hematogenous route: Gram-positive cocci — Enterococcus faecalis, S. aureus, S. saprophyticus. Common underlying causes: stones, strictures, prostatic obstruction, vesicoureteric reflux, neurogenic bladder, catheters, DM, sickle-cell disease, PCKD, immunosuppression, post-renal transplant, instrumentation, pregnancy.

Knowing the route of infection predicts the likely organism — ascending (GN bacilli) vs. hematogenous (GP cocci). E. coli is still the most common overall. Suspected Enterococcus requires ampicillin. DMSA nuclear medicine scan is used when pyelonephritis diagnosis is uncertain — a photopenic defect confirms active infection or scarring.

**Q:** What is the epidemiology and recurrence rate of urinary stone disease?

**A:** Prevalence: ~8% (increasing). Sex ratio: M:F = 2:1. Peak incidence: 30–50 years. Recurrence rate: 10% at 1 year, 50% at 5 years, 60–80% lifetime. Most common stone type: calcium oxalate. Others: uric acid (5–10%), struvite (5–10%), calcium phosphate, cystine (1%).

Stone recurrence rates are clinically important because they justify metabolic workup and preventive therapy. Most important risk factor is minimal fluid intake. The high lifetime recurrence rate (60–80%) means virtually all stone patients require dietary counseling and often pharmacologic prevention after the first stone.

**Q:** What are the four narrowest passage points for upper urinary tract stones?

**A:** The four narrowest passage points for upper tract stones are: (1) Ureteropelvic junction (UPJ); (2) Pelvic brim (where ureter crosses iliac vessels); (3) Under the vas deferens/broad ligament; (4) Ureterovesical junction (UVJ) — the narrowest point.

Stones most commonly lodge at these anatomic narrowings, with UVJ being the tightest. Location of obstruction determines pain radiation pattern — UVJ stones cause trigonal irritation (frequency, urgency) and may radiate to the tip of the penis or labia. Stones <5 mm at any location have a 75% spontaneous passage rate.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of renal colic from ureteral stones?

**A:** Renal colic features: urinary obstruction → upstream distention → pain. Types: (1) Flank pain from renal capsular distention (non-colicky); (2) Severe waxing and waning pain radiating from flank to groin, testis, or tip of penis (ureteral colic from distended collecting system/ureter). Associated features: writhing persistent discomfort, nausea/vomiting, hematuria (90% microscopic), diaphoresis, tachycardia, tachypnea. UVJ stones: trigonal irritation symptoms (frequency, urgency). Bladder stones: storage and voiding LUTS, terminal hematuria, suprapubic pain.

Hematuria (90% microscopic) is nearly universal in ureteral colic. The writhing/restless nature of colic differentiates it from peritoneal irritation (where patients lie still). The pain pattern follows the ureteral course. Fever with colic mandates ruling out concurrent pyelonephritis/obstruction — a urologic emergency requiring emergent decompression.

**Q:** What medications are associated with kidney stone formation?

**A:** Medications causing kidney stones: Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide — hypercalciuria), acetazolamide (alkaline urine → calcium phosphate stones), topiramate and zonisamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitors → hypocitraturia, alkaline urine), indinavir and atazanavir (HIV protease inhibitors — radiolucent stones), acyclovir, sulfadiazine, triamterene.

Indinavir and atazanavir stones are radiolucent on KUB x-ray and even on CT (unlike most stones which are radiopaque on CT). Topiramate/zonisamide cause stone formation via carbonic anhydrase inhibition leading to hypocitraturia and alkaline urine (calcium phosphate stones). This is an MCCQE1 high-yield drug-side-effect pairing.

**Q:** What are the key features of calcium oxalate stones (etiology, radiologic appearance, prevention)?

**A:** Calcium stones (75–85%): Etiology — hypercalciuria (most common), hyperuricosuria (25% of calcium stone patients), hyperoxaluria (<5%), hypocitraturia (12%), hypomagnesemia, high dietary Na+, high urinary pH/low urine volume, hyperparathyroidism, obesity, gout, DM. Radiology: radiopaque on KUB x-ray. Prevention: increase fluids (>2 L/day), reduce animal protein, oxalate, Na+, sucrose, fructose; avoid high-dose vitamin C. NOTE: Reducing dietary calcium is NOT recommended (increases GI oxalate absorption → higher urinary calcium oxalate). Medications: thiazide diuretics (hypercalciuria), potassium citrate (hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria), allopurinol (hyperuricosuria).

The counterintuitive teaching — low dietary calcium increases stone risk — is MCCQE1 high-yield. Low Ca2+ in the gut means more oxalate absorption → more urinary oxalate → more calcium oxalate precipitation. Citrate is a stone inhibitor (forms soluble complex with calcium), explaining why potassium citrate prevents stones. Thiazides reduce urinary calcium excretion.

**Q:** What are the key features of uric acid stones?

**A:** Uric acid stones (5–10%): Etiology — uric acid precipitates in low-volume, acidic urine with high uric acid concentration; caused by hyperuricosuria, diet (purine-rich red meats), drugs (ASA, thiazides), high cell turnover (leukemia, cytotoxic drugs), gout. Key features: radiolucent on KUB x-ray but radiopaque on CT; acidic urine (pH <5.5) — NOT necessarily elevated urinary uric acid. Treatment: increase fluid intake to >2 L/day, alkalinize urine to pH 6.5–7 (potassium citrate, sodium bicarbonate), ± allopurinol; avoid making urine too alkaline (pH >7 → calcium phosphate stones).

The characteristic radiolucent appearance on KUB is the key radiologic feature — uric acid stones are the main radiolucent stone type (along with indinavir/atazanavir stones). Unlike other stones, uric acid stones can be dissolved with urinary alkalinization — this is a unique feature. The pH target of 6.5–7 is critical to know for the exam.

**Q:** What are the key features of struvite (infection) stones?

**A:** Struvite stones (5–10%): Etiology — infection with urea-splitting organisms (Proteus, Pseudomonas, Providencia, Klebsiella, Mycoplasma, Serratia, S. aureus) → alkaline urinary pH → precipitation of magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite). Key features: often staghorn calculi; perpetuates UTI (stone harbours organisms); positive urine dipstick and cultures; M:F = 1:3 (UTI more common in females). Note: E. coli does NOT cause struvite stones. Treatment: complete stone clearance required (stone harbours organisms), antibiotics for 6 weeks, regular follow-up urine cultures. ESWL partially effective; mixed calcium-struvite requires stone removal to treat infection.

Struvite stones are the most clinically dangerous — they perpetuate infection and grow to fill the collecting system (staghorn calculi). Complete stone clearance is mandatory because organisms embedded in the stone matrix cannot be eradicated with antibiotics alone. This distinguishes struvite from other stone types where medical management may be adequate.

**Q:** What are the key features of cystine stones?

**A:** Cystine stones (1%): Etiology — autosomal recessive defect in intestinal mucosal absorption and renal tubular absorption of dibasic amino acids → COLA in urine (cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine). Key features: aggressive stone disease in children and young adults, recurrent stone formation, family history, often staghorn calculi, faintly radiopaque on KUB x-ray. Diagnosis: positive urine sodium nitroprusside test, urine chromatography for cystine. Treatment: increased fluid intake (3–4 L/day), alkalinize urine (bicarbonate, potassium citrate), penicillamine/α-mercaptopropionylglycine or captopril (form soluble complex with cystine); SWL NOT effective.

Cystine stones are the only stone type caused by a hereditary amino acid transport defect. The COLA mnemonic (Cystine, Ornithine, Lysine, Arginine) covers all dibasic amino acids affected. SWL failure is clinically important — these hard stones require ureteroscopy or PCNL. The nitroprusside test (turns purple with cystine) is a bedside diagnostic tool.

**Q:** What are the indications for urgent intervention in renal stone disease?

**A:** Urgent intervention is required for renal stones in the following situations: (1) Solitary kidney with obstruction; (2) Bilateral obstructing stones; (3) Renal failure from obstruction; (4) Sepsis with obstruction (urgent decompression via ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy — definitive stone treatment delayed until sepsis resolves). Hospitalization is indicated if: pain cannot be controlled, unable to tolerate fluids/medications, persistent vomiting, high-grade obstruction, solitary kidney, comorbidities affecting stone passage.

Sepsis + obstructing stone is the most critical emergency — decompression takes precedence over stone removal. A ureteric stent (via cystoscopy) is the first-line decompression method; percutaneous nephrostomy is second-line. Definitive stone treatment (SWL, ureteroscopy, PCNL) is delayed until the sepsis resolves. This principle is frequently tested on the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What are the indications for percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL)?

**A:** Indications for PCNL: (1) Stone size >2 cm; (2) Staghorn calculus; (3) UPJ obstruction with concurrent stone requiring correction; (4) Large cystine stones (poorly fragmented with SWL); (5) Anatomical abnormalities preventing retrograde access; (6) Failure of less invasive modalities (SWL, ureteroscopy).

The treatment hierarchy for renal stones: medical management → ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, less invasive) → ureteroscopic laser lithotripsy (more invasive, higher success) → PCNL (requires admission, general/spinal anesthesia, for large stones) → laparoscopic/open surgery (rare, only if concomitant procedure needed). PCNL is definitive for stones >2 cm.

**Q:** What is the evidence for α-blockers as medical expulsive therapy (MET) for ureteral stones?

**A:** Per Cochrane meta-analysis (2018) of 67 RCTs (10,509 adult patients): α-blockers resulted in increased stone clearance compared to standard therapy (RR 1.45, 95% CI: 1.36–1.55; low-quality evidence). Subgroup analysis suggests α-blockers may be less effective for smaller stones (<5 mm). Slight increase in major adverse events noted. Conclusion: α-blockers likely increase stone clearance but also slightly increase risk of major adverse events; benefit most apparent for stones 5–10 mm.

Medical expulsive therapy (MET) with α-blockers (tamsulosin) is used for distal ureteral stones 5–10 mm. The rationale is that α1-receptors in the distal ureter promote smooth muscle relaxation, facilitating stone passage. Stones <5 mm have a 75% spontaneous passage rate and may not benefit significantly from MET. Stones >10 mm require procedural intervention.

**Q:** What investigations are performed for renal stones and when is each test indicated?

**A:** All patients: CBC, U/A, urine C&S. KUB x-ray: first episode renal colic (shows 90% radiopaque stones; good for follow-up; cannot see uric acid stones; do not confuse phleboliths for stones). Ultrasound: pregnant patients, recurrent stone formers, pediatric cases, uncertain diagnosis (avoids radiation, 90% sensitivity, can assess hydronephrosis, no radiation). CT (non-contrast): gold standard diagnostic test; sees adjacent organs, exact stone location, can plan surgery; can assess stone density; concern: radiation (especially young females). Cystoscopy: suspected bladder stones, pediatric cases, stone not seen on KUB. Urine pH: suspected uric acid stone (pH <5.5). PTH + 24-hour urine ×2 (for volume, Cr, Ca, Na+, PO4, Mg, oxalate, citrate, cystine): recurrent calcium stone formers — to rule out metabolic cause.

CT non-contrast is the gold standard for acute renal colic (sensitivity >95%). The 24-hour urine collection is done twice for reproducibility and guides long-term prevention strategy. PTH is checked to rule out primary hyperparathyroidism as a cause of hypercalciuria. Ultrasound is preferred in pregnancy to avoid radiation.

**Q:** What are the stone pathogenesis mechanisms and inhibitory factors?

**A:** Stone pathogenesis requires: (1) Supersaturation of stone constituents (at appropriate temperature and pH); (2) Stasis, low flow, and low urine volume (dehydration); (3) Crystal formation and stone nidus; (4) Loss of inhibitory factors. Inhibitory factors preventing stone formation: Citrate (forms soluble complex with calcium), Magnesium (forms soluble complex with oxalate), Pyrophosphate, Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein.

Understanding why certain patients form stones despite normal serum levels requires recognizing urinary inhibitor deficiency. Hypocitraturia is common in stone formers and treated with potassium citrate supplementation. Citric acid intake (lemon juice, orange juice) is a dietary source. This mechanistic understanding explains why potassium citrate prevents both calcium oxalate and uric acid stones.

**Q:** What is reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome) and how does it relate to urethritis?

**A:** Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome) is a triad of urethritis, uveitis (or conjunctivitis), and arthritis — remembered by the mnemonic 'can't pee, can't see, can't climb a tree.' It is triggered by Chlamydia trachomatis urethritis (or GI infections). It represents an immune-mediated reaction to infection, not direct bacterial invasion of the joints/eyes.

Reactive arthritis is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy associated with HLA-B27. On the MCCQE1, a young male with urethritis + joint pain + eye redness should prompt consideration of reactive arthritis. Chlamydia is the most common genitourinary trigger; Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, and Yersinia are common GI triggers.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for UTI development?

**A:** UTI risk factors: (1) Stasis and obstruction — residual urine from impaired flow (PUVs, reflux, medications, BPH, urethral stricture, cystocele, neurogenic bladder); (2) Foreign body — catheter or instrumentation introduces pathogens or acts as nidus; (3) Decreased host resistance — DM, malignancy, immunosuppression, spermicide use, estrogen depletion, prior antimicrobial use; (4) Other — trauma, anatomic abnormalities, female sex, sexual activity, menopause, fecal incontinence.

Spermicide use disrupts vaginal flora (kills Lactobacillus), predisposing to E. coli colonization. Estrogen depletion in post-menopausal women causes urogenital atrophy and reduced Lactobacillus — vaginal estrogen is recommended for recurrent UTIs in these women. Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) is the most common hospital-acquired infection.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for acute pyelonephritis based on clinical severity?

**A:** Hemodynamically stable: outpatient oral antibiotics plus single initial IV dose (ciprofloxacin ± ceftriaxone 1g IV or ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV × 1). Severe or non-resolving: admit, hydrate, IV antibiotics (fluoroquinolone, gentamicin + ampicillin, extended-spectrum cephalosporin, extended-spectrum penicillin, or carbapenem). Emphysematous pyelonephritis: most patients require nephrectomy after IV antibiotics started and patient stabilized (consider temporization with nephrostomy tubes). Renal obstruction: admit for emergent stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy tube. Suspected/confirmed Enterococcus: requires ampicillin.

The step-down approach (IV → oral) is standard once clinical improvement occurs (usually 48–72 hours). Total duration is 10–14 days. The key decision point is whether to admit — fever + poor oral intake + inability to tolerate fluids = inpatient. Emphysematous pyelonephritis is a gas-forming infection usually in diabetics with a mortality of >50% if managed conservatively.

**Q:** What dietary modifications prevent kidney stone recurrence?

**A:** Universal dietary changes for all stone types: increase fluid intake (>2 L/day). Calcium oxalate stones: ↑ citrate (lemon/orange juice), ↓ animal protein, ↓ oxalate-rich foods, ↓ Na+, ↓ sucrose/fructose, avoid high-dose vitamin C; do NOT reduce dietary calcium (increases GI oxalate absorption). Uric acid stones: ↓ purine-rich foods (red meats), alkalinize urine to pH 6.5–7. Cystine stones: ↑ fluids to 3–4 L/day, alkalinize urine. General: weight loss if BMI >30, avoid living/working in extreme heat.

The anti-intuitive calcium advice is the most frequently tested dietary prevention pearl. Normal dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut, preventing absorption — so restricting calcium paradoxically worsens oxaluria. Sodium restriction is important because high Na+ increases urinary calcium excretion. Oxalate restriction is secondary to fluid/sodium management.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) in children, and what is the rate of spontaneous resolution?

**A:** Primary VUR is caused by a congenitally short intravesical ureter. Spontaneous resolution occurs in approximately 60% of cases, particularly in lower grades (I-II).

VUR is graded I-V. Lower grades (I-II) are managed medically with daily antibiotic prophylaxis (TMP/SMX, trimethoprim, amoxicillin, or nitrofurantoin at half the treatment dose). The RIVUR trial showed prophylaxis reduces recurrent UTIs but does not reduce renal scarring risk. Siblings and family members may be screened (though controversial).

**Q:** What are the surgical indications for treatment of vesicoureteral reflux (VUR)?

**A:** Indications include: failure of medical management, renal scarring (renal insufficiency, HTN), breakthrough UTIs, and persistent high-grade (IV or V) reflux.

Surgical options include ureteral reimplantation with ureteroplasty, or subureteric injection with bulking agents (Deflux® or Macroplastique®). Medical treatment (daily antibiotic prophylaxis) is tried first for lower grades. Surgery addresses both the anatomical defect and complications like renal damage.

**Q:** Define hypospadias and state the recommended timing of surgical correction.

**A:** Hypospadias is a condition where the urethral meatus opens on the ventral (underside) of the penis, proximal to the normal location in the glans. Optimal surgical repair is before age 2 years.

Hypospadias occurs in 1 in 300 live male births; distal types are more common than proximal. It may be associated with ventral penile curvature (chordee), disorders of sexual differentiation, undescended testicles, or inguinal hernia. Neonatal circumcision should be deferred as foreskin tissue may be needed for surgical reconstruction.

**Q:** What is the exstrophy-epispadias complex and how does severity range?

**A:** A spectrum of defects from rupture of the cloacal membrane: epispadias (least severe — urethra opens dorsally), bladder exstrophy (exposed bladder lumen through abdominal wall defect), and cloacal exstrophy (most severe — exposed bladder and bowel with imperforate anus, associated with spina bifida >50%).

The incidence is rare: 1 in 30,000, with M:F = 3:1. It represents failure of closure of the cloacal membrane. Surgical correction is performed at birth; later corrections may be needed for incontinence, VUR, and low bladder capacity. Morbidity is high with multiple reconstructive surgeries required.

**Q:** What is Wilms' tumour (nephroblastoma) and what are its key clinical features?

**A:** Wilms' tumour is an embryonal tumour arising from abnormal proliferation of metanephric blastema. Key features: large firm unilateral abdominal mass (80%), flank pain (30-40%), microscopic hematuria (12-25%), nausea/vomiting. Average age of incidence is 3.5 years.

It is the most common primary malignant renal tumour of childhood, accounting for 5-10% of childhood cancers. It is bilateral in 5% and associated with congenital malformation syndromes (WAGR — Wilms', aniridia, genital anomaly, retardation; Beckwith-Wiedemann; Denys-Drash). Treatment: radical or nephron-sparing nephrectomy + radiation + chemotherapy. 5-year survival is ~90%.

**Q:** Which syndromes are associated with Wilms' tumour?

**A:** WAGR syndrome (Wilms', aniridia, genital anomaly, intellectual disability/retardation), Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, and Denys-Drash syndrome.

Recognition of these associated syndromes prompts earlier surveillance for Wilms' tumour. Bilateral Wilms' tumour occurs in ~5% of cases. The contralateral kidney and renal vein (for tumour thrombus) must always be evaluated before treatment. Bilateral disease is treated with nephron-sparing surgery after neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

**Q:** What is cryptorchidism and what are the long-term complications of untreated bilateral cryptorchidism?

**A:** Cryptorchidism is the abnormal location of a testis somewhere along the normal path of descent (inguinal canal, abdominal). Untreated bilateral cryptorchidism leads to ~100% infertility (due to Leydig and germ cell loss) and increased malignancy risk (intra-abdominal > inguinal location).

Prevalence is 1-4.6% in full-term newborns; most descend within 4 months of life. Denis Browne pouch (between external oblique fascia and Scarpa's fascia) is the most common ectopic location. Treatment is orchiopexy. Paternity rates in formerly bilateral cryptorchid men are 33-65% vs. 93% in normal men. Surgery also facilitates testicular monitoring and may reduce malignancy risk.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of disorders of sexual differentiation (DSD) in 46,XX individuals?

**A:** Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), most commonly due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency, is the most common cause of 46,XX DSD. It creates a shunt in the steroid biosynthetic pathway leading to excess androgens.

Undiagnosed and untreated CAH can cause life-threatening electrolyte abnormalities (salt-wasting CAH) in newborns. Bilateral non-palpable testicles in apparent males should be considered 46,XX with salt-wasting CAH until proven otherwise. Laboratory tests include plasma 17-OH-progesterone (elevated in CAH) and serum electrolytes. Treatment includes steroid supplementation.

**Q:** What key laboratory test confirms the diagnosis of 21-hydroxylase deficiency (most common cause of CAH)?

**A:** Elevated plasma 17-OH-progesterone (measured after 36 hours of life) confirms 21-hydroxylase deficiency.

Other labs: elevated plasma 11-deoxycortisol indicates 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency; serum testosterone and DHT pre- and post-hCG stimulation assess testicular function; serum electrolytes assess for salt-wasting. Chromosomal evaluation (karyotype) and ultrasound of adrenals, gonads, uterus, and fallopian tubes are also part of the workup. Management includes sex assignment after extensive consultation and reconstruction between 6-12 months.

**Q:** What is the French (Fr) scale used for in urological catheterization, and what is the standard catheter size?

**A:** The French scale measures catheter circumference in mm (30 Fr = 1 cm diameter). Each 1 mm increase in diameter equals approximately 3 Fr. Standard catheter size is 14-18 Fr.

A two-way Foley catheter is standard for indwelling use; a three-way Foley has an irrigation port and is used for continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) to prevent clot formation (22-24 Fr recommended). Coudé tip catheters are used for difficult catheterization due to BPH (angled tip navigates around enlarged prostate — always angle up/anteriorly).

**Q:** What are the medical indications and contraindications for neonatal circumcision per the Canadian Paediatric Society?

**A:** Indications: pathological phimosis, recurrent paraphimosis, recurrent UTIs (especially in infants with urinary abnormalities), balanitis xerotica obliterans. Contraindications: unstable/sick infant, congenital genital abnormalities (hypospadias, epispadias, penoscrotal webbing, concealed penis), family history of bleeding disorders.

The Canadian Paediatric Society (2015, reaffirmed 2018) does not recommend routine circumcision of every newborn male. The procedure reduces HIV acquisition in heterosexual men by 38-66% (per WHO meta-analysis), but this does not justify universal neonatal circumcision in Canada. Complication rate is 0.6-2%, including early risks (bleeding, infection, glans injury) and late risks (adhesions, meatal stenosis, phimosis).

**Q:** What is post-TURP (transurethral resection syndrome) and how is it treated?

**A:** Post-TURP syndrome is caused by absorption of large volumes of hypotonic irrigation solution (glycine) through perforated venous sinusoids, causing hypervolemic hyponatremia. Features: dilutional hyponatremia, confusion, nausea, vomiting, HTN, bradycardia, visual disturbances, CHF, and pulmonary edema. Treatment: diuresis and (if severe) hypertonic saline administration.

This complication does NOT occur with bipolar TURP because normal saline is used as irrigation instead of glycine. TURP is not a cancer operation — it relieves obstruction by resecting the periurethral transition zone. Chronic complications include retrograde ejaculation (>75%), erectile dysfunction (5-10%), urethral stricture, and bladder neck contracture. Incontinence after TURP is <1%.

**Q:** What are the vasectomy post-procedure criteria for abandoning contraception?

**A:** Contraception can be abandoned if: azoospermia is confirmed OR fewer than 100,000 immotile spermatozoa are found on semen analysis at 3-4 months post-vasectomy.

Post-vasectomy semen analyses should be performed at approximately 3 and 4 months. If >100,000 immotile sperm or any motile sperm are present on repeat analysis 4-8 weeks after initial analysis, a redo vasectomy is indicated. Risk of pregnancy after vasectomy is ~0.1%. No-scalpel vasectomy has lower early complication rates. Complications include chronic scrotal pain (1-14%), sperm granuloma, and congestive epididymitis.

**Q:** What are the indications for cystoscopy?

**A:** Indications include: hematuria, lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS — storage or voiding), urethral and bladder neck strictures, bladder stones, bladder tumour surveillance, and evaluation of upper tracts with retrograde pyelography.

Cystoscopy provides endoscopic inspection of the lower urinary tract (urethra, prostate, bladder, ureteral orifices). It is done under local anesthesia without NPO or routine antibiotics; antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended only for high-risk or immunosuppressed patients. Complications include infection, urinary retention (short-term), and stricture (long-term). Scopes can be flexible or rigid.

**Q:** What are the complications of radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer?

**A:** Late complications: moderate-to-severe stress urinary incontinence (3-10%), mild stress urinary incontinence (20-30%), erectile dysfunction (30-50%, depending on whether neurovascular bundles are preserved). Perioperative: lymphocele (if lymphadenectomy), blood loss, urine leak from anastomosis.

After radical prostatectomy there is no ejaculate because the seminal vesicles are removed. Intraoperative complications are rare but include rectal, ureteral, or obturator nerve injury. A Cochrane review (2017) showed laparoscopic (LRP) and robotic-assisted (RARP) prostatectomy have similar urinary and sexual quality of life outcomes compared to open surgery, with shorter hospital stays.

**Q:** What are the phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) inhibitors used for erectile dysfunction, and what are their key contraindications?

**A:** PDE5 inhibitors: sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil. Mechanism: inhibit PDE5 (which degrades cGMP), leading to sinusoidal smooth muscle relaxation and increased blood flow. Key contraindications: concurrent nitrates (severe hypotension risk), history of priapism, or conditions predisposing to priapism (leukemia, sickle cell disease, myelofibrosis, polycythemia).

Adverse effects include flushing, headaches, and dyspepsia. Alternative agents for ED include alprostadil (PGE1 — urethral suppository MUSE or intracavernosal injection), papaverine, and phentolamine. Intracavernosal injections can cause Peyronie's plaque (thickening of tunica albuginea) with repeated injections. Triple therapy (papaverine + phentolamine + PGE1) is also used.

**Q:** What is the mechanism and key adverse effect unique to tamsulosin/alfuzosin/silodosin compared to non-selective alpha-blockers for BPH?

**A:** Tamsulosin, alfuzosin, and silodosin are selective alpha-1A adrenergic antagonists. Their key unique adverse effect is intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) if cataract surgery is performed. Non-selective alpha-blockers (terazosin, doxazosin) more commonly cause orthostatic presyncope and leg edema.

All alpha-blockers reduce the dynamic component of bladder outlet obstruction by reducing stromal smooth muscle tone. Common class effects include retrograde ejaculation, headache, asthenia, and nasal congestion. Patients planning cataract surgery should inform their ophthalmologist of alpha-blocker use. Finasteride/dutasteride (5-alpha reductase inhibitors) address the static component by reducing prostatic volume and PSA levels.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of GnRH agonists (leuprolide, goserelin) in prostate cancer treatment, and what is the 'flare' phenomenon?

**A:** GnRH agonists initially stimulate LH, causing a transient increase in testosterone ('flare'), which can worsen bone pain if bone metastases are present. With continued use, they cause downregulation of GnRH receptors, leading to sustained low testosterone levels (castration levels).

To prevent the flare, a non-steroidal antiandrogen (e.g., bicalutamide) is often given for the first 2-4 weeks. Common adverse effects of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT): hot flashes, headache, decreased libido, gynecomastia, osteoporosis. Abiraterone inhibits CYP17, blocking androgen synthesis in tumour, testes, and adrenal glands — requires concurrent steroids to prevent adrenal insufficiency.

**Q:** What is mirabegron's mechanism of action for overactive bladder, and how does it differ from anticholinergic agents?

**A:** Mirabegron is a beta-3 adrenergic agonist. It causes detrusor relaxation (anticholinergic-like effect) and urinary sphincter contraction (sympathomimetic effect). Unlike anticholinergics, it does not cause dry mouth, blurred vision, or constipation, but blood pressure should be monitored.

Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, darifenacin, trospium, fesoterodine, propiverine) inhibit acetylcholine on smooth muscle, decreasing uninhibited detrusor contractions. All anticholinergics are equally effective; long-acting formulations are better tolerated. Botulinum toxin A injections are used for refractory overactive bladder (both neurogenic and non-neurogenic) — risks include urinary retention and UTI.

**Q:** What did the MTOPS trial demonstrate about combination therapy for BPH?

**A:** The MTOPS trial (NEJM 2003) demonstrated that combination therapy with doxazosin (alpha-blocker) and finasteride (5-alpha reductase inhibitor) reduced clinical progression of BPH significantly more than either therapy alone (66% risk reduction vs. 39% for doxazosin alone and 34% for finasteride alone).

Clinical progression was defined as ≥4-point increase in AUA symptoms score, acute urinary retention, urinary incontinence, renal insufficiency, or recurrent UTI. Mean follow-up was 4.5 years. Combination therapy also reduced the need for invasive therapy long-term. This trial supports the current practice of combining alpha-blockers with 5-alpha reductase inhibitors in men with moderate-to-severe BPH symptoms.

**Q:** What did the CHAARTED trial demonstrate for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer?

**A:** The CHAARTED trial (NEJM 2015) showed that combining docetaxel with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) resulted in 13.6 months longer median overall survival compared to ADT alone, in men with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.

Methods: Patients randomized to ADT + docetaxel vs. ADT alone. Results: median overall survival 13.6 months longer with combination; median time to progression 20.2 vs. 11.7 months. This established docetaxel plus ADT as a standard of care for high-volume metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer. The ERSPC trial (updated 2014) showed PSA screening reduces prostate cancer mortality by ~21% at 13 years but is associated with high rates of overdiagnosis.

**Q:** What is the PCPT trial finding regarding finasteride and prostate cancer?

**A:** PCPT (NEJM 2003): Finasteride reduced prostate cancer prevalence by 24.8% over 7 years compared to placebo. However, there was a significant increase in high-grade (Gleason high) disease in the finasteride group (6.4% vs. 5.1%). Long-term follow-up (2013) attributed the high-grade increase to better detection, not true increase in risk.

18,882 men ≥55 years with normal DRE and PSA ≤3.0 ng/mL were randomized to finasteride 5 mg/day or placebo for 7 years. This was the first study to show a medication reduces prostate cancer likelihood. The reduction was primarily in low-grade cancers. Finasteride reduces PSA by approximately 50% — this must be accounted for when monitoring PSA in men on finasteride.

**Q:** What is bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD) in children and what are the treatment principles?

**A:** BBD describes voiding and defecation symptoms without a neurogenic or anatomic cause. Storage symptoms: urgency, frequency, urge incontinence. Voiding symptoms: hesitancy, slow flow, intermittency. GI symptoms: constipation and encopresis. Treatment: stool softeners (polyethylene glycol 3350), urotherapy and bladder retraining, pelvic floor physiotherapy, anticholinergics (solifenacin, propiverine, tolterodine), neuromodulation via transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation.

BBD is important to recognize and treat because it is associated with vesicoureteral reflux and recurrent UTIs. The RIVUR trial studied children with VUR and found antibiotic prophylaxis reduced UTI recurrence but not renal scarring. Constipation management (with osmotic laxatives) is a key component because it directly affects bladder function through shared neural pathways and pelvic floor mechanics.

**Q:** What is the RIVUR trial conclusion regarding antibiotic prophylaxis for children with vesicoureteral reflux?

**A:** The RIVUR trial (2-year multicenter placebo-controlled RCT, n=607) concluded that antibiotic prophylaxis (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) reduced the risk of recurrent UTIs in children with VUR after a first or second symptomatic UTI, particularly when the index infection was febrile and in those with baseline bladder and bowel dysfunction. However, it was NOT associated with a reduced risk of renal scarring.

This evidence supports the use of prophylaxis for recurrence prevention but argues against its use solely for preventing renal scarring. This changes the risk-benefit calculation: if the primary concern is preventing renal scarring, prophylaxis alone may not be sufficient and surgical correction should be considered for high-grade VUR. Surgical correction is indicated for grades IV-V, breakthrough UTIs, and failed medical management.

**Q:** What is gender-affirming penile inversion vaginoplasty and what are its key follow-up requirements?

**A:** Penile inversion vaginoplasty forms a vaginal cavity and vulva (clitoris, urethra, mons, labia) using penile and scrotal skin. Key follow-up: lubrication required for penetration, prostate exams conducted vaginally, regular dilation of vaginal cavity to avoid stenosis. Complications: granulation tissue, urinary symptoms, fistula formation, hair growth in neovagina.

Per WPATH Standards of Care Version 8, some gender-affirming procedures require 1 year of hormone therapy trial and preoperative evaluation letters from mental health professionals. Orchiectomy eliminates the need for testosterone blockers. Phalloplasty (radial forearm or anterolateral thigh flap) has high complication rates related to urethral connections (fistula, stricture, retention). Metoidioplasty (release of hormonally-enlarged clitoris) has lower complications but is not capable of penetrative intercourse.

## Urology Hematuria

**Q:** What does gross, painless hematuria in an adult signify until proven otherwise?

**A:** Bladder cancer (urothelial carcinoma) until proven otherwise.

Painless gross hematuria is the classic presentation of bladder carcinoma. All adults with this finding require cystoscopy and upper tract imaging (CTU) as part of a malignancy workup per CUA guidelines.

**Q:** What is the definition of microscopic hematuria?

**A:** >2 RBCs per high power field (HPF) on urinalysis on at least two separate samples.

Microscopic hematuria is not visible to the naked eye. The CUA threshold is >2 RBCs/HPF confirmed on two separate samples before initiating a full workup, distinguishing it from transient causes (e.g., exercise, menstruation).

**Q:** What is the preferred imaging modality for evaluating the upper urinary tract in hematuria workup?

**A:** CT urography (CTU) — the standard test to evaluate the renal parenchyma and collecting system.

CTU provides superior evaluation of the renal parenchyma, collecting system, and ureters compared to IVP or ultrasound alone. It involves IV contrast and radiation exposure. MR urography is an alternative when ionizing radiation must be avoided (e.g., pediatric, pregnant patients).

**Q:** A 65-year-old male presents with gross hematuria. List the most common etiologies in this age group.

**A:** BPH, bladder tumour, UTI, RCC, prostate cancer (males >60).

In patients >60, BPH and urothelial (bladder) tumours are the leading urological causes of hematuria in men. All require cystoscopy and upper tract imaging to exclude malignancy.

**Q:** How is severe bladder hemorrhage with clots managed acutely?

**A:** 1. Manual irrigation via catheter with normal saline to remove clots. 2. Continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) using a large (20–24 French) 3-way Foley catheter. 3. Cystoscopy to identify and coagulate bleeding sources or resect tumours.

Clot retention requires physical clot removal before CBI is effective. Cystoscopy allows direct visualization and treatment of the bleeding source under general or regional anesthesia.

**Q:** Where in the urinary stream does hematuria occur, and what does the timing suggest anatomically?

**A:** Beginning of stream: anterior urethra. End of stream: bladder neck or prostatic urethra. Throughout entire stream: bladder and above (ureter, kidney).

The timing of hematuria within the urinary stream helps localize the bleeding source. Presence of blood clots suggests significant bleeding. Associated pyuria and dysuria suggest infection; flank pain radiating to groin suggests ureteral stone.

**Q:** What are risk factors for malignancy-related hematuria?

**A:** Smoking, chemical exposures (aniline dyes, benzene), history of cyclophosphamide therapy, pelvic radiation.

These risk factors are associated with urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma of the bladder and upper urinary tract. Cyclophosphamide causes hemorrhagic cystitis and increases bladder cancer risk. Smoking is the single most important modifiable risk factor.

## Urology Lower Urinary Tract

**Q:** What are the two phases of lower urinary tract function and their requirements?

**A:** 1. Storage phase: requires bladder accommodation/compliance, no involuntary contractions. 2. Voiding phase: requires coordinated detrusor contraction, synchronous sphincter relaxation, and no anatomic obstruction.

Lower urinary tract dysfunction is classified as failure to store (bladder or outlet problem) or failure to void (bladder or outlet problem). This framework guides diagnosis and management of incontinence, retention, and LUTS.

**Q:** What are the storage (irritative) LUTS symptoms? Use the mnemonic.

**A:** FUN: Frequency, Urgency (rush to toilet), Nocturia.

Storage symptoms result from failure of the bladder to store urine normally. They may indicate overactive bladder, UTI, bladder tumour, or interstitial cystitis, among other conditions.

**Q:** What are the voiding (obstructive) LUTS symptoms? Use the mnemonic.

**A:** SHED: Stream changes/straining, Hesitancy, incomplete Emptying, post-void Dribbling.

Voiding symptoms reflect bladder outlet obstruction (e.g., BPH, urethral stricture) or impaired detrusor contractility. The IPSS/AUA-SI questionnaire quantifies symptom severity.

**Q:** What is acute urinary retention and how is it managed?

**A:** Acute retention is a medical emergency: suprapubic pain, inability to void, palpable/percussible bladder. Management: immediate catheterization (urethral Foley), monitor fluid status and electrolytes (risk of post-obstructive diuresis), investigate and treat the underlying cause.

Unlike chronic retention (which can be painless with greatly increased bladder volume), acute retention is painful. Chronic retention can cause detrusor hypertrophy followed by atony. Patients with elevated creatinine are at risk for post-obstructive diuresis after catheterization.

**Q:** What is urethral stricture, and what are its most common causes?

**A:** Urethral stricture is a decrease in urethral caliber due to scar formation. Most common causes: iatrogenic (instrumentation, catheterization, TURP, radical prostatectomy, radiation), idiopathic, trauma (straddle injury, pelvic fracture), STI (gonorrhea, chlamydia), inflammation (BXO/lichen sclerosis).

Urethral stricture causes obstructive voiding symptoms. It is more common in males (M>F). Flow rates <10 mL/s on uroflowmetry and RUG/VCUG demonstrate the location and length of the stricture.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for urethral stricture, from least to most invasive?

**A:** 1. Urethral dilation: temporary, high recurrence. 2. Visual internal urethrotomy (VIU): endoscopic incision, equal to dilation for mid-bulbar strictures <2 cm, 30–80% recurrence. 3. Open urethroplasty: stricture excision with anastomosis or graft (e.g., buccal mucosa), highest long-term success.

VIU should be avoided in younger patients given high recurrence rates. Urethroplasty (open reconstruction) has significantly better long-term patency, especially for longer or recurrent strictures. Urethrostomy is an alternative in select patients.

**Q:** What is the role of the sympathetic nervous system in bladder storage?

**A:** During storage: sympathetic (T10–L2) via hypogastric nerve releases norepinephrine. Alpha-1 receptors cause internal urethral sphincter contraction. Beta-3 receptors cause detrusor relaxation. This keeps the bladder relaxed and outlet closed.

The storage phase requires sympathetic dominance: detrusor muscle relaxes (beta-3) while the internal sphincter contracts (alpha-1). This is why alpha-1 blockers reduce urethral resistance in BPH, and why beta-3 agonists (mirabegron) relax the detrusor in OAB.

**Q:** What is the role of the parasympathetic nervous system in bladder voiding?

**A:** During voiding: parasympathetic (S2–S4) via pelvic nerve releases ACh → muscarinic receptors → detrusor contraction. Simultaneously, internal and external sphincters relax (sympathetic and somatic inhibition).

The voiding phase requires coordinated parasympathetic activation of the detrusor with simultaneous relaxation of both sphincters. Anticholinergic medications block muscarinic receptors to suppress detrusor overactivity in OAB and urgency incontinence.

**Q:** What is post-obstructive diuresis (POD) and in which patients is it a risk?

**A:** POD is a large diuresis following relief of urinary obstruction. Risk is highest in patients with elevated creatinine (impaired renal function) at the time of catheterization.

POD results from osmotic diuresis (retained urea), impaired tubular reabsorption, and natriuretic factor accumulation. It can cause severe electrolyte abnormalities and volume depletion. Fluid status and electrolytes must be closely monitored after catheterization in these patients.

**Q:** What is balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO) and how does it cause urethral stricture?

**A:** BXO (lichen sclerosis/chronic progressive sclerosing dermatosis of male genitalia) causes meatal and urethral stenosis through chronic inflammatory scarring of the penile skin and urethra.

BXO is the most common cause of meatal stenosis and distal urethral stricture in men. It presents as white, atrophic plaques on the glans and prepuce. Buccal mucosa graft urethroplasty is the preferred surgical treatment as BXO affects genital skin, precluding its use for grafting.

## Urology Incontinence

**Q:** What are the four types of urinary incontinence and their definitions?

**A:** 1. Stress: leakage with increased intra-abdominal pressure (cough, sneeze, exertion). 2. Urgency: leakage preceded by a strong, sudden urge to void. 3. Mixed: features of both stress and urgency. 4. Overflow: leakage associated with urinary retention.

Understanding the type of incontinence guides management: stress incontinence is treated with pelvic floor exercises/slings; urgency with anticholinergics/beta-3 agonists; overflow by treating the underlying cause and catheterization.

**Q:** What is the etiology of stress urinary incontinence?

**A:** Sphincter incompetence and/or urethral hypermobility. Common in middle-aged and older women, and men following prostate cancer treatment.

Stress urinary incontinence results from inadequate urethral closure mechanism during increases in intra-abdominal pressure. In men, it is most commonly iatrogenic following radical prostatectomy.

**Q:** What is urgency, and is it the same as urgency incontinence?

**A:** Urgency is a compelling desire to void that is difficult to defer. It is NOT the same as urgency incontinence — urgency can occur without actual leakage.

This distinction is clinically important: urgency alone (overactive bladder without incontinence) may respond to behavioral measures alone, whereas urgency incontinence typically requires pharmacological treatment (anticholinergics, beta-3 agonists).

**Q:** What is the mnemonic DIAPERS and when is it used?

**A:** DIAPERS = causes of transient incontinence in the elderly: Delirium, Infection (UTI), Atrophic vaginitis/urethritis, Pharmaceuticals/Psychological, Excess UI, Restricted mobility/Retention, Stool impaction.

Many elderly patients have reversible incontinence from non-urological causes. Identifying and treating these causes first can resolve incontinence before considering urological interventions.

## Urology Bph

**Q:** What are the absolute indications for surgical intervention in BPH?

**A:** RRHAMS: Renal failure with obstructive uropathy, Refractory urinary retention, Hematuria refractory to medical treatment, Absolute urgency, Malignancy (rule out other causes), Severe symptoms unresponsive to medical therapies.

When these absolute indications are present, conservative or medical management is insufficient and surgical correction (e.g., TURP, laser prostatectomy) is required regardless of symptom severity score.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and etiology of BPH?

**A:** Proliferation of epithelial, connective, and smooth muscle tissue in the prostatic transition zone. Requires DHT (converted from testosterone by 5-alpha reductase). Estrogens, impaired apoptosis, and other growth factors may also play a role.

BPH occurs in the transition zone surrounding the urethra, producing obstructive symptoms. It is extremely age-related: 50% prevalence at age 50, 80% at age 80, with 25% requiring treatment.

**Q:** What medications are used in the treatment of BPH and their mechanisms?

**A:** 1. Alpha-adrenergic antagonists (e.g., tamsulosin): reduce smooth muscle tone at bladder neck, prostate, urethra. 2. 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (e.g., finasteride, dutasteride): block testosterone→DHT conversion, reduce prostate size. 3. Antimuscarinics or beta-3 agonists: for storage LUTS without elevated PVR. 4. PDE5 inhibitors: for ED and both storage/voiding LUTS. 5. Desmopressin: for nocturia (risk of hyponatremia in >65 yr).

Combination therapy with alpha-blocker + 5-ARI is synergistic and superior for men with prostate volume >40 mL and PSA >1.5 ng/mL. Alpha-blockers should be avoided before planned cataract surgery due to intraoperative floppy iris syndrome.

**Q:** What is the IPSS and how are scores interpreted?

**A:** International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) uses FUNWISE: Frequency, Urgency, Nocturia, Weak stream, Intermittency, Slow stream (incomplete emptying). Each graded 0–5 (total 0–35). 0–7 = mild, 8–19 = moderate, 20–35 = severe.

The IPSS (identical to AUA-SI) quantifies LUTS severity and guides treatment decisions. Notably, dysuria is NOT included in the score but is commonly associated with BPH. Scores guide escalation from watchful waiting to medical or surgical therapy.

**Q:** Why should alpha-adrenergic antagonists be avoided before planned cataract surgery in men with BPH?

**A:** Risk of intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS), which can complicate cataract surgery.

Alpha-1 antagonists (especially tamsulosin) cause relaxation of the iris dilator muscle, leading to IFIS characterized by billowing iris, progressive miosis, and iris prolapse during phacoemulsification. Men should not start alpha-blockers until after cataract surgery is completed.

**Q:** What does the BPH transition zone contain, and how does it differ from other prostate zones?

**A:** The transition zone surrounds the urethra and is the site of BPH development. The peripheral zone is the most common site of prostate cancer. The central zone surrounds the ejaculatory ducts.

Understanding prostatic zonal anatomy is essential: BPH occurs in the transition zone (causing obstructive LUTS), while prostate cancer predominantly arises in the peripheral zone (detectable by DRE and biopsy). The ejaculatory zone/central zone is involved in seminal vesicle pathology.

**Q:** What are the complications of untreated BPH?

**A:** Urinary retention, overflow incontinence, hydronephrosis, renal insufficiency, infection (UTI, prostatitis), gross hematuria, bladder stones.

Chronic bladder outlet obstruction from BPH can lead to progressive upper tract damage via hydronephrosis and renal insufficiency — this constitutes an absolute indication for surgical intervention. Bladder stones form from urinary stasis.

**Q:** What is the prostate size that correlates with approximate fruit sizes in BPH?

**A:** 20 cc = chestnut, 25 cc = plum, 50 cc = lemon, 75 cc = orange, 300 cc = grapefruit.

Prostate size (volume) is used to guide BPH treatment: TURP is preferred for moderate sizes, open/robotic simple prostatectomy for >100 cc, and technologies like UroLift are limited to <80 cc. Importantly, prostate size does NOT correlate with symptom severity.

**Q:** What are the minimally invasive surgical therapies (MIST) for BPH and their prostate size limitations?

**A:** TUMT (transurethral microwave therapy), UroLift (<80 cc), Convective water vapour energy ablation (Rezum™), BPKVP bipolar plasma kinetic vaporization (<60 cc), TUIP (<30 cc), Aquablation (<80 cc), Prostatic stent (unfit for surgery), Prostatic artery embolization.

MIST options allow treatment with lower morbidity in patients who wish to avoid or cannot tolerate major surgery. Size thresholds limit their applicability: UroLift preserves ejaculatory function and is favored in younger, sexually active men with smaller glands.

## Urology Trauma

**Q:** In a trauma patient, what findings suggest urethral injury and make urethral catheterization contraindicated?

**A:** Blood at the urethral meatus, scrotal hematoma, and/or high-riding prostate on DRE. Catheterization is contraindicated unless performed by urology.

These findings suggest urethral disruption, usually from pelvic fractures. Blind urethral catheterization risks converting a partial tear to a complete one. A retrograde urethrogram (RUG) should be performed to delineate the injury before any catheterization attempt.

**Q:** What are the classification and common etiologies of renal trauma?

**A:** Classification: Minor (contusions, superficial lacerations/hematomas—90% of blunt trauma) and Major (medullary/collecting system laceration, major vascular injury, shattered kidney). Etiology: 80% blunt (MVC, assault, falls), 20% penetrating (stab, gunshot).

Degree of hematuria does NOT correlate with severity of renal injury. CT with contrast (triphasic) is indicated for stable patients. Staging I-V guides management.

**Q:** Describe the staging of renal trauma (Grade I–V).

**A:** Grade I: contusion/hematoma. Grade II: <1 cm laceration, no urinary extravasation. Grade III: >1 cm laceration, no urinary extravasation. Grade IV: laceration causing urinary extravasation and/or main arterial or vein injury with contained hematoma. Grade V: shattered kidney or avulsion of pedicle.

Staging guides management but does not always correlate with clinical status. The majority of renal trauma is now managed conservatively/non-operatively. Absolute surgical indications: hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability. Relative: non-viable tissue, urinary extravasation, vascular injury, expanding perirenal mass.

**Q:** What are the absolute and relative surgical indications for renal trauma?

**A:** Absolute: hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability. Relative: non-viable tissue, major laceration, urinary extravasation, vascular injury, expanding or pulsating perirenal mass.

The trend is toward non-operative management of renal trauma using angiography and embolization when possible. Microscopic hematuria with isolated minor injuries can be managed without hospitalization. Gross hematuria with contusion requires hospitalization and bedrest.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of bladder trauma, and how does it present?

**A:** Blunt trauma (MVC, falls, crush injury); associated with pelvic fracture in 97% of blunt bladder trauma cases. Presentation: gross hematuria (90%), abdominal tenderness, distention, peritonitis, inability to void, suprapubic pain.

Bladder trauma types: contusion (no extravasation), intraperitoneal rupture (bladder dome, requires surgical repair), extraperitoneal rupture (anterior/lateral wall, typically managed non-operatively with Foley catheter). Mortality ~20%, usually from associated injuries.

**Q:** How is intraperitoneal bladder rupture managed differently from extraperitoneal rupture?

**A:** Intraperitoneal rupture: usually requires surgical repair and suprapubic catheterization. Extraperitoneal rupture: typically non-operative with Foley catheter insertion followed by cystogram; surgery if infected urine, rectal/vaginal perforation, bony spike, bladder neck involvement, or failed conservative management.

Intraperitoneal rupture often involves the bladder dome and urine leaks freely into the peritoneum causing peritonitis. CT cystogram (including post-drainage films) is the key diagnostic investigation.

**Q:** What are the classic clinical features of urethral injury?

**A:** Blood at urethral meatus, high-riding prostate on DRE, butterfly perineal hematoma, penile/scrotal hematoma, sensation of voiding without urinary output, distended bladder.

All patients with suspected urethral injury should undergo retrograde urethrogram (RUG) before catheterization. Posterior urethral injury (membranous-prostatic junction) is associated with pelvic fractures/MVCs. Anterior urethral injury (bulbar) occurs with straddle injury.

**Q:** What is the initial investigation and management principle for suspected urethral injury?

**A:** Initial investigation: retrograde urethrogram (RUG) or cystoscopy BEFORE attempting catheterization. Management: simple contusions (no treatment), partial disruption (gentle catheterization by urologist or suprapubic cystostomy), complete disruption (immediate repair if stable; suprapubic tube if unstable, delayed repair).

Blind catheterization risks converting partial to complete disruption. The most common complication is urethral stricture. Posterior urethral injury from pelvic fracture has the classic triad: blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, inability to void.

## Urology Neurogenic Bladder

**Q:** What is neurogenic bladder and what are the main categories of neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction?

**A:** Neurogenic bladder = bladder dysfunction due to innervation deficiency. Categories: 1. Neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) — lesion above PMC (stroke, tumour, MS, Parkinson's). 2. Detrusor sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) — suprasacral spinal cord lesion. 3. Detrusor atony/areflexia — sacral cord or peripheral nerve lesion (DM, disc herniation, trauma). 4. Peripheral autonomic neuropathy (DM).

The level of neurological lesion determines the pattern of dysfunction: suprapontine lesions cause NDO without DSD; suprasacral spinal lesions cause DSD; sacral/peripheral lesions cause flaccid atonic bladder. Treatment is tailored accordingly.

**Q:** What is detrusor sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) and what lesion causes it?

**A:** DSD is loss of coordination between the detrusor and urethral sphincter — detrusor contracts against a closed sphincter (or vice versa). Caused by suprasacral spinal cord lesion (e.g., trauma, MS, arteriovenous malformation, transverse myelitis).

DSD results in high bladder pressures, which can cause hydronephrosis and renal deterioration if untreated. The pontine micturition centre coordinates normal voiding; suprasacral lesions interrupt this coordination while preserving the reflex arc.

**Q:** What are the goals and treatment options for neurogenic bladder?

**A:** Goals: prevent renal deterioration, prevent UTI, achieve social continence. Treatment: CIC (if unable to void), antimuscarinics (bladder hyperactivity), botulinum toxin injection into detrusor (if refractory), augmentation cystoplasty, urinary diversion (severe cases). Flaccid bladder: CIC.

Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) is the cornerstone of management for retention. Botulinum toxin A injected into the detrusor muscle effectively suppresses neurogenic detrusor overactivity for 6–12 months.

**Q:** What is 'spinal shock' and how does it affect the bladder?

**A:** Spinal shock initially manifests as an atonic (flaccid) bladder immediately after acute spinal cord injury, regardless of lesion level.

Spinal shock is a transient state of areflexia that occurs after acute spinal cord injury. The bladder becomes atonic and requires CIC during this phase. As spinal shock resolves (weeks to months), the expected pattern of dysfunction (NDO ± DSD for suprasacral injuries) emerges.

**Q:** What is the role of urodynamic studies in neurogenic bladder evaluation?

**A:** Urodynamics assesses: uroflowmetry (flow rate/pattern), filling cystometrogram (CMG: capacity, compliance, detrusor overactivity), voiding CMG/pressure-flow study (contractility, outlet obstruction), video study (bladder/urethra visualization), EMG (diagnosis of DSD).

Urodynamics is essential in moderate/high-risk neurogenic bladder (spinal cord injury, spina bifida, MS) to detect harmful bladder pressures, guide treatment, and monitor for upper tract deterioration. Renal ultrasound and renal profile are obtained in all these patients.

## Urology Infectious

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis of dysuria?

**A:** Infectious (cystitis, urethritis, prostatitis, epididymitis, cervicitis, vulvovaginitis, TB), Neoplasm (bladder, prostate, penis, vagina/vulva, BPH), Calculi (bladder, urethral, ureteral stone), Inflammatory (reactive arthritis, interstitial cystitis, CPPS, drugs), Hormonal (endometriosis, hypoestrogenism), Trauma (catheter, post-coital cystitis), Psychogenic, Other (foreign body, radiation cystitis, contact sensitivity).

UTI is the most common cause, but non-infectious causes must be considered especially in recurrent or treatment-refractory dysuria. Urethral discharge should be tested for gonorrhea and chlamydia; vaginal discharge warrants wet mount.

## Urology Assessment

**Q:** What urologic history features are assessed using the mnemonic OPQRSTU?

**A:** Onset, Position, Quality, Radiation, Severity, Temporality, déjà vu (history). Applied to urologic symptoms including LUTS (FUN/SHED), hematuria, dysuria, incontinence, and sexual/erectile function.

A thorough urologic history also includes risk factors (prior UTI, stones, STIs, cancers, anatomic abnormalities), medications (anticoagulants, alpha-blockers, anticholinergics), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol), and the psychological impact of symptoms on QoL.

## Urology Erectile Dysfunction

**Q:** What is the significance of new onset erectile dysfunction in the context of vascular disease screening?

**A:** New onset ED may be one of the first symptoms of concomitant vascular disease. It should prompt screening for diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular risk factors.

The penile arteries are small-caliber vessels that manifest atherosclerotic disease earlier than larger coronary or peripheral vessels. ED as a harbinger of cardiovascular disease is well-established; new ED warrants assessment for DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking.

**Q:** What are the autonomic and somatic nerve contributions to erection and ejaculation?

**A:** Erection (POINT): parasympathetics (S2-4) → NO release → increased cGMP → arteriolar dilation and sinusoidal relaxation. Emission (SHOOT): sympathetics (T11-L2) → secretions enter prostatic urethra. Ejaculation (SHOOT): bladder neck closure (sympathetic), bulbocavernosus contraction (somatic S2-4). Detumescence: norepinephrine/endothelin-1 → arteriolar constriction.

PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) prevent breakdown of cGMP, maintaining arteriolar dilation. The mnemonic POINT AND SHOOT is helpful: Parasympathetics for erection, Sympathetics for emission/ejaculation.

**Q:** How do you differentiate psychogenic from organic erectile dysfunction?

**A:** Psychogenic ED: sudden onset, sporadic, varies with partner/circumstance, younger age, nocturnal/morning erections preserved, no organic risk factors. Organic ED: gradual onset, present in all circumstances, no variation, older age, absent nocturnal erections, organic risk factors present (HTN, DM, dyslipidemia).

Preserved nocturnal penile tumescence confirms intact vascular and neurological function, pointing to psychogenic cause. Organic causes can be remembered with IMPOTENCE: Iatrogenic, Mechanical, Psychological, Occlusive, Testosterone deficiency, Neurogenic, Chemical, Endocrine.

**Q:** What are the organic risk factors for erectile dysfunction (mnemonic IMPOTENCE)?

**A:** I - Iatrogenic (pelvic surgery). M - Mechanical (Peyronie's, post-priapism). P - Psychological (depression, stress, PTSD). O - Occlusive (HTN, DM, smoking, hyperlipidemia, PVD). T - Testosterone deficiency. E - Endocrine (COPD, sleep apnea, malnutrition). N - Neurogenic (CNS: Parkinson's, MS, SCI; PNS: DM, peripheral neuropathy). C - Chemical (antihypertensives, SSRIs, antiandrogens, 5-ARIs, statins, GnRH agonists).

ED is often multifactorial. Organic ED is more common than psychogenic. Key medications causing ED include antihypertensives, SSRIs, antiandrogens, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors, and GnRH agonists.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of PDE5 inhibitors, and how do the four agents differ?

**A:** PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil, avanafil) inhibit phosphodiesterase-5, increasing intracavernosal cGMP → sustained arteriolar dilation and erection. Tadalafil has longer half-life, no cyanopsia, and can be taken on empty or full stomach. All four have similar effectiveness; onset of action difference is not clinically significant. Note: avanafil is not available in Canada.

PDE5 inhibitors are first-line oral therapy for organic ED. They are contraindicated with nitrates (severe hypotension). Tadalafil can be taken as needed or as a daily low-dose regimen. These agents work only with sexual stimulation (require NO release).

**Q:** What are the treatment options for erectile dysfunction in order from least to most invasive?

**A:** 1. Non-invasive: lifestyle changes, psychological counseling, change precipitating medications, treat underlying causes. 2. Minimally invasive: oral PDE5 inhibitors, vacuum devices, MUSE (urethral PGE1 suppository), low-intensity shockwave therapy. 3. Invasive: intracavernous vasoactive injection (triple therapy, bimix, or PGE1 alone). 4. Surgical: penile implant (malleable or inflatable).

MUSE (medicated urethral system for erection) delivers alprostadil (PGE1) directly to the urethra. Intracavernous injection complications include priapism (overdose) and fibrosis (Peyronie's-like). Initial trial of MUSE or intracavernosal injection should be done under medical supervision.

## Urology Anatomy

**Q:** What are the anatomy and layers of the scrotum from outside inward?

**A:** Skin → Dartos fascia → External spermatic fascia → Cremaster muscle → Internal spermatic fascia → Tunica vaginalis → Testis.

The scrotal layers correspond to the layers of the abdominal wall (external oblique → external spermatic fascia; internal oblique → cremaster; transversalis fascia → internal spermatic fascia). This anatomy is important for understanding scrotal pathology and surgical approaches.

**Q:** What are the components of the pampiniform plexus and what is its clinical relevance?

**A:** The pampiniform plexus is a network of veins surrounding the testicular artery in the spermatic cord, which functions as a countercurrent heat exchanger to maintain testicular temperature below core body temperature. Dilation forms a varicocele.

The pampiniform plexus cools arterial blood before it reaches the testis. Varicocele (dilation of the plexus) impairs this mechanism, raising testicular temperature and potentially causing infertility. The left varicocele is more common due to the left testicular vein draining at a right angle into the left renal vein.

**Q:** What are the segments of the male urethra from proximal to distal?

**A:** Posterior urethra (prostatic urethra → membranous urethra containing the external sphincter/rhabdosphincter) → Anterior urethra (bulbar urethra → penile/spongy urethra → meatus).

The bulbar urethra is the most common site of urethral stricture from straddle injuries and instrumentation. The membranous urethra (containing the external sphincter) is most vulnerable to injury in pelvic fractures. The spongy urethra runs through the corpus spongiosum.

**Q:** What are the layers of the scrotum from external to internal?

**A:** Skin → Dartos muscle and fascia → Epithelium → Cremasteric fascia → Internal spermatic fascia → Tunica vaginalis.

Knowledge of scrotal layers is essential for understanding spread of infection, hematoma, and surgical anatomy. The tunica vaginalis is the innermost layer, derived from peritoneum.

## Urology Renal Neoplasms

**Q:** What is the Bosniak classification system used for, and what imaging modality is it based on?

**A:** The Bosniak classification stratifies renal cysts for risk of malignancy based on cyst features from contrast CT.

Bosniak Class I and II are simple cysts with near-zero to minimal malignancy risk requiring no follow-up. Class IIF has up to 20% risk and needs imaging follow-up. Class III (>50%) and IV (>90%) require surgical resection.

**Q:** What are the Bosniak class features and risk of malignancy for each class?

**A:** I: simple, no septa/calcification/enhancement, <20 HU — near zero. II: thin septum, fine calcification, no enhancement, <3 cm — minimal. IIF: multiple thin septa, calcifications, no enhancement, >3 cm — <20%. III: irregular thickened calcified septa with enhancement — >50%. IV: irregular thickened septa with enhancement plus enhancing soft-tissue components — >90%.

Class I and II require no follow-up; IIF requires imaging at 6-12 months with resection if evolving; Class III and IV require surgical resection. Growing literature suggests surveillance may be safe for some Class III lesions.

**Q:** What is the management approach for small solid renal masses based on size?

**A:** <2 cm: active surveillance with imaging q3-6 months, intervene if growth >2 cm or >0.5 cm/yr. 2-4 cm: active surveillance or definitive therapy (biopsy + thermal ablation or partial nephrectomy). If limited life expectancy: watchful waiting.

Initial workup includes kidney function (Cr, eGFR), CXR, contrast CT or MRI, ± renal biopsy. The management is tailored to tumour size and patient factors including life expectancy.

**Q:** What are the distinguishing features of angiomyolipoma (AML) on CT?

**A:** Negative attenuation (-20 HU) on CT is pathognomonic for AML due to fat content. AML consists of blood vessels (angio-), smooth muscle (-myo-), and fat (lipoma).

AML is a clonal neoplasm. 20% are associated with tuberous sclerosis, especially if multiple/recurrent. Most are incidental; symptomatic cases (pain, bleeding) or high bleeding risk (e.g., pregnancy) may require surgical excision or embolization. mTOR inhibitors have a potential role in unresectable/metastatic disease.

**Q:** Why is renal oncocytoma treated as RCC until proven otherwise?

**A:** Renal oncocytoma is difficult to distinguish from RCC on imaging. It appears as a spherical, capsulated mass with possible central scar on CT; biopsy may be performed to rule out malignancy.

Oncocytoma accounts for 3-7% of renal tumours and is histologically composed of eosinophilic cells originating from intercalated cells of the collecting duct. Most are managed with surveillance; large masses may need partial/radical nephrectomy.

**Q:** What cell of origin and histological subtypes characterize renal cell carcinoma (RCC)?

**A:** RCC originates from proximal convoluted tubule epithelial cells (clear cell subtype). Subtypes: clear cell (75-85%), papillary (10-15%), chromophobe (5-10%), collecting duct (<1%). Sarcomatoid/rhabdoid elements in any subtype indicate poor prognosis.

RCC accounts for 85% of primary malignant kidney tumours and ~3% of all malignancies. Peak incidence is ages 50-60 with M:F ratio of 1.5:1. Hereditary forms occur with von Hippel-Lindau syndrome, hereditary papillary renal carcinoma, Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome, and hereditary leiomyomatosis.

**Q:** What is the 'too late triad' of renal cell carcinoma, and how commonly is it seen?

**A:** The classic triad is: gross hematuria (50%), flank pain (<50%), and palpable mass (<30%). It is seen in only 10-15% of patients and indicates advanced disease.

The majority of RCCs (>50%) are now diagnosed incidentally by U/S or CT. The triad indicates late presentation. Poor prognostic indicators include weight loss, weakness, anemia, and bone pain. One-third of new cases present with metastases; an additional 20-40% develop metastases later.

**Q:** What are the most common metastatic sites of RCC and what local vascular complications can occur?

**A:** Most common metastatic sites: bone, brain, lung, and liver. RCC may invade renal veins and the IVC, potentially causing ascites, hepatic dysfunction, right atrial tumour, varicocele, and pulmonary emboli.

One-third of RCC patients present with metastases. IVC extension is clinically significant and must be assessed with MRI preoperatively. Right-sided varicocele that does not decompress with supine position should raise suspicion for IVC obstruction by tumour.

**Q:** What is the TNM T staging for RCC (AJCC 8th edition)?

**A:** T1: <7 cm confined to renal parenchyma (T1a: <4 cm, T1b: 4-7 cm). T2: >7 cm confined (T2a: >7-10 cm, T2b: >10 cm). T3: extends into major veins or perinephric tissues but NOT into ipsilateral adrenal or beyond Gerota's fascia (T3a: renal vein/sinus fat; T3b: infradiaphragmatic IVC; T3c: supradiaphragmatic IVC). T4: beyond Gerota's fascia including ipsilateral adrenal.

Stage at diagnosis is the most important prognostic factor: T1 has 90-100% 5-year survival; T2-T3 approximately 60%; metastatic disease <5% 10-year survival.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for advanced/metastatic RCC?

**A:** Immune checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, nivolumab), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, sorafenib, axitinib, lenvatinib), HIF-2α inhibitors (belzutifan). IFN-α monotherapy has largely been replaced by molecularly targeted agents. Palliative radiation to painful bony lesions and SBRT for non-surgical candidates.

Surgical removal of solitary metastasis may also be considered. Radical nephrectomy removes kidney, tumour, ipsilateral adrenal gland (upper pole tumours), and intact Gerota's fascia. Partial nephrectomy is used for tumours <4 cm or solitary kidney.

**Q:** What is von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome and what urological manifestations are associated with it?

**A:** VHL syndrome is associated with multiple bilateral renal cysts and/or renal cell carcinomas (50% incidence of RCC). Other features include cerebellar, spinal, and retinal hemangioblastomas, pancreatic and epididymal cysts, and pheochromocytomas.

VHL is a hereditary form of RCC. Patients with VHL need regular surveillance with imaging of the kidneys (and other affected organs). Genetic testing for VHL should be considered for non-clear cell carcinoma, bilateral/multifocal tumours, and onset before age 45.

## Urology Bladder Cancer

**Q:** What are the risk factors and key epidemiology of carcinoma of the renal pelvis and ureter?

**A:** Risk factors: smoking, aristolochic acid (Balkan endemic nephropathy, Chinese herbal nephropathy), industrial dyes (aniline dyes), analgesic misuse (acetaminophen, ASA, phenacetin), prior cyclophosphamide exposure. Epidemiology: 5% of all UC, frequently multifocal, 2-5% bilateral, M:F=3:1. Relative incidence bladder:renal:ureter = 100:10:1.

Lynch syndrome should be considered if there is personal or family history of other malignancies (colorectal, stomach, prostate, endometrial, etc.). Treatment is radical nephroureterectomy with excision of ipsilateral bladder cuff.

**Q:** What is the gold standard for diagnosis of bladder carcinoma?

**A:** TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) is the gold standard — both diagnostic and often therapeutic. It establishes diagnosis and determines depth of penetration. Involvement of muscularis propria confirms muscle invasion (T2).

Initial investigations also include U/A, urine C&S, urine cytology, ultrasound, contrast CT, and cystoscopy with biopsy for small lesions. Cystoscopy is the initial procedure of choice for diagnosis and staging of urothelial malignancy.

**Q:** What is the most important risk factor for bladder carcinoma, and what is the predominant histological type?

**A:** Smoking is the main risk factor, implicated in 60% of new cases. The predominant histological type is urothelial carcinoma (UC), accounting for >90% of cases.

Other risk factors include aromatic amines (naphthylamines, benzidine), cyclophosphamide, prior pelvic radiation, Schistosoma haematobium (associated with SCC), chronic irritation (cystitis, chronic catheterization, bladder stones — also SCC), and aristolochic acid. Bladder is the 2nd most common urological malignancy; M:F=3:1; mean age 65.

**Q:** What is the 'field defect' theory in bladder carcinoma?

**A:** The field defect theory explains why UC has multiple lesions and high recurrence rates: the entire urothelium from renal pelvis to bladder is bathed in carcinogens, predisposing the entire urothelial surface to malignant change.

This is why the ENTIRE urinary tract must be evaluated in patients with hematuria (unless there is clear evidence of glomerular bleeding). Cystoscopy with CT urography is required for complete evaluation.

**Q:** What is carcinoma in situ (CIS) of the bladder and why is it clinically significant?

**A:** CIS is a flat, non-papillary erythematous lesion characterized by dysplasia confined to the urothelium. It is clinically significant because it is more aggressive, has worse prognosis, higher recurrence rates, is associated with radioresistance, is usually multifocal, and may progress to invasive UC.

Storage urinary symptoms should prompt consideration of CIS. CIS is staged as Tis. Despite being non-invasive, it has a high risk of progression and requires aggressive intravesical BCG therapy.

**Q:** What is the key symptom of bladder carcinoma and what percentage of patients present with it?

**A:** Hematuria is the key symptom, occurring in 85-90% of bladder carcinoma patients at diagnosis. Unexplained hematuria in any individual >40 years must be investigated to rule out malignancy.

Bladder carcinoma may also present as asymptomatic (20%), pain (50%), clot retention (17%), storage urinary symptoms (consider CIS), palpable mass on bimanual exam (suggests muscle invasion), or obstruction of ureters causing hydronephrosis/uremia (bad prognostic factor).

**Q:** What are the stages and prognoses of urothelial carcinoma of the bladder at diagnosis?

**A:** Non-muscle invasive (75%): >80% overall survival; 15% progress to invasive UC. Invasive (25%): 50-60% 5-year survival; 85% have no prior superficial UC (de novo); 50% have occult metastases at diagnosis.

5-year survival by T stage: T1=90%, T2=55%, T3=30%, T4/N+/M+ <5%. Tumour grade is the single most important prognostic factor for progression.

**Q:** What is the treatment for non-muscle invasive bladder carcinoma (NMIBC) based on risk stratification?

**A:** Low risk (Ta low-grade): TURBT + intravesical chemo, follow-up with cystoscopy and cytology. Intermediate risk (multifocal, recurrent Ta): TURBT + intravesical chemo + BCG. High risk (T1, Tis, Ta high-grade): TURBT + intravesical chemo, repeat TURBT in 2-6 weeks (T1 only), BCG (3 years).

BCG is the only intravesical therapy associated with decreased risk of bladder cancer progression. Standard BCG is induction with weekly instillations for 6 weeks, followed by maintenance (weekly over 3 weeks) at months 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36.

**Q:** What does radical cystectomy involve in males vs. females?

**A:** Male: removal of bladder, prostate, and seminal vesicles. Female: removal of bladder, uterus, ovaries, and anterior vagina.

Radical cystectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) is the treatment for muscle-invasive disease. Urinary diversion options include ileal conduit, Indiana pouch, or ileal neobladder. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus cystectomy improves outcomes over cystectomy alone for locally advanced disease.

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis for a filling defect in the urinary tract?

**A:** Differential diagnosis of filling defect in the urinary tract: urothelial carcinoma (differentiate via cytology and CT), uric acid stone (differentiate via cytology and CT), blood clot, pyeitis cystica, papillary necrosis, fungus ball, gas bubble from gas-producing organisms.

CTU (CT urography) is the investigation of choice for upper tract lesions. Cystoscopy with retrograde pyelogram is also used. The clinical context (hematuria, risk factors, age) helps prioritize the differential.

**Q:** What is the TNM T staging of bladder carcinoma (AJCC 8th edition)?

**A:** Ta: noninvasive papillary carcinoma. Tis: CIS (flat tumour). T1: invades subepithelial connective tissue. T2: invades muscularis propria (T2a: inner half; T2b: outer half). T3: invades perivesical tissue (T3a: microscopically; T3b: macroscopically). T4: invades prostatic stroma, seminal vesicles, uterus, vagina, pelvic wall, or abdominal wall (T4a: prostatic stroma/uterus/vagina; T4b: pelvic/abdominal wall).

Non-muscle invasive disease includes Ta, Tis, and T1 — managed without cystectomy initially. T2 and above is muscle-invasive and typically requires radical cystectomy. A palpable mass on bimanual exam or obstructive symptoms suggest muscle invasion.

**Q:** What is the treatment for muscle-invasive and advanced/metastatic bladder carcinoma?

**A:** Muscle-invasive: radical cystectomy + pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine-platinum) improves outcomes. Advanced/metastatic (T4, N+, M+): chemotherapy + radiation + immunotherapy.

Chemoradiation (maximal TURBT + chemoradiation) is an alternative for those not fit for radical cystectomy. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus cystectomy is superior to cystectomy alone for locally advanced bladder cancer.

## Urology Prostate Cancer

**Q:** What are the epidemiology and key risk factors for prostate cancer?

**A:** Most prevalent cancer in males; 3rd leading cause of male cancer deaths (following lung and colon). Lifetime risk of death from prostate cancer is 3%; 75% diagnosed between ages 60-85. Risk factors: age >50 (increases 1%/yr after 65), African descent, high dietary fat (2x risk), family history (1st degree relative 2x; 1st and 2nd degree 9x), positive BRCA mutation.

Up to 50% risk of prostate cancer at age 50 (histological). Most prostate cancers are adenocarcinoma (>95%), often multifocal, graded by Gleason score. 60-70% of nodules arise in the peripheral zone.

**Q:** What are the anatomical zones of the prostate and their relative risk of cancer development?

**A:** Peripheral zone: 60-70% of cancers arise here. Transition zone: 10-20%. Central zone: 5-10%.

The peripheral zone is the most common site of adenocarcinoma and is palpable on DRE. The transition zone is where BPH typically arises. Prostate cancer is often multifocal and the peripheral zone location means many cancers are detectable by DRE.

**Q:** What are the most common metastatic sites of prostate cancer and its patterns of spread?

**A:** Bony metastases to the axial skeleton are most common. Visceral metastases (liver, lung, adrenal gland) are less common. Lymphatic spread: obturator > iliac > presacral/para-aortic nodes. Leg pain and edema occur with nodal metastases obstructing lymphatic and venous drainage.

Hematogenous dissemination occurs early. Metastatic prostate cancer commonly causes osteoblastic (sclerotic) bone lesions, distinguishing it from most other cancers which cause osteolytic lesions.

**Q:** What is the TNM T staging for prostate cancer (AJCC 8th edition)?

**A:** T1: clinically undetectable, normal DRE and TRUS (T1a: incidental <5% of TURP tissue; T1b: incidental >5% of TURP tissue; T1c: identified by needle biopsy due to elevated PSA). T2: palpable, confined to prostate (T2a: <1/2 one lobe; T2b: >1/2 one lobe; T2c: both lobes). T3: extends through capsule (T3a: extracapsular extension; T3b: seminal vesicle invasion). T4: invades adjacent structures besides seminal vesicles.

There is no pathological T1 — pathological staging begins at pT2. The T prefix (c) = clinical; (p) = pathological.

**Q:** What defines low-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk prostate cancer?

**A:** Low-risk: PSA <10, Gleason score <7 (Grade Group 1), stage pT1-2a. Intermediate-risk: PSA 10-20, Gleason score 7 (Grade Groups 2 and 3), stage pT2b-T2c. High-risk: PSA >20, Gleason score 8-10 (Grade Groups 4 and 5), stage pT3/4.

Risk stratification guides treatment decisions. Low-risk disease is appropriate for active surveillance. Intermediate and high-risk disease generally requires definitive therapy. Active surveillance for low-risk, small volume Gleason ≤7 prostate cancer is safe for most patients.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for localized prostate cancer and their key limitations?

**A:** Watchful waiting: short life expectancy; only palliative therapy if progression. Active surveillance (serial PSA, DRE, biopsies): low-grade disease; risk of disease progression, serial testing burden. Brachytherapy: low volume, low PSA, low grade; ED (50%), incontinence (<5%). EBRT: locally advanced, older patients; radiation proctitis (5%), ED (25-50%), risk of rectal/bladder cancer. Radical prostatectomy (RP): young (<75 yr), high-risk; incontinence (10%), ED (30-50%).

Minimal differences in cure or recurrence rates between definitive treatment modalities for localized disease. Other options include cryosurgery, HIFU, hormonal ablation, focal laser ablation.

**Q:** What is the treatment for metastatic/advanced prostate cancer (N>0 or M>0)?

**A:** Hormonal therapy (ADT): GnRH agonists (leuprolide, goserelin) or GnRH antagonist (degarelix) + one of enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide + docetaxel, abiraterone, or docetaxel alone. Bilateral orchiectomy decreases testosterone by 90%. Combined androgen blockade (ADT + anti-androgen e.g. bicalutamide). Local irradiation of painful metastases.

For T3/T4: ADT + calcium + vitamin D + bisphosphonates + EBRT/docetaxel/abiraterone. Castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC): maintain ADT; non-metastatic CRPC: apalutamide, enzalutamide, or darolutamide; metastatic CRPC: abiraterone, enzalutamide, docetaxel. Bone metastases: denosumab and/or zoledronic acid.

**Q:** What is PSA and what are important considerations about its interpretation?

**A:** PSA is a glycoprotein produced by epithelial cells of the prostate gland that leaks into circulation when glandular architecture is disrupted. <4 ng/mL is traditionally considered normal but there is no single justifiable cut-off. Serum PSA = 15% free + 85% bound. Decreased free:total PSA, elevated PSA velocity, and elevated PSA density are associated with increased CaP rates. PSA is specific to the prostate, NOT to prostate cancer.

Non-malignant causes of elevated PSA include BPH, prostatitis, prostatic ischemia/infarction, prostate biopsy/surgery, prostatic massage, acute urinary retention, urethral catheterization, cystoscopy, TRUS, strenuous exercise, ejaculation, acute renal failure, cardiac surgery-related ischemia, and radiation therapy.

**Q:** What are the 2022 Canadian Urological Association recommendations for PSA screening?

**A:** Men electing PSA screening should start at age 50; men at increased risk should start at age 45. Screening intervals based on PSA levels: PSA <1 ng/mL = q4 years; PSA 1-3 ng/mL = q2 years; PSA >3 ng/mL = more frequent testing or adjunctive strategies. Men with PSA <1 ng/mL at age 60 may consider discontinuing. All other men: discontinue at age 70. Men with life expectancy <10 years: discontinue.

DRE should be included as part of initial screening. Suspicious DRE findings include abnormal feeling, nodularity, focal lesion, or discrete change in texture/fullness/symmetry. Elevated PSA or abnormal DRE leads to biopsy.

## Urology Testicular Cancer

**Q:** What is the most common solid malignancy in young males aged 15-35 years?

**A:** Testicular tumours are the most common solid malignancy in young males aged 15-35 years, though overall they are rare.

Any solid testicular mass or acute hydrocele in a young patient must rule out malignancy. Slightly more common in right testis (corresponding to higher right-sided cryptorchidism incidence). Risk factors include cryptorchidism, atrophy, abnormal sex hormone exposure, HIV, infertility, and family/personal history of testicular cancer.

**Q:** What are the pathological types of testicular tumours and their relative frequencies?

**A:** 95% are germ cell tumours (all malignant): seminoma (50%) — classic, anaplastic, spermatocytic; NSGCT — embryonal cell carcinoma (10%), teratoma (5%), choriocarcinoma (<1%), yolk sac (<1%), mixed cell type (35%). 5% are non-germ cell tumours (usually benign): Leydig cells (produce testosterone, cause precocious puberty), Sertoli cells (cause gynecomastia, decreased libido). Secondary testicular tumours (male >50 years) are usually lymphoma or metastases.

Cryptorchidism increases malignancy risk by 10-40 times. The most common bilateral testicular tumour in men >50 years is lymphoma. Secondary tumours from lung, prostate, and GI tract can also occur.

**Q:** What are the tumour markers for testicular cancer and which are elevated in seminoma vs. NSGCT?

**A:** Tumour markers: β-hCG, LDH, AFP. β-hCG and AFP are positive in 85% of non-seminomatous tumours (NSGCT). β-hCG is positive in only 7% of pure seminomas. AFP is NEVER elevated with pure seminoma.

Elevated marker levels return to normal postoperatively if no metastasis remains. These markers are used for diagnosis, staging, and monitoring treatment response. LDH is a non-specific marker of tumour burden.

**Q:** Why is trans-scrotal biopsy or orchiectomy contraindicated for testicular cancer diagnosis?

**A:** The scrotum has different lymphatic drainage than the testis. Trans-scrotal approach risks tumour seeding and disrupts the normal lymphatic drainage, potentially altering spread patterns. Radical inguinal orchiectomy with ligation of the spermatic cord inside the inguinal canal is the correct approach.

Testicular lymphatics drain to retroperitoneal (para-aortic) nodes via the spermatic cord, whereas scrotal skin drains to inguinal nodes. Scrotal violation can lead to inguinal or pelvic nodal spread in addition to retroperitoneal spread, complicating staging and treatment.

**Q:** What is the staging of testicular cancer and how does lymphatic spread differ between right and left testis?

**A:** Stage I: disease limited to testis, epididymis, or spermatic cord. Stage II: disease limited to retroperitoneal nodes. Stage III: metastatic to supradiaphragmatic nodal or visceral sites. Right testis drains to medial, paracaval, anterior, and lateral nodes. Left testis drains to left lateral and anterior para-aortic nodes. Cross-over metastases from right to left are common, but not from left to right.

Investigations for staging include CT chest (lung metastases), CT abdomen/pelvis (retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy), and tumour markers (β-hCG, AFP, LDH). Hematogenous spread is most commonly to lung, liver, bones, and kidney.

**Q:** What is the prognosis of testicular cancer and what is the standard initial management for all stages?

**A:** Prognosis: 99% cured with Stage I and II disease; 70-80% complete remission with advanced disease. Standard initial management for all stages: radical orchiectomy through inguinal incision with ligation of the spermatic cord inside the inguinal canal. Also consider sperm banking and testicular prosthesis.

Testicular cancer is one of the most curable solid tumours. Orchiopexy (surgical descent of undescended testis) does not eliminate malignancy risk but allows for self-examination and reduces infertility risk. Needle aspiration is contraindicated due to risk of tumour seeding.

**Q:** What is the relationship between cryptorchidism and testicular cancer?

**A:** Cryptorchidism increases the risk of testicular malignancy by 10-40 times. 2-3% of testicular cancers are bilateral. Orchiopexy (surgical descent of the undescended testis) does not eliminate the risk of malignancy but allows for earlier detection by self-examination and reduces infertility risk.

Cryptorchidism is the most important risk factor for testicular cancer. Evidence of testicular microlithiasis is NOT a risk factor for testicular cancer. Any solid testicular mass in a young patient must be worked up for malignancy regardless of history.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of testicular tumours?

**A:** Painless testicular enlargement (painful if intratesticular hemorrhage or infarction), dull/heavy ache in lower abdomen/anal area/scrotum, associated hydrocele (10%), coincidental trauma (10%), gynecomastia (from secretory tumour effects), supraclavicular and inguinal lymphadenopathy, abdominal mass (retroperitoneal lymph node metastases), infertility (rarely presenting complaint).

The painless nature is the most clinically important feature — any painless testicular mass should be worked up with U/S. Testicular U/S showing hypoechoic area within tunica albuginea is high suspicion for testicular cancer.

## Urology Penile Cancer

**Q:** What is the most common histological type of penile cancer, and what are its major risk factors?

**A:** Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) >95% of penile malignancies. Risk factors: chronic inflammatory disease, STIs, phimosis, uncircumcised penis.

Penile SCC accounts for <1% of male cancers in the U.S. and <2% of all urogenital cancers. Circumcision reduces risk by eliminating phimosis and reducing chronic inflammation. Definitive diagnosis requires full-thickness biopsy.

**Q:** What is the difference between Bowen's disease and erythroplasia of Queyrat?

**A:** Both are penile carcinoma in situ (CIS). Bowen's disease: crusted red plaques on the penile shaft. Erythroplasia of Queyrat: velvet red, ulcerated plaques on the glans.

Both are pre-invasive squamous cell carcinoma of the penis. Treatment options for both include local excision, laser, radiation, topical 5-fluorouracil, or topical imiquimod. Distinction is primarily anatomical location.

**Q:** What are the pre-malignant penile lesions?

**A:** Balanitis xerotica obliterans, leukoplakia, and Buschke-Lowenstein tumour (giant condyloma acuminatum).

These lesions carry risk of malignant transformation to SCC. Buschke-Lowenstein tumour, despite being locally destructive, is histologically benign but behaves in a borderline malignant fashion.

## Urology Scrotal Conditions

**Q:** How do you clinically differentiate testicular torsion from epididymitis?

**A:** Torsion: absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn's sign, diffuse tenderness, horizontal lie. Epididymitis: present cremasteric reflex, positive Prehn's sign, epididymal tenderness.

Prehn's sign: elevation of the testicle relieves pain in epididymitis but not torsion. The cremasteric reflex is absent in torsion due to ischemia of the cord. Torsion is a urological emergency—testicular necrosis occurs in 5-6 hours.

**Q:** What is the 'blue dot sign' and what does it indicate?

**A:** The blue dot sign is a blue infarcted appendage visible through the scrotal skin in children, indicating torsion of the testicular or epididymal appendix.

Torsion of the testicular appendix is clinically similar to testicular torsion but can be distinguished by: preserved cremasteric reflex, vertical lie of testicle, and blue dot sign. Treatment is analgesia; most resolve in 5-7 days. Surgical excision if refractory pain.

**Q:** What are the varicocele grades?

**A:** Grade 1: palpable only with Valsalva. Grade 2: palpable without Valsalva. Grade 3: visible through scrotal skin.

Varicocele is dilated and tortuous pampiniform plexus veins due to incompetent venous valves, 90% left-sided. The 'bag of worms' appearance is characteristic. Treatment indications: impaired sperm quality/quantity, pain affecting QOL, failure of affected testis to grow in adolescents.

**Q:** What are the indications for treatment of varicocele?

**A:** 1. Impaired sperm quality or quantity. 2. Pain or dull ache affecting quality of life. 3. Affected testis fails to grow in adolescents. 4. Cosmetic indications.

Varicocele repair (surgical ligation or percutaneous vein occlusion) may improve sperm count and motility. Varicocele is found in 35-40% of infertile males and is the most common correctable cause of male infertility.

**Q:** How does a hydrocele differ from a spermatocele on clinical exam?

**A:** Hydrocele: testis NOT separable from the mass, cord palpable, transilluminates. Spermatocele: testis IS separable from the mass, cord palpable, transilluminates, non-tender cystic mass.

Both transilluminate (fluid-filled), but separation of the testis from the mass distinguishes spermatocele (benign epididymal retention cyst). Hydrocele is a collection of serous fluid in the tunica vaginalis—communicating (patent processus vaginalis, pediatric) or non-communicating (adult).

**Q:** What is the emergency management of testicular torsion?

**A:** Emergency surgical exploration and bilateral orchiopexy. Definitive diagnosis is NOT required before taking the patient to OR. Necrosis occurs within 5-6 hours.

Any acute scrotum in a young male should be treated as torsion until proven otherwise. Manual detorsion can be attempted but surgery is required regardless. Orchiectomy is performed if flow cannot be restored.

## Urology Penile Conditions

**Q:** What is Peyronie's disease and how is it managed?

**A:** Acquired penile curvature due to fibrous thickening of the tunica albuginea. Management: supportive (PDE5 inhibitor for ED, NSAIDs for pain), medical (traction device, intralesional verapamil or collagenase), surgical (plaque excision, plication surgery, penile prosthesis).

Peyronie's is associated with DM, vascular disease, autoimmunity, Dupuytren's contracture, and erectile dysfunction. Trauma/repeated inflammation leads to fibrous plaque formation. Intralesional collagenase clostridium histolyticum (Xiaflex) is an approved non-surgical option.

**Q:** What is priapism? Differentiate ischemic (low-flow) from non-ischemic (high-flow) priapism.

**A:** Priapism: prolonged erection >4h without sexual excitement. Ischemic (low-flow): thromboembolic (e.g. sickle cell), painful, requires urgent treatment (aspiration, phenylephrine injection, surgical shunt). Non-ischemic (high-flow): traumatic arteriovenous fistula, often painless, self-limited, consider arterial embolization.

Ischemic priapism is a urological emergency. Treatment escalation: needle aspiration → phenylephrine intracorporeal injection q3-5min → surgical shunt if no response. Sickle cell disease is the most common hematological cause.

**Q:** What is paraphimosis and how is it managed?

**A:** Paraphimosis: retracted foreskin behind the glans that cannot be reduced, causing a constricting band proximal to the corona. Management: manual pressure/analgesia, dorsal slit, or circumcision (urgent to prevent recurrence).

Paraphimosis is a urological emergency—the constricting band impairs venous and lymphatic drainage causing progressive swelling and can lead to ischemia of the glans. Causes include iatrogenic (post-instrumentation), trauma, or infection.

**Q:** What is premature ejaculation and how is it classified and treated?

**A:** Premature ejaculation: ejaculation prior to when one or both partners desire, with ejaculatory latency <1 min and inability to control/delay ejaculation. Primary (lifelong): no period of acceptable control. Secondary (acquired): after period of control, not associated with general medical condition. Treatment: rule out medical conditions, address psychiatric concerns (counseling), SSRIs or clomipramine, topical lidocaine-prilocaine.

SSRIs (dapoxetine is specifically approved; other SSRIs used off-label) delay ejaculation by their serotonergic effects on ejaculatory reflex. Topical anesthetics reduce penile sensitivity. Combined pharmacological and behavioral therapy is most effective.

## Urology Infertility

**Q:** What is the definition of infertility and what are the relative contributions of male, female, and combined factors?

**A:** Failure to conceive after 1 year of unprotected and properly timed intercourse. Incidence 15% of all couples: female factor 35-40%, male factor 20%, combined 25-30%.

In Canada, evaluation typically begins after 1 year (or 6 months if female partner >35 years). Both partners must be investigated. Male factor infertility is often overlooked—semen analysis is the key initial test.

**Q:** What are the most common causes of male infertility?

**A:** Idiopathic (40-50%), varicocele (35-40%), post-infectious (epididymo-orchitis, mumps orchitis), congenital (Klinefelter syndrome), obstructive (vas deferens absence, CF, gonorrhea, chlamydia, trauma), endocrine (HPTA: Kallmann's syndrome, hyperprolactinemia), cryptorchidism.

Varicocele is the most common identifiable and correctable cause. Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) presents with triad: small firm testes, gynecomastia, azoospermia. CFTR gene mutation causes congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens.

**Q:** What are the normal semen analysis (SA) parameters?

**A:** Volume: 1.5–7.6 mL. Concentration: >15 million sperm/mL. Morphology: >30% normal forms. Motility: >40% with adequate forward progression. Liquefaction: complete in 20 min. pH: 7.2–7.8. WBC: <10/HPF or <10^6 WBC/mL.

Two SA specimens collected 1-2 weeks apart are required for diagnosis. Common terminology: teratospermia (abnormal morphology), asthenospermia (abnormal motility), oligospermia (decreased sperm count), azoospermia (no sperm), oligoasthenospermia (combined).

**Q:** What medications are known to impair male fertility (mnemonic SPERM COUNT)?

**A:** S - Systemic factors/Smoking. P - ? (Psychotropics/SSRIs). E - Estrogens. R - Retrograde ejaculation drugs. M - Medications (cytotoxic, GnRH agonists, anabolic steroids, nitrofurantoin, cimetidine, sulfasalazine, spironolactone, alpha-blockers). C - Cocaine/Cannabis. O - ? N - Narcotics. T - Testicular (prior chemo/radiation, heat).

Key drug classes: cytotoxic chemotherapy (direct gonadotoxicity), GnRH agonists (suppress HPT axis), anabolic steroids (suppress endogenous testosterone), sulfasalazine (directly impairs spermatogenesis), spironolactone (antiandrogenic). Exogenous testosterone alone suppresses spermatogenesis—hypogonadal men wanting fertility should NOT use isolated testosterone therapy.

**Q:** What hormonal axis governs male reproduction, and what are the roles of LH and FSH?

**A:** HPTA axis: pulsatile GnRH (hypothalamus) → LH and FSH release (anterior pituitary). LH: acts on Leydig (interstitial) cells → testosterone synthesis. FSH: acts on Sertoli cells → structural/metabolic support to spermatogenic cells. Both FSH and testosterone support spermatogenesis.

Sperm route: epididymis → vas deferens → ejaculatory ducts → prostatic urethra. Klinefelter syndrome: elevated FSH/LH, low testosterone. Kallmann's syndrome: congenital GnRH deficiency with anosmia. Prolactin excess suppresses GnRH.

**Q:** What genetic investigations are indicated in male infertility?

**A:** Chromosomal studies (Klinefelter syndrome – 47,XXY), Y-chromosome microdeletion studies, CF gene mutation (CFTR) testing—especially before ICSI in patients with congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens.

ICSI requires CF screening of both patient and partner when there is congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens (CBAVD), as the offspring may be carriers or affected. Y-microdeletions are found in men with azoospermia or severe oligospermia.

**Q:** What is Kartagener's syndrome and how does it cause male infertility?

**A:** Kartagener's syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder causing defective cilia action (primary ciliary dyskinesia). It causes male infertility via immotile sperm (asthenospermia/azoospermia) due to absent or abnormal dynein arms.

The classic triad is: bronchiectasis, sinusitis, and situs inversus. Infertility results from immotile sperm (same ciliary defect affecting flagella). Females may also have reduced fertility. Diagnosis: nasal brushing biopsy, electron microscopy of cilia.

**Q:** What is Klinefelter syndrome and what is its classic triad?

**A:** Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY): classic triad of small firm testes, gynecomastia, and azoospermia. It causes primary hypogonadism with elevated LH/FSH and low testosterone.

Klinefelter is the most common sex chromosome aneuploidy in males. It is suggested by learning disabilities and poor school performance. Infertile patients with Klinefelter may still have focal spermatogenesis detectable by testicular biopsy. Confirmed by karyotype. Managed with testosterone replacement.

**Q:** What investigations are required in the workup of male infertility?

**A:** 1. Semen analysis (x2, collected 1-2 weeks apart). 2. Hormonal evaluation (testosterone, FSH; add LH and prolactin if abnormal). 3. Genetic evaluation (karyotype for Klinefelter, Y-chromosome microdeletion, CFTR mutation). 4. Immunologic studies (antisperm antibodies). 5. Testicular biopsy (if azoospermic). 6. Scrotal U/S (varicocele, testicular size). 7. Vasography (assess vas deferens patency).

Azoospermic patients with NORMAL FSH may be assumed to be obstructive without a testicular biopsy. Abnormal FSH with azoospermia suggests primary testicular failure. The algorithm branches based on bilateral vs. unilateral testicular atrophy and FSH level.

**Q:** What are the assisted reproductive technology (ART) options for male infertility?

**A:** 1. Sperm washing + intrauterine insemination (IUI). 2. In vitro fertilization (IVF). 3. Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)—especially for severe male factor; requires CF screening of patient and partner when congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens is present.

ICSI bypasses natural fertilization barriers, allowing a single sperm to be injected directly into an oocyte. CF screening is mandatory before ICSI in CBAVD cases to counsel couples on risk of having an affected child. ART referral is to an infertility specialist.

## Urology Andrology

**Q:** What are the clinical features of testosterone deficiency (hypogonadism) in men?

**A:** Sexual: decreased libido, delayed ejaculation, reduced ejaculate volume, decreased orgasm intensity, ED, loss of morning erections, infertility. Cognitive: fatigue, mood changes, depression, insomnia, irritability. Physical: decreased energy, anemia, gynecomastia, hot flashes, decreased muscle mass, increased visceral fat, osteopenia, testicular atrophy, loss of androgenic hair.

Diagnosis requires clinical features PLUS morning total testosterone <10 nmol/L. Measure LH to distinguish primary (high LH) from secondary (low/normal LH) hypogonadism. Absolute contraindications to testosterone therapy: male breast cancer, desiring fertility, advanced/metastatic prostate cancer.

## Urology Pediatric

**Q:** What is antenatal hydronephrosis and what are its causes?

**A:** Antenatal hydronephrosis is dilation of the fetal renal collecting system detected on U/S (1-5% of fetal U/S). Causes: transient primary hydronephrosis, UPJ obstruction, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), UVJ obstruction/megaureter, ureterocele, ectopic ureter, megacystitis (posterior urethral valves, Prune Belly syndrome).

Majority of antenatal hydronephroses resolve during pregnancy or within the first year of life. ABx prophylaxis at birth is controversial but may benefit high-grade hydronephrosis with dilated ureter or bladder abnormality (amoxicillin, cephalexin, or trimethoprim).

**Q:** What are the SFU grades of hydronephrosis?

**A:** Grade 0: no dilation, calyceal walls opposed. Grade 1 (mild): dilation of renal pelvis only. Grade 2 (mild): dilation of pelvis and some calyces, pelvicalyceal pattern retained, no parenchymal atrophy. Grade 3 (moderate): moderate dilation of pelvis and calyces, thinning/blunting of fornices, flattening of papillae. Grade 4 (severe): gross dilation of pelvis and calyces with parenchymal thinning.

SFU (Society for Fetal Urology) grading is based on ultrasound and should be supplemented with UTD (Urinary Tract Dilation) classification. Higher grades have worse prognosis and are less likely to resolve spontaneously.

**Q:** What is posterior urethral valves (PUV) and how does it present across different ages?

**A:** PUV: most common congenital obstructive urethral lesion in male infants due to abnormal mucosal folds at the distal prostatic urethra. Antenatal: bilateral hydronephrosis, distended bladder, oligohydramnios. Neonatal (recognized): palpable abdominal mass, urinary ascites, respiratory distress, weak urinary stream. Neonatal (not recognized): urosepsis, dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, failure to thrive. Toddlers: UTIs, voiding dysfunction. School-aged: urinary incontinence.

PUV 'keyhole sign' on prenatal U/S = dilated posterior urethra. VCUG shows dilated/elongated posterior urethra and trabeculated bladder. Treatment: immediate catheterization then cystoscopic resection; vesicostomy if resection not possible. Associated with renal dysplasia and secondary VUR.

**Q:** What is UPJ obstruction? How does it present and how is it treated?

**A:** UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) obstruction is the most common congenital defect of the ureter; M:F=2:1, up to 40% bilateral. Etiology: adynamic ureteral segment, stenosis, strictures, extrinsic compression, aberrant blood vessels. Presentation: infants (abdominal mass, UTI), children (pain, vomiting, failure to thrive), adolescents/adults (Dietl's crisis—episodic pain after alcohol/high fluid intake). Treatment: surgical pyeloplasty; consider nephrectomy if <15% differential renal function.

Dietl's crisis is episodic UPJ obstruction triggered by increased diuresis (e.g., after alcohol). Investigation: antenatal serial U/S, postnatal renal scan with furosemide (MAG3 or DMSA). Pyeloplasty has >90% success rate.

**Q:** What is vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), and how is it graded?

**A:** VUR: retrograde passage of urine from bladder through UVJ into ureter. Grading: Grade I—ureters only fill. Grade II—ureters and pelvis fill. Grade III—ureters and pelvis fill with some calyceal filling, mild dilation. Grade IV—moderate dilation/tortuosity of ureters and pelvis. Grade V—ureters, pelvis, and calyces fill with gross dilation and tortuous ureters.

Primary VUR: incompetent UVJ (short submucosal segment). Secondary VUR: high intravesical pressure from PUV or neuropathic bladder. Risk factors: white race, female gender, age <2 years, genetic predisposition. Higher grades associated with renal scarring. Diagnosed with VCUG.
