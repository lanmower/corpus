# FamilyMedicine

## Preventive Medicine & Health Maintenance

**Q:** What immunizations are routinely recommended for adults in Canada?

**A:** Influenza (annual), Td/Tdap (every 10 years), Pneumococcal (PCV20 once, or PCV15+PPSV23 sequence for age ≥65 or high risk), Shingles (Shingrix x2, age ≥50), COVID-19 (updated as recommended), Hepatitis A/B (if not immune), RSV (≥60 years)

NACI adult immunization schedule is updated annually. Shingrix (recombinant zoster vaccine) is >90% effective at preventing shingles — recommended for all adults ≥50 regardless of prior chickenpox. HPV vaccine recommended for adults up to age 45 who were not previously vaccinated.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to cardiovascular risk reduction in primary prevention?

**A:** 10-year Framingham Risk Score or SCORE2; low-risk: lifestyle modification; intermediate risk (10-20%): shared decision-making for statin; high risk (>20%) or established CVD: statin + lifestyle + BP control + aspirin (secondary prevention only)

CCS Dyslipidemia Guidelines: statins reduce CVD events proportional to LDL reduction (~22% reduction per 1 mmol/L LDL drop). CTFPHC recommends aspirin only for secondary prevention — primary prevention aspirin increases bleeding risk without clear net benefit in low-risk patients.

**Q:** What lifestyle counseling should be offered to all adult patients?

**A:** Smoking cessation (most important), physical activity (150 min/week moderate intensity), healthy diet (DASH or Mediterranean), weight management (BMI 18.5-24.9), alcohol reduction (≤10 drinks/week), stress management, sleep hygiene

CTFPHC and CMA: brief counseling for lifestyle modification at every primary care visit. 5 A's model (Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange) for smoking cessation. Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines: 150 min moderate-vigorous activity + 2 muscle-strengthening days per week.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to prostate cancer screening in primary care?

**A:** Individualized decision-making for men 50-70 with PSA: discuss benefits (mortality reduction ~20-30%) and harms (overdiagnosis, overtreatment, impotence, incontinence); CTFPHC recommends against routine PSA screening

CTFPHC recommends against routine PSA screening due to net harm from overdiagnosis and overtreatment. However, patient autonomy allows informed men to choose PSA testing after a thorough discussion. High-risk men (Black men, family history of prostate cancer): earlier screening discussion.

**Q:** When should low-dose aspirin be prescribed for primary prevention?

**A:** Low-dose aspirin (81 mg/day) is NOT recommended for primary prevention of CVD in adults without established CVD due to increased bleeding risk (GI bleeding, hemorrhagic stroke) outweighing modest CV benefit

CTFPHC 2022 guideline: recommends against initiating aspirin for primary prevention in adults ≥60 years. For 40-59 years with ≥10% 10-year CVD risk: individualized decision (marginal net benefit). Aspirin continues for secondary prevention (after MI, stroke, established CAD).

**Q:** What is the Canadian approach to depression screening in primary care?

**A:** PHQ-2 (2-question screen) at every appropriate visit; if positive, full PHQ-9; CTFPHC: screening adults ≥18 for depression if there is adequate care available to treat and follow up detected cases

Canadian population health: depression affects 11% of Canadians annually. Collaborative care models (primary care + mental health specialist) improve outcomes. CTFPHC recommends against screening if treatment is not available. Brief intervention (counseling or antidepressants) in primary care is effective.

**Q:** What is the evidence for health screening in older adults and when should screening be stopped?

**A:** Screening should only be done when patient would benefit from early detection and treatment; general guideline: stop screening when life expectancy <10 years or when patient would not undergo further investigation or treatment

CTFPHC: de-prescribing of screening in frail elderly is appropriate. Mammogram in a 90-year-old with dementia and life expectancy 2 years: no benefit. Shared decision-making about discontinuing preventive interventions as functional status declines.

**Q:** What is the Canadian recommendation for universal HIV testing?

**A:** CTFPHC: HIV testing recommended for all adults 15-65 as part of routine care; universal testing in high-prevalence settings (correctional facilities, addiction programs); annual testing for high-risk individuals

PHAC: 1 in 4 Canadians with HIV is unaware of their status. Universal testing in primary care increases detection. CTFPHC 2019 recommendation: opt-out testing for all adults at least once. HIV testing in pregnancy: universal at first prenatal visit.

**Q:** What are the recommended blood pressure targets and monitoring intervals in primary prevention?

**A:** All adults: measure at every visit; if normal (<130/80): every 2-3 years; if elevated (130-139/80-89): confirm with AOBP or home BP, lifestyle counseling; if ≥140/90: initiate treatment per Hypertension Canada guidelines

Hypertension Canada recommends AOBP as the standard office measurement. BP ≥130/80 with high CVD risk: consider treatment. Hypertension Canada: 90% of Canadians will develop hypertension if they live into their 80s — lifestyle modification throughout life is key.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to fall prevention in older adults?

**A:** Multifactorial assessment + individualized intervention: exercise (balance and strength), medication review (polypharmacy, benzodiazepines, antihypertensives), vision correction, home hazard reduction, foot care, Vitamin D supplementation

CTFPHC: exercise programs (tai chi, strength/balance training) have the strongest evidence for fall prevention. Vitamin D 800 IU/day reduces falls in deficient elderly. Annual medication review — fall-risk medications (opioids, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, antihypertensives) should be minimized.

**Q:** What is healthy aging and the key preventive measures in adults over 65?

**A:** Healthy aging: maintaining functional ability; interventions: exercise (150 min/week), protein intake ≥1.2 g/kg, fall prevention, cognitive engagement, social connection, managing chronic diseases, advance care planning, vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal, shingles, COVID-19)

CPS and CTFPHC healthy aging guidelines: frailty screening (Clinical Frailty Scale) guides approach to treatment. Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) optimizes care for frail elderly. Medication review (Beers Criteria or STOPP/START) essential to identify inappropriate medications.

**Q:** What is the CAGE questionnaire and when should it be used in primary care?

**A:** CAGE: Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener — 2+ positive = probable alcohol use disorder; use in primary care for alcohol screening in all adult patients at every visit or annual health exam

CAMH recommends integrating alcohol and substance use screening into routine primary care. CAGE has lower sensitivity for hazardous drinking (does not detect it); AUDIT-C preferred for detecting problem drinking spectrum. SBIRT approach: brief intervention after positive screen.

**Q:** What are the benefits of physical activity for primary prevention?

**A:** Physical activity reduces: CVD risk (35%), T2DM risk (50%), colon cancer (25-30%), breast cancer (20-30%), depression and anxiety, cognitive decline, falls in elderly, all-cause mortality (30%)

CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology) Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines: 150 min moderate-vigorous activity + 2 strength training sessions per week for adults. Even 15 min/day of brisk walking reduces mortality by 14%. "Every step counts" — sedentary time should be minimized.

**Q:** What is comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) and when should it be used?

**A:** CGA: multidimensional interdisciplinary assessment of functional status (ADLs/IADLs), cognition, mood, social support, medications, nutrition, gait/balance, sensory impairment — indicated for complex elderly patients, before major surgery, on hospital admission

CGA by geriatrician reduces iatrogenic harm, inappropriate medication, functional decline. STOPP (Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions) and START criteria guide medication review. Frailty assessment using Clinical Frailty Scale (1-9) guides goals of care and treatment intensity.

**Q:** What is the approach to smoking cessation counseling in primary care?

**A:** 5 A's: Ask (tobacco use status), Advise (clear advice to quit), Assess (readiness to quit), Assist (prescribe cessation aid — varenicline first-line), Arrange (follow-up within 1 week); 3B's alternative: Brief, Simple, and Directive

CTFPHC: physicians should screen for tobacco use at every visit. Brief advice (30 seconds) increases quit rates. Combined pharmacotherapy + behavioral support doubles success rates. Varenicline 12 weeks of treatment doubles long-term abstinence vs. NRT. Nicotine replacement therapy is available OTC.

**Q:** What is included in the periodic health exam for adults?

**A:** BP screening all adults, BMI, depression screening (PHQ-9), alcohol (AUDIT-C), diabetes risk q3y if >=40, lipids based on FRS, STI screening if risk factors

CTFPHC recommendations vary by age/sex. Annual exam not evidence-based as a package but individual components are. Shared decision-making for cancer screening.

**Q:** What components make up the Canadian periodic health exam for adults 18-39?

**A:** BP screening, cervical cancer screening (age 25+), chlamydia/gonorrhea screening if sexually active, mental health assessment, substance use screening, immunization update, contraception counseling

The periodic health exam is age/sex/risk-stratified. CTFPHC maneuvers guide evidence-based preventive care. Avoid unnecessary routine blood work in low-risk young adults. Focus on lifestyle counseling: diet, exercise, smoking cessation, alcohol.

**Q:** What components make up the periodic health exam for adults 40-74?

**A:** BP, lipid screening, diabetes (FPG/A1c q3-5y from 40), colorectal Ca (FIT q2y from 50), breast Ca (mammo q2-3y 50-74), cervical Ca (Pap q3y 25-69), lung Ca (LDCT if 30+pack-yr 55-74), AAA (once men 65-80 ever-smoked)

Cardiovascular risk assessment using Framingham Risk Score. Statin therapy if 10-year CV risk >20% or >10% with risk-enhancing factors. Osteoporosis screening (FRAX/BMD) for women 65+. Vision/hearing assessment.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC smoking cessation recommendations?

**A:** Ask about tobacco at every visit. Advise all smokers to quit. Recommend NRT, varenicline, or bupropion. Brief interventions effective. Combine pharmacotherapy + behavioral counseling

Varenicline most effective single agent (OR 2.3 vs placebo). NRT forms: patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, spray. Bupropion also treats depression. All provinces provide some smoking cessation coverage. E-cigarettes not recommended by CTFPHC.

**Q:** What are the Canadian cardiovascular disease prevention guidelines for lipid screening?

**A:** Screen all adults >=40 (or earlier if risk factors). Use FRS for 10-year risk. High risk (>=20%): treat with statin. Moderate (10-19%): consider statin if LDL>3.5. Low (<10%): lifestyle

CCS 2021 lipid guidelines. Alternative: use CLEM score. Primary targets: LDL <2.0 or >50% reduction for high risk. ApoB <0.8. Non-HDL <2.6. Consider ASCVD risk enhancers: family history, South Asian ethnicity, CKD, inflammatory disease.

**Q:** What are the Canadian guidelines for alcohol screening and brief intervention?

**A:** Screen with AUDIT-C or CAGE. Canada Low-Risk Alcohol Drinking Guidelines (2023 update): no safe amount; <=2 drinks/wk low risk, 3-6 moderate, >=7 increasingly high risk

2023 CCSA guidance significantly reduced recommended limits from previous 10F/15M per week. Brief intervention (FRAMES: Feedback, Responsibility, Advice, Menu, Empathy, Self-efficacy) reduces consumption. Screen for alcohol use disorder with DSM-5 criteria (>=2 of 11 criteria).

**Q:** What are the osteoporosis screening guidelines in Canada?

**A:** FRAX or CAROC assessment for all >=50 with risk factors. BMD (DXA) for: all women >=65, men >=70, postmenopausal women or men >=50 with risk factors. T-score <=-2.5 = osteoporosis

Osteoporosis Canada 2010 guidelines. 10-year fracture risk: low <10%, moderate 10-20%, high >20%. Treat high risk. Moderate: consider if additional risk factors. Vertebral fracture assessment adds to DXA. Calcium 1000-1200mg + Vit D 800-2000 IU daily for all >=50.

**Q:** What are the Canadian Hypertension Education Program (now Hypertension Canada) BP targets?

**A:** General: <140/90. Diabetes: <130/80. High-risk (CKD, CV disease): <120 systolic considered. Office BP >=130/85 warrants out-of-office measurement (ABPM or HBPM)

Hypertension Canada 2020. Diagnosis requires elevated BP on 2+ visits or ABPM/HBPM confirmation. First-line: thiazide, ACEi/ARB, long-acting CCB, or combination. Lifestyle: DASH diet, sodium <2000mg/d, exercise 30-60min 4-7d/wk, limit alcohol, maintain healthy weight.

**Q:** What immunizations should be discussed at each periodic health exam?

**A:** Influenza annually. Tdap/Td q10y. Pneumococcal >=65. Shingrix >=50. HPV catch-up <=26. HepB if not immunized. COVID per NACI. Travel vaccines as needed

Pregnancy: Tdap each pregnancy 27-32wk, influenza. Immunocompromised: avoid live vaccines. Catch-up schedules available at CANImmunize.ca. Provincial coverage varies. Document and maintain immunization records.

**Q:** What are the recommendations for physical activity counseling in primary care?

**A:** Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines: 150 min/wk moderate-vigorous aerobic + 2 days strength. Reduce sedentary time. Brief exercise prescription (FITT: Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type)

Exercise is medicine. Benefits: 30% reduction all-cause mortality, improves depression, CV risk, diabetes control, bone health. Even 15 min/day provides benefit. Assess readiness to change (transtheoretical model). Address barriers.

**Q:** What are the Canadian guidelines for skin cancer prevention?

**A:** Sun protection counseling for all. No population-based skin cancer screening recommended by CTFPHC. Clinical skin examination for high-risk individuals (FHx melanoma, >50 nevi, fair skin, prior skin cancer)

Canadian Dermatology Association recommends sun protection. SPF 30+ broad-spectrum, reapply q2h. Avoid tanning beds. Educate on ABCDE for self-monitoring. Dermoscopy improves diagnostic accuracy in primary care.

**Q:** What are the indications for ASA for primary cardiovascular prevention?

**A:** CTFPHC 2019: do NOT recommend ASA for primary prevention in most adults. May consider in high CV risk (10-year >20%) ages 40-70 if low bleeding risk, after shared decision-making

Differs from previous recommendations. ASPREE, ARRIVE, ASCEND trials showed harms (bleeding) outweigh benefits for most. Secondary prevention: ASA 81mg remains indicated post-ACS/stroke. Consider clopidogrel if ASA-intolerant.

**Q:** What are the elements of anticipatory guidance for new parents?

**A:** Safe sleep (back to sleep, firm surface, no bedsharing), breastfeeding support, immunization schedule, car seat safety, injury prevention, postpartum depression screening, contraception

CPS Well-Baby Visit schedule: 1-2wk, 1mo, 2mo, 4mo, 6mo, 9mo, 12mo, 15mo, 18mo, then annually. Rourke Baby Record is Canadian standardized tool for well-child visits. Screen maternal mental health at each visit.

**Q:** What is the approach to STI screening in primary care?

**A:** Screen sexually active <25 and high-risk for chlamydia/gonorrhea (urine NAAT). HIV screening offered to all. Syphilis in MSM, endemic exposure, pregnancy. HBV/HCV in high-risk

Canadian STI Guidelines (PHAC). Chlamydia: azithromycin 1g PO or doxycycline 100mg BID x 7d. Gonorrhea: ceftriaxone 250mg IM + azithromycin 1g. Report gonorrhea/syphilis to public health. Partner notification mandatory.

**Q:** What are the key elements of the well-child visit in Canada?

**A:** Growth/development monitoring (Rourke Baby Record/Nipissing), immunizations per schedule, nutrition counseling, injury prevention, vision/hearing screening, dental health, family assessment, maternal mental health

Developmental screening at 18-24 months with validated tool (M-CHAT for autism). Milestone monitoring at every visit. Anemia screening (CBC) at 9-12 months in high-risk. Lead screening if risk factors.

**Q:** What cancer screening is recommended for patients with strong family history?

**A:** Breast: start 10y before youngest affected relative, consider MRI if BRCA. Colon: colonoscopy from 40 or 10y before youngest case, q5y. Consider genetic referral for: >=2 first-degree relatives, young onset, Lynch/BRCA patterns

High-risk screening protocols differ from average-risk CTFPHC guidelines. Genetic counseling and testing criteria: multiple affected relatives, young onset (<50), associated cancers (Lynch: colon+endometrial+ovarian). Provincial genetics programs provide assessment.

**Q:** What is the PDSA cycle in quality improvement?

**A:** Plan (identify problem, develop intervention), Do (implement small-scale test), Study (analyze results), Act (adopt, adapt, or abandon). Iterative rapid-cycle improvement

QI methodology. Root cause analysis for sentinel events. Swiss cheese model: multiple system failures align to cause harm. Incident reporting systems are non-punitive to encourage disclosure.

**Q:** What is motivational interviewing and its core principles?

**A:** Patient-centred counselling to elicit behaviour change. OARS: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries. Express empathy, develop discrepancy, roll with resistance, support self-efficacy

Effective for: substance use, smoking cessation, diet/exercise, medication adherence. Avoids confrontation. Change talk (patient's own reasons for change) is more effective than provider advice.

**Q:** What are the key patient safety practices to prevent healthcare-associated infections?

**A:** Hand hygiene (WHO 5 moments), surgical safety checklist, antimicrobial stewardship, C. diff prevention (avoid unnecessary PPIs/antibiotics), MRSA/VRE screening and contact precautions

HAIs affect ~8% of hospitalized Canadians. Central line bundles reduce CLABSI. Ventilator bundles reduce VAP. Medication reconciliation at transitions prevents adverse drug events.

**Q:** What is the Swiss cheese model of accident causation, and how does it apply to healthcare?

**A:** James Reason's Swiss cheese model posits that adverse events result from multiple system failures aligned simultaneously (holes in multiple slices of cheese). Each slice represents a defence layer (training, protocols, checklists, technology). An error reaches the patient only when holes in all layers align. It shifts focus from individual blame to system-level defences.

The Swiss cheese model is foundational to patient safety science and is endorsed by Healthcare Excellence Canada (formerly CPSI). It underpins root cause analysis methodology used in Canadian hospitals. The model explains why redundant safety systems (e.g., double-checks for high-alert medications, surgical checklists) are essential.

**Q:** What is root cause analysis (RCA), and what are the key steps?

**A:** RCA is a structured method for investigating adverse events to identify underlying system causes. Steps include: (1) Identify the event, (2) Assemble a multidisciplinary team, (3) Map the sequence of events (timeline), (4) Identify contributing factors using tools like fishbone diagrams, (5) Determine root causes (system-level, not individual blame), (6) Develop and implement corrective actions, (7) Monitor effectiveness.

RCA is required by Accreditation Canada after sentinel events. Healthcare Excellence Canada provides frameworks and training. Key principle: root causes are always system-level (e.g., inadequate staffing, poor design, missing protocols), not just individual errors. The 'Five Whys' technique helps drill down from proximate to root causes.

**Q:** Describe the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle and how it is applied in healthcare quality improvement.

**A:** PDSA is an iterative QI method: Plan (identify the problem, develop a hypothesis and plan a small test of change), Do (implement the test on a small scale), Study (analyze results, compare to predictions), Act (adopt, adapt, or abandon the change based on results). Multiple rapid cycles allow progressive refinement before large-scale implementation.

PDSA is the core method of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Model for Improvement, widely used in Canadian healthcare. Key principle: start small and iterate rapidly rather than attempting large-scale change. Canadian teaching hospitals use PDSA cycles for residency QI projects, and the Royal College requires QI competency in all residency programs.

**Q:** What is medication reconciliation, and when should it be performed?

**A:** Medication reconciliation is the process of creating an accurate, complete list of a patient's current medications and comparing it to medication orders at every transition of care (admission, transfer, discharge). Discrepancies are identified and resolved with the prescriber. It is a Required Organizational Practice (ROP) by Accreditation Canada.

Medication discrepancies occur in up to 70% of hospital admissions. Accreditation Canada mandates medication reconciliation at admission and discharge as a Required Organizational Practice. ISMP Canada (Institute for Safe Medication Practices) provides tools and best practices. Pharmacist-led medication reconciliation has been shown to reduce adverse drug events.

**Q:** Which medications are classified as 'high-alert' by ISMP Canada, and what safety strategies apply to them?

**A:** High-alert medications include: insulin, opioids, anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin, DOACs), concentrated electrolytes (KCl, NaCl >0.9%), neuromuscular blocking agents, chemotherapy agents, and look-alike/sound-alike drugs. Strategies include: independent double-checks, tall man lettering, limited access, standardized concentrations, clinical decision support, and removal of concentrated electrolytes from patient care areas.

ISMP Canada maintains and updates the high-alert medication list for Canadian institutions. These medications carry a heightened risk of significant patient harm when used in error. Accreditation Canada requires organizations to implement safeguards specifically for high-alert medications as a Required Organizational Practice.

**Q:** What are the three phases of the Surgical Safety Checklist, and what is verified during each?

**A:** Based on the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (adapted in Canada): (1) Sign In (before anesthesia): patient identity, procedure, site marking, consent, allergy/airway assessment, blood loss risk. (2) Time Out (before incision): team introductions, confirmation of patient/procedure/site, anticipated critical events, antibiotic prophylaxis, imaging displayed. (3) Sign Out (before patient leaves OR): procedure name confirmed, instrument/sponge count, specimen labelling, equipment concerns, key recovery plan.

The Surgical Safety Checklist was pioneered by the WHO and championed in Canada by Dr. Atul Gawande's Safe Surgery Saves Lives initiative. Implementation in Ontario hospitals was associated with a significant reduction in surgical mortality. It is a Required Organizational Practice by Accreditation Canada for all procedural settings.

**Q:** Define and distinguish: sentinel event, adverse event, near miss, and no-harm incident.

**A:** Sentinel event: an unexpected occurrence involving death or serious physical/psychological injury (e.g., wrong-site surgery). Adverse event: an event that results in unintended harm to the patient related to healthcare (not the disease process). Near miss: an event that could have caused harm but was intercepted before reaching the patient. No-harm incident: an event that reached the patient but did not cause detectable harm.

The Canadian Incident Analysis Framework (Healthcare Excellence Canada) uses these standardized definitions. Reporting all incident types, including near misses, is essential for proactive safety improvement. The Canadian Adverse Events Study (2004) found that 7.5% of hospital admissions were associated with an adverse event, with 37% judged preventable.

**Q:** A hospital ward experiences a cluster of Clostridioides difficile (C. diff) infections. What infection prevention and control measures should be implemented?

**A:** Contact precautions (gown and gloves), hand hygiene with soap and water (alcohol-based hand rub is ineffective against C. diff spores), enhanced environmental cleaning with sporicidal agents (e.g., bleach-based), antimicrobial stewardship (reduce unnecessary fluoroquinolone and cephalosporin use), patient isolation or cohorting, and notification to infection control and public health.

C. diff is the most common healthcare-associated infection in Canadian hospitals. Provincial mandatory reporting exists in several provinces (e.g., Ontario's mandatory C. diff surveillance). Antimicrobial stewardship targeting high-risk antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, clindamycin, cephalosporins) is a key prevention strategy endorsed by AMMI Canada.

**Q:** What are the key differences in infection control precautions for MRSA versus VRE in a Canadian hospital?

**A:** Both require contact precautions (gown, gloves, dedicated equipment). MRSA screening often includes nasal swabs; VRE screening uses rectal/perianal swabs. MRSA decolonization may be attempted (mupirocin nasal ointment + chlorhexidine bathing). VRE decolonization is generally not recommended. Both require enhanced environmental cleaning. Alcohol-based hand rub is effective for both (unlike C. diff).

MRSA and VRE are major antimicrobial-resistant organisms (AROs) tracked by the Canadian Nosocomial Infection Surveillance Program (CNISP). Provincial Infectious Diseases Advisory Committee (PIDAC) guidelines in Ontario provide standardized ARO management protocols. Active surveillance screening on admission is recommended for high-risk patients in many Canadian facilities.

**Q:** What is an antimicrobial stewardship program, and what are its core strategies?

**A:** Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) optimize antimicrobial use to improve patient outcomes, reduce adverse effects, and limit antimicrobial resistance. Core strategies include: prospective audit and feedback, formulary restriction and preauthorization, de-escalation protocols, IV-to-oral conversion, dose optimization, clinical practice guidelines, and education. ASPs are a Required Organizational Practice by Accreditation Canada.

AMMI Canada (Association of Medical Microbiology and Infectious Disease) and the Public Health Agency of Canada support antimicrobial stewardship as essential to combating antimicrobial resistance. Canada's Pan-Canadian Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance identifies stewardship as a key pillar. The MCCQE1 may test appropriate antibiotic use scenarios.

**Q:** What is the 5A's approach to smoking cessation?

**A:** Ask (smoking status), Advise (quit), Assess (readiness), Assist (pharmacotherapy + counselling), Arrange (follow-up). Pharmacotherapy: NRT, varenicline (most effective), bupropion

Varenicline: partial nicotinic agonist, 3x quit rate vs placebo. NRT: patch + short-acting (gum/lozenge/inhaler). Combination NRT as effective as varenicline. E-cigarettes: not recommended by Health Canada for cessation.

**Q:** What are the criteria for bariatric surgery in Canada?

**A:** BMI >=40 OR BMI >=35 with obesity-related comorbidity (T2DM, HTN, OSA, NAFLD). Failed adequate trial of non-surgical management. Multidisciplinary assessment required

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy most common. Benefits: sustained weight loss, T2DM remission (60-80%), reduced CV mortality. Risks: nutritional deficiencies (B12, iron, calcium), dumping syndrome.

**Q:** What is the Canadian well-child visit schedule and what anticipatory guidance topics are discussed at key ages?

**A:** Schedule: 1-2 weeks, 2 months, 4 months, 6 months, 9 months, 12 months, 15 months, 18 months, 2-3 years, then annually. Key anticipatory guidance: Newborn: breastfeeding support, safe sleep (ABCs — alone/back/crib), jaundice monitoring, vitamin D 400 IU/day. 2-6 months: tummy time, immunizations, car seat rear-facing. 6-12 months: introduction of solids (iron-rich foods first), dental care, choking hazards. 12-18 months: language development, limit screen time, switch to whole milk. 2-5 years: toilet training, social skills, dental visits, booster vaccines. School age: bullying, physical activity (60 min/day), nutrition.

The Rourke Baby Record (RBR) is the standard Canadian well-child visit guide used across most provinces, providing age-specific screening and guidance recommendations. CPS (Canadian Paediatric Society) recommends zero screen time for children <2 years and <1 hour/day for ages 2-5.

**Q:** What are the current Canadian screening recommendations for cervical, breast, colorectal, and lung cancer?

**A:** Cervical: ages 25-69, Pap q3yr (some provinces transitioning to HPV primary screening q5yr); discontinue at 70 if 3 consecutive negative results. Breast: ages 50-74, mammography q2-3yr (CTFPHC — no routine screening 40-49; however, some provinces offer it); shared decision-making 40-49. Colorectal: ages 50-74, FIT (fecal immunochemical test) q2yr or flexible sigmoidoscopy q10yr; high-risk: colonoscopy. Lung: ages 50-74 with ≥20 pack-year smoking history (current or quit <15yr), low-dose CT annually. Not recommended: routine PSA screening (shared decision-making).

CTFPHC (Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care) provides evidence-based screening guidelines. The breast cancer screening age recommendation is controversial — CTFPHC says 50+, but many provinces and advocacy groups recommend starting at 40 (Ontario expanded to 40 in 2024). FIT has replaced guaiac FOBT as the preferred stool test for CRC screening.

**Q:** What additional periodic health screening is recommended for Canadian adults beyond cancer screening?

**A:** Osteoporosis: DXA scan for women ≥65 and men ≥70 (earlier if risk factors — FRAX assessment); Diabetes: FPG or A1C q3yr starting age 40 (earlier if risk factors); AAA: one-time abdominal ultrasound for men aged 65-80 who have ever smoked; Hypertension: BP measurement at every visit; Dyslipidemia: lipid panel q5yr starting age 40 (earlier with risk factors); use Framingham Risk Score; Depression: screen when risk factors present (CTFPHC does not recommend universal screening); Hepatitis C: one-time screening for those born 1945-1975 (baby boomer cohort); STI: screen based on risk factors.

CTFPHC guidelines differ from USPSTF in some areas — notably, CTFPHC does not recommend universal depression screening, while USPSTF does. AAA screening is specific to male ever-smokers — the only population with evidence of mortality benefit. FRAX and CAROC tools guide osteoporosis screening and treatment decisions in Canada.

**Q:** Describe the 5A's approach to smoking cessation and the pharmacotherapy options available in Canada.

**A:** 5A's: Ask (about tobacco use at every visit), Advise (clear, personalized advice to quit), Assess (readiness to quit — stages of change), Assist (develop quit plan + pharmacotherapy), Arrange (follow-up within 1-2 weeks). Pharmacotherapy: NRT (patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, nasal spray — OTC, can combine patch + short-acting); Varenicline (Champix): partial nicotinic agonist — most effective single agent, 12-week course, titrate over 1 week; Bupropion (Zyban): start 1-2 weeks before quit date, 150 mg BID x 7-12 weeks. Combination: varenicline + NRT may be more effective than either alone.

Varenicline has the highest quit rates (~30% at 1 year vs ~15% NRT vs ~7% placebo). CAN-ADAPTT (Canadian guideline) recommends offering pharmacotherapy to all patients who want to quit. The 'black box warning' for neuropsychiatric effects was removed by Health Canada — varenicline is safe in psychiatric patients. Even brief physician advice (3 minutes) increases quit rates by 30%.

**Q:** How is obesity classified and what are the current management approaches including pharmacotherapy and surgical criteria?

**A:** BMI classification: 18.5-24.9 normal, 25-29.9 overweight, 30-34.9 class I obesity, 35-39.9 class II, ≥40 class III (severe). Management: Lifestyle (all patients): caloric deficit 500-750 kcal/day, 150+ min/wk moderate activity, behavioral counseling. Pharmacotherapy (BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with comorbidity): liraglutide 3.0 mg/day (Saxenda), semaglutide 2.4 mg/wk (Wegovy) — both GLP-1 agonists with 10-15% weight loss; orlistat (lipase inhibitor, modest effect). Bariatric surgery (BMI ≥40, or ≥35 with comorbidity): Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy — 25-35% weight loss, sustained.

Obesity Canada clinical practice guidelines (2020) emphasize obesity as a chronic disease requiring long-term management, not simply 'eat less, move more.' GLP-1 agonists have transformed obesity pharmacotherapy with unprecedented weight loss. Bariatric surgery remains the most effective intervention for severe obesity and can resolve type 2 diabetes in 60-80% of patients.

**Q:** How is cardiovascular risk assessed in Canada and when should statin therapy be initiated?

**A:** Risk assessment: Framingham Risk Score (FRS) or modified FRS — estimates 10-year CVD risk. Low <10%, moderate 10-19%, high ≥20%. Statin indications (CCS 2021): 1) Clinical atherosclerosis (secondary prevention — always treat); 2) FRS ≥20% (high risk); 3) LDL ≥5.0 mmol/L (familial hypercholesterolemia likely); 4) Diabetes age ≥40 or with microvascular disease; 5) CKD (eGFR <60, >3 months); 6) FRS 10-19.9% with LDL ≥3.5 or additional risk enhancers. ASA for primary prevention: NO LONGER recommended (bleeding risk outweighs benefit per CCS guidelines).

CCS (Canadian Cardiovascular Society) 2021 dyslipidemia guidelines align with the shift away from treating to LDL targets toward risk-based statin intensity. High-risk patients should receive high-intensity statins (atorvastatin 40-80 mg or rosuvastatin 20-40 mg). ASA is no longer recommended for primary CVD prevention — a significant change from prior practice that is commonly tested.

**Q:** What is the current NACI-recommended adult immunization schedule in Canada?

**A:** Influenza: annually for all adults (especially ≥65, chronic disease, healthcare workers, Indigenous). Tdap: one dose in adulthood if not previously received, then Td q10yr; Tdap in each pregnancy (27-32 weeks). Pneumococcal: PNEU-P-23 for all ≥65 and high-risk adults; PNEU-C-20 (PCV20) emerging as single-dose option replacing sequential PCV15+PNEU-P-23. Shingles: Shingrix (recombinant, 2 doses) for all ≥50 years and immunocompromised ≥18. COVID-19: per current NACI guidance (evolving). HPV: catch-up up to age 26 (or 27-45 shared decision). Hepatitis B: if not vaccinated in childhood and at risk.

NACI (National Advisory Committee on Immunization) provides Canadian-specific vaccine recommendations. Shingrix replaced Zostavax — it is non-live (safe in immunocompromised) with >90% efficacy. Tdap in pregnancy is recommended every pregnancy regardless of interval. PCV20 is simplifying pneumococcal vaccination by replacing the previous two-step approach.

**Q:** What are the key considerations in a return-to-work assessment and fitness-to-work evaluation?

**A:** Return-to-work (RTW): assess functional capacity (physical demands of job vs patient's abilities); graduated RTW (modified duties, reduced hours); barriers (pain, psychological, workplace conflict); prognosis and expected recovery timeline; workplace accommodations (ergonomic, schedule); coordinate with employer and occupational therapist. Fitness-to-work: evaluate if medical condition poses risk to self, coworkers, or public; assess specific job demands (safety-sensitive positions require higher threshold); document limitations clearly and objectively; duty to accommodate (employer's legal obligation under Canadian human rights legislation); physician should NOT act as gatekeeper — focus on functional abilities rather than diagnosis.

Canadian physicians have a dual responsibility: advocate for the patient while providing objective medical information. The duty to accommodate to the point of 'undue hardship' is a key Canadian legal principle. Workers' compensation (WSIB in Ontario, WCB in other provinces) claims require specific functional capacity documentation.

**Q:** What workplace injury reporting obligations exist for Canadian physicians?

**A:** Physicians must: 1) Provide a First Medical Report to the provincial Workers' Compensation Board (WCB/WSIB/WorkSafeBC etc.) for any work-related injury/illness; 2) Report occupational diseases to public health where required (e.g., asbestosis, silicosis); 3) Document mechanism of injury, diagnosis, functional limitations, treatment plan, and expected duration of disability; 4) Provide ongoing progress reports. Physicians should NOT: disclose unrelated medical history to the employer; determine fault or causation (that is the Board's role); provide return-to-work clearance without adequate assessment.

Workers' compensation is a no-fault provincial system in Canada — workers cannot sue employers for workplace injuries in exchange for guaranteed benefits. Physician reporting is legally required and fees for WCB reports are set by provincial fee schedules. Privacy legislation (PIPEDA/provincial) limits what medical information can be shared with employers — only functional limitations, not diagnoses.

## Multimorbidity & Chronic Disease Management

**Q:** A 72-year-old man has HTN, T2DM, CKD stage 3, and osteoarthritis. He takes metformin, ramipril, amlodipine, and naproxen. Which medication should be stopped first?

**A:** Naproxen

NSAIDs worsen CKD, raise BP, increase CV risk, and antagonize antihypertensive agents. In multimorbid elderly patients, NSAIDs are high-priority deprescribing targets. Acetaminophen is preferred for OA pain.

**Q:** What framework is most useful for prioritizing treatment goals in patients with multimorbidity?

**A:** Patient-centred goal-setting using shared decision-making

Guidelines designed for single diseases may conflict in multimorbid patients. Shared decision-making to identify the patient's priorities (e.g., functional independence vs. longevity) helps tailor care. The NICE multimorbidity guideline recommends this approach.

**Q:** Which validated tool helps identify potentially inappropriate medications in older adults with multimorbidity?

**A:** STOPP/START criteria (Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions / Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment)

STOPP lists explicit criteria for medications to stop; START lists evidence-based medications to consider adding. They are more sensitive than the Beers Criteria for detecting drug-related hospital admissions in older Canadians.

**Q:** A 68-year-old woman with heart failure (EF 35%), T2DM, and CKD stage 3b (eGFR 38) is started on an SGLT2 inhibitor. What additional benefit does this provide beyond glycemic control?

**A:** Reduction in HF hospitalizations and slowing of CKD progression

SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) reduce HF hospitalization and CV death in HFrEF and slow CKD progression independent of glycemic effect. They are now guideline-directed therapy for both HFrEF and CKD.

**Q:** What is the primary goal of the "4Ms" framework used in age-friendly health systems for older adults with multimorbidity?

**A:** To focus care on What Matters, Medication, Mentation, and Mobility

The 4Ms framework (IHI/AHA) ensures care addresses patient priorities (What Matters), avoids harmful medications, manages cognitive/mood issues (Mentation), and maintains Mobility — all simultaneously across conditions.

**Q:** A 75-year-old with COPD, depression, and T2DM has poor medication adherence. Which intervention most improves adherence in multimorbid patients?

**A:** Medication reconciliation plus simplified regimens (dose consolidation, pill organizers, blister packs)

Reducing pill burden, synchronizing refills, and using compliance aids significantly improve adherence. A pharmacist-led medication review is effective and recommended in Canadian practice.

**Q:** Which condition is most commonly associated with multimorbidity as an "anchor" diagnosis in primary care?

**A:** Hypertension

HTN co-occurs with DM, CKD, CVD, stroke, and atrial fibrillation more than any other condition. It serves as the central node in most multimorbidity clusters seen in Canadian family practice.

**Q:** A 70-year-old man with AF, CKD stage 4 (eGFR 22), and HTN requires anticoagulation. Which agent is preferred?

**A:** Warfarin (vitamin K antagonist) with careful INR monitoring

DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban) are generally avoided when eGFR <15–25 mL/min due to renal elimination. Warfarin remains the anticoagulant of choice in advanced CKD with AF, though apixaban has emerging evidence down to eGFR 25.

**Q:** What is "treatment burden" in the context of multimorbidity?

**A:** The effort required of patients to carry out recommended treatments across multiple conditions

Treatment burden includes pill-taking, monitoring, lifestyle modifications, appointments, and costs. High treatment burden is associated with non-adherence and poorer outcomes in multimorbid patients.

**Q:** A 65-year-old woman with T2DM and CKD stage 3 develops hypertension. Which drug class is first-line for both nephroprotection and BP control?

**A:** ACE inhibitor or ARB

ACEi/ARBs reduce intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria in diabetic nephropathy. They are first-line for CKD with proteinuria per Hypertension Canada and Diabetes Canada guidelines.

**Q:** Which mental health condition is most prevalent in patients with multimorbidity and significantly worsens outcomes?

**A:** Depression

Depression occurs in 20–30% of patients with two or more chronic conditions. It impairs self-management, medication adherence, and is independently associated with increased mortality in patients with CVD, DM, and COPD.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to statin therapy in a frail 82-year-old with CVD, dementia, and limited life expectancy?

**A:** Consider deprescribing statins if the patient's life expectancy is short and QoL is the priority

Statins provide primary/secondary prevention over 3–5+ year horizons. In frail elderly with dementia and limited life expectancy, the harms (myopathy, interactions, pill burden) may outweigh benefits. Shared decision-making and goals of care should guide deprescribing.

**Q:** A 78-year-old with HF, CKD, and T2DM has hemoglobin of 95 g/L. What is the most likely cause?

**A:** Anemia of chronic kidney disease (renal anemia)

CKD causes decreased erythropoietin production. In patients with multimorbidity involving CKD, renal anemia is common. Check iron studies first; if iron replete, consider erythropoiesis-stimulating agents if eGFR <60 and Hb <100.

**Q:** What is the role of a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) in multimorbid older adults?

**A:** To evaluate functional status, cognition, mental health, nutrition, medications, and social support to create an integrated care plan

CGA identifies reversible contributors to functional decline, guides appropriate investigation intensity, and prioritizes interventions. It is recommended for frail older adults with multiple chronic conditions.

**Q:** A 73-year-old man with COPD, HF, and anxiety is prescribed a beta-blocker for HF. What is the appropriate approach given his COPD?

**A:** Use a cardioselective beta-blocker (e.g., bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate) — COPD is not an absolute contraindication

Cardioselective beta-blockers have minimal effect on beta-2 receptors at low doses. The mortality benefit of beta-blockers in HFrEF outweighs risk of mild COPD exacerbation. They should not be withheld in clinically stable COPD.

**Q:** What is polypharmacy and why is it a concern in multimorbid patients?

**A:** Polypharmacy: >=5 concurrent medications. Problematic polypharmacy: more medications than clinically indicated. Risks: ADRs (doubled with >=5 meds), falls, cognitive impairment, non-adherence, drug interactions

~30% Canadian seniors on 5+ medications. Prescribing cascades: drug A causes side effect treated by drug B. Apply Beers Criteria and STOPP/START criteria. Deprescribing.org provides Canadian evidence-based deprescribing guidelines.

**Q:** What are the principles of deprescribing?

**A:** 1) Identify medications to discontinue. 2) Prioritize by harm risk. 3) Plan tapering schedule. 4) Monitor for withdrawal/disease recurrence. 5) Shared decision-making with patient. Use deprescribing.org algorithms

Key targets: PPIs (taper over 4-8wk), benzodiazepines (10% reduction q1-2wk), anticholinergics, opioids, antipsychotics in elderly. Deprescribing reduces falls, improves cognition, reduces ADRs. STOPP criteria identify potentially inappropriate prescriptions.

**Q:** How should diabetes be managed in the context of multimorbidity?

**A:** Individualize A1c targets: <=7.0% generally, <=8.0% or <=8.5% for frail elderly with limited life expectancy. Avoid hypoglycemia. Prefer agents with low hypo risk (metformin, SGLT2i, GLP1-RA). Simplify regimens

Diabetes Canada 2018. SGLT2i (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) have CV and renal benefits. GLP1-RA reduce CV events. Deprescribe sulfonylureas and insulin where possible in frail elderly. Renal dosing: metformin to eGFR 30, SGLT2i to eGFR 20-25.

**Q:** How is chronic pain managed in patients with multiple comorbidities?

**A:** Multimodal approach: non-pharmacological first (exercise, CBT, physiotherapy). Pharmacological: acetaminophen, topical NSAIDs, duloxetine/gabapentinoids for neuropathic. Avoid systemic NSAIDs if CKD/CV/GI risk. Opioids last resort

Canadian Pain Guidelines 2017 emphasize non-opioid multimodal analgesia. If opioids needed: lowest effective dose, reassess regularly, naloxone co-prescribing if >=50 MME/day. Monitor with PMP (provincial prescription monitoring programs).

**Q:** What is the approach to treatment burden in multimorbid patients?

**A:** Assess patient capacity vs treatment workload. Minimize visits, simplify regimens, coordinate care, set realistic goals. Use ARIADNE principles: assess interactions, rank priorities, individualize, decide together, evaluate regularly

Treatment burden includes medications, appointments, monitoring, lifestyle changes, self-management tasks. Minimally disruptive medicine framework. Single-disease guidelines may not apply. Focus on patient-centered outcomes not disease-specific targets.

**Q:** How is hypertension managed in patients with CKD and diabetes?

**A:** Target <130/80 per Hypertension Canada. First-line: ACEi or ARB (renal protection). Monitor creatinine and K+ at 1-2 weeks. Accept up to 30% rise in creatinine. Add thiazide-like or CCB as needed

Do not combine ACEi+ARB (ONTARGET trial: no benefit, more harm). Hold ACEi/ARB for acute illness (sick day rules). SGLT2i provide additional renal and CV benefit. Avoid NSAIDs (worsen CKD and HTN). Refer nephrology if eGFR <30 or rapidly declining.

**Q:** What drug interactions should be monitored in multimorbid patients on anticoagulation?

**A:** Warfarin: CYP2C9 interactions (amiodarone increases INR, rifampin decreases). DOACs: P-gp inhibitors (amiodarone, dronedarone increase levels). All anticoagulants + NSAIDs/ASA = increased bleeding. Check renal function for DOACs

Common dangerous interactions: macrolides + warfarin, antifungals + DOACs, SSRIs increase bleeding risk. Dose-adjust DOACs for renal function and age/weight. INR monitoring for warfarin q4-12wk when stable. Fall risk alone is NOT a contraindication to anticoagulation.

**Q:** How is heart failure managed in patients with concurrent COPD?

**A:** Cardioselective beta-blockers (bisoprolol, metoprolol) are SAFE in COPD-HF. Avoid non-selective (carvedilol with caution). LAMA/LABA preferred over ICS for COPD. Avoid systemic corticosteroids if possible (fluid retention)

Beta-blockers reduce HF mortality and should NOT be withheld for COPD. Cardioselective agents at appropriate doses do not significantly worsen FEV1. Short-acting beta-agonists safe in HF. Diuretics for volume management must balance COPD dehydration.

**Q:** What are the Beers Criteria and STOPP/START criteria?

**A:** Beers: list of potentially inappropriate medications in >=65 (AGS updated 2023). STOPP: screening tool for older persons prescriptions to STOP. START: screening tool to START indicated but omitted medications

Beers high-risk drugs: anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, NSAIDs, long-acting sulfonylureas, meperidine, first-gen antipsychotics. STOPP/START more applicable to Canadian practice than Beers alone. Both tools reduce ADRs and inappropriate prescribing in elderly.

**Q:** How should chronic kidney disease be managed in primary care?

**A:** Classify by eGFR and albuminuria (KDIGO). ACEi/ARB for proteinuria. SGLT2i (dapagliflozin/empagliflozin) for eGFR 20-90 with albuminuria. BP <130/80. Avoid nephrotoxins. Finerenone for DKD

Refer nephrology: eGFR <30, rapid decline (>5/yr), nephrotic proteinuria, refractory HTN, electrolyte abnormalities. Adjust medications for GFR. Avoid gadolinium if eGFR <30. Annual monitoring: eGFR, ACR, electrolytes, CBC, PTH, vitamin D.

**Q:** What is the approach to managing depression in patients with chronic medical illness?

**A:** SSRIs first-line (sertraline/escitalopram). Consider drug interactions (CYP2D6 inhibitors, bleeding with anticoagulants). Avoid TCAs in cardiac disease. Combine with CBT/behavioral activation. Address pain/sleep

Depression occurs in 20-30% of chronic disease patients and worsens outcomes. Treat comorbid depression to improve adherence and quality of life. Exercise is evidence-based adjunct. Collaborative care models improve outcomes. Screen with PHQ-9.

**Q:** How is atrial fibrillation managed in elderly multimorbid patients?

**A:** Anticoagulation: CHADS2-VASc score >=2 (most elderly). DOACs preferred over warfarin (less ICH). Rate control preferred: bisoprolol or diltiazem (not both). Target HR <100 resting. Avoid rhythm control if asymptomatic

Falls are NOT a reason to withhold anticoagulation (need 295 falls/year for bleeding risk to exceed stroke prevention benefit). Dose-adjust DOACs: apixaban 2.5mg BID if >=2 of: age>=80, weight<=60kg, Cr>=133. Assess bleeding risk (HAS-BLED) but do not use to withhold therapy.

**Q:** What are the sick day rules for patients on chronic medications?

**A:** Hold during acute illness: ACEi/ARB, diuretics, NSAIDs, metformin, SGLT2i, sulfonylureas (SADMANS mnemonic). Resume when eating/drinking normally. Educate patients proactively

SADMANS: Sulfonylureas, ACEi, Diuretics/Direct-acting anticoagulants, Metformin, ARBs, NSAIDs, SGLT2i. Prevents AKI, hypoglycemia, hyperkalemia during volume depletion. Especially important in elderly, CKD, HF. Provide written instructions.

**Q:** How is the patient with diabetes, CKD, and heart failure optimally treated pharmacologically?

**A:** Metformin (if eGFR>30) + SGLT2i (triple benefit: glycemic, renal, cardiac) + ACEi/ARB + beta-blocker (bisoprolol/carvedilol/metoprolol) + MRA if symptomatic HF + statin. Finerenone if DKD

SGLT2i (empagliflozin/dapagliflozin) have evidence in all three conditions (EMPA-REG, DAPA-CKD, DAPA-HF). Quadruple HFrEF therapy: ACEi/ARB/ARNI + BB + MRA + SGLT2i. Adjust doses for renal function. Monitor K+ closely with ACEi+MRA combination.

**Q:** What role does advance care planning play in multimorbidity management?

**A:** Early discussions about goals of care, values, acceptable outcomes. Document SDM/POA. Use Serious Illness Conversation Guide. Reassess with disease progression. Integrate palliative approach early

Advance Care Planning Canada provides tools. Discussions improve goal-concordant care, reduce unwanted ICU admissions, improve family satisfaction. Not just about resuscitation status - explores values, fears, hopes. GCD (Goals of Care Designation) standardized in many provinces.

**Q:** What is the approach to chronic pain management using non-opioid strategies?

**A:** Multimodal: exercise/PT, CBT/psychological therapy, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, gabapentinoids, TCAs/SNRIs, topical agents, interventional procedures. Opioids last resort with monitoring

Canadian Pain Task Force guidelines. Chronic pain affects 1 in 5 Canadians. Risk assessment before opioids: current substance use, mental health, sleep apnea. Opioid agreements, urine drug testing, naloxone prescription.

**Q:** What is the SBAR handover communication framework?

**A:** Situation (patient ID, chief concern), Background (relevant history), Assessment (current status, vitals), Recommendation (what you need/suggest)

Reduces communication errors during handovers. I-PASS alternative: Illness severity, Patient summary, Action list, Situation awareness, Synthesis by receiver. Standardized handover reduces adverse events.

**Q:** What are the roles of interprofessional team members in primary care?

**A:** NP: autonomous diagnosis/prescribing. Pharmacist: medication management/review. PT: mobility/rehab. OT: functional/ADL assessment. SW: psychosocial/resources. RD: nutrition. Psych: mental health

Patient-centred medical home model. Collaborative practice improves chronic disease outcomes. Scope of practice varies by province. Physician as consultant/collaborator, not gatekeeper.

**Q:** What is the Beers Criteria and what are the key medications to avoid in elderly?

**A:** AGS Beers: potentially inappropriate medications in >=65. Avoid: benzodiazepines (falls, confusion), anticholinergics (delirium), NSAIDs long-term (GI bleed, renal), sulfonylureas (hypoglycemia), first-gen antihistamines (sedation)

STOPP/START complementary tool. START identifies beneficial drugs often underused in elderly (e.g., osteoporosis treatment, anticoagulation for AF). Deprescribing.org provides tapering algorithms.

**Q:** What are the roles and scope of practice of a Nurse Practitioner (NP) in Canadian primary care?

**A:** NPs in Canada can independently assess patients, order and interpret diagnostic tests, diagnose conditions, prescribe medications (including controlled substances in most provinces), perform procedures within their scope, and refer to specialists. Their scope varies by province but generally enables them to provide comprehensive primary care independently.

NP scope of practice is regulated provincially. In Ontario, NPs are authorized prescribers under the Nursing Act. NP-led clinics have expanded across Canada to address primary care shortages, particularly in rural and underserved areas. NPs hold graduate-level education and national certification through the Canadian Nurses Association.

**Q:** What is the SBAR communication tool, and when is it used in healthcare?

**A:** SBAR stands for: Situation (what is happening now), Background (relevant clinical context), Assessment (what you think the problem is), Recommendation (what you think should be done). It is used for structured clinical handover, escalation of patient concerns, and interprofessional communication, particularly during urgent situations.

SBAR was adapted from the US Navy for healthcare use and is mandated by Accreditation Canada as a standardized communication tool. Research shows SBAR reduces communication errors during handovers and improves patient safety. It is especially useful when nursing staff communicate concerns to physicians about deteriorating patients.

**Q:** What is the I-PASS handover system, and what does each component stand for?

**A:** I-PASS stands for: Illness severity (stable/watcher/unstable), Patient summary (brief clinical summary), Action list (to-do items and timeline), Situation awareness and contingency planning (what to watch for, 'if-then' plans), and Synthesis by receiver (read-back and clarifying questions). It was developed to reduce medical errors during transitions of care.

The I-PASS study demonstrated a 30% reduction in medical errors after implementation in pediatric residency programs. It has been adopted across Canadian academic centres. Unlike SBAR (which is for escalation), I-PASS is specifically designed for shift-to-shift patient handover and includes a synthesis step requiring active receiver participation.

**Q:** A pharmacist on the interprofessional team identifies a potential drug interaction in a patient's medication list and contacts you. What is the pharmacist's scope within the Canadian healthcare team?

**A:** Canadian pharmacists can review medication therapy, identify drug interactions and adverse effects, recommend medication adjustments, provide medication counselling, administer vaccinations (in most provinces), prescribe for minor ailments (expanding across provinces), adapt prescriptions (e.g., change dose or formulation), and conduct medication reconciliation.

Pharmacist scope has expanded significantly across Canadian provinces. In Alberta, pharmacists can prescribe independently for a broad range of conditions. Pharmacist-led medication reconciliation is a Required Organizational Practice by Accreditation Canada. Collaborative pharmacy practice has been shown to improve chronic disease outcomes, particularly for diabetes and hypertension.

**Q:** A conflict arises between the attending physician and a nurse regarding a patient's pain management plan. What approaches to conflict resolution are recommended in healthcare teams?

**A:** Recommended approaches include: addressing the conflict directly and respectfully, focusing on the patient's best interest rather than professional hierarchy, using 'I' statements, active listening, seeking to understand the other's clinical perspective, involving a neutral third party if needed, and using graded assertiveness tools like CUS (Concerned-Uncomfortable-Safety issue) for escalation.

The Canadian Patient Safety Institute (now Healthcare Excellence Canada) and Accreditation Canada emphasize psychological safety in healthcare teams, where all members feel safe to speak up. The CUS framework provides escalating language for safety concerns: 'I am Concerned,' 'I am Uncomfortable,' 'This is a Safety issue.' Hierarchical silence contributes to preventable adverse events.

**Q:** What is the Patient's Medical Home (PMH) model endorsed by the College of Family Physicians of Canada?

**A:** The PMH is a model of primary care featuring: a personal family physician, team-based care, timely access, comprehensive scope of services, continuity of care, electronic medical records, patient participation in practice governance, and accountability through quality improvement. It is the CFPC's vision for strengthening primary care in Canada.

The College of Family Physicians of Canada (CFPC) developed the Patient's Medical Home model as Canada's adaptation of the patient-centred medical home concept. It aligns with the Starfield principles of primary care (first-contact access, longitudinality, comprehensiveness, coordination). Provinces are funding primary care teams and interprofessional models such as Ontario's Family Health Teams to realize this vision.

**Q:** Differentiate the roles of physiotherapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), and social work (SW) in the rehabilitation of a stroke patient.

**A:** PT focuses on mobility, gait, balance, strength, and motor recovery. OT addresses activities of daily living (dressing, bathing, cooking), cognitive rehabilitation, home modifications, and adaptive equipment. SW provides psychosocial support, facilitates access to community resources, assists with discharge planning, caregiver support, and navigates financial/insurance issues.

Interprofessional stroke rehabilitation is standard of care in Canadian Stroke Best Practice Recommendations (Heart & Stroke Foundation). Clear understanding of each discipline's scope prevents duplication and ensures comprehensive patient care. The CanMEDS Collaborator competency specifically tests understanding of team member roles.

**Q:** What is scope of practice overlap, and how should it be managed in interprofessional teams?

**A:** Scope of practice overlap occurs when multiple professionals are authorized to perform similar activities (e.g., both NPs and physicians can prescribe). It should be managed through: clear team agreements defining roles, mutual respect for competencies, collaborative practice agreements, patient-centred task allocation based on availability and expertise, and regular team communication.

In Canada, the Regulated Health Professions Act (Ontario) and equivalent legislation in other provinces defines scopes of practice, but overlapping controlled acts are common. The CanMEDS Collaborator competency emphasizes that physicians must work effectively within teams, negotiate role clarity, and avoid turf conflicts that compromise patient care.

**Q:** A Physician Assistant (PA) is working in your emergency department. What is the PA's scope of practice in Canada?

**A:** PAs in Canada work in a dependent practice model under physician supervision. They can perform history and physical examinations, order and interpret tests, formulate diagnoses, develop treatment plans, perform procedures, and assist in surgery. Their scope is determined by their supervising physician's scope and the practice setting. PAs are not independently licensed in most provinces.

The PA profession in Canada is relatively newer compared to the US. The Canadian Association of Physician Assistants (CAPA) and the Canadian PA Education Association (CanPAEA) support the profession. PAs are increasingly employed in Canadian Forces, emergency departments, and surgical services. Manitoba was the first province to formally regulate PAs.

**Q:** During a complex discharge planning meeting, the interprofessional team disagrees on whether a patient is safe to go home. What is the recommended approach?

**A:** The team should: hold a structured meeting with all disciplines present, allow each member to share their assessment using their professional expertise, focus on objective functional criteria and safety risks, involve the patient and family in the discussion, document each discipline's recommendations, and develop a compromise plan with appropriate community supports and follow-up if discharge proceeds.

Accreditation Canada's Required Organizational Practices include safe transitions of care. The CanMEDS Collaborator role requires physicians to participate in, and contribute to, interprofessional team decisions rather than unilaterally overriding them. When disagreement persists, a patient-centred approach that weighs autonomy against safety should guide the final decision.

**Q:** What are the STOPP criteria and how are they applied in medication review for elderly patients?

**A:** STOPP (Screening Tool of Older People's Prescriptions) identifies potentially inappropriate prescribing in patients >=65. Examples: long-acting benzodiazepines (fall risk), anticholinergics with dementia, NSAIDs with CKD or heart failure, PPI >8 weeks without indication. Applied systematically during comprehensive medication review.

STOPP/START criteria are endorsed by Canadian Geriatrics Society for medication optimization. STOPP identifies drugs to discontinue; START identifies drugs that should be initiated but are missing. Application reduces adverse drug events by 35% and healthcare costs. Should be applied at every care transition.

**Q:** What are the START criteria? Give 3 examples of commonly missed medications in elderly patients.

**A:** START (Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment) identifies medications that should be prescribed but are omitted. Examples: 1) Statin for documented CVD (unless end-of-life). 2) ACEi/ARB for heart failure with reduced EF. 3) Bisphosphonate for osteoporosis with fragility fracture. 4) Anticoagulation for AF with CHA2DS2-VASc >=2.

Prescribing omission is as clinically significant as inappropriate prescribing. START criteria complement STOPP by identifying under-treatment. Canadian Geriatrics Society emphasizes that therapeutic nihilism in elderly patients leads to preventable morbidity. Each omission should be evaluated against individual goals of care.

**Q:** What is a systematic approach to deprescribing in elderly patients?

**A:** 1) Identify all medications and indications. 2) Assess ongoing need (original indication still present?). 3) Evaluate risk-benefit in current context (life expectancy, goals of care). 4) Prioritize discontinuation (highest risk, least benefit first). 5) Taper gradually (especially benzodiazepines, opioids, antidepressants). 6) Monitor for withdrawal/disease recurrence.

Canadian Deprescribing Network provides evidence-based algorithms for PPIs, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, and cholinesterase inhibitors. Deprescribing reduces falls, hospitalizations, and mortality in frail elderly. Patient engagement is essential; the Bruyere Research Institute deprescribing guidelines are free Canadian resources.

**Q:** What non-opioid approaches should be considered for chronic non-cancer pain management?

**A:** Pharmacologic: acetaminophen, NSAIDs (short-term), SNRIs (duloxetine), TCAs (low-dose amitriptyline/nortriptyline), gabapentinoids, topical agents (capsaicin, lidocaine, diclofenac). Non-pharmacologic: exercise, CBT, physiotherapy, acupuncture, mindfulness-based stress reduction, self-management programs.

Canadian guideline for opioid prescribing (2017) recommends optimizing non-opioid therapies before considering opioids. Exercise has the strongest evidence for chronic low back pain and osteoarthritis. Multidisciplinary pain programs show superior outcomes. SNRIs are first-line for neuropathic pain and fibromyalgia.

**Q:** How should treatment priorities be established in a patient with multiple chronic conditions?

**A:** 1) Identify patient goals and values (what matters most). 2) Assess disease burden and prognosis. 3) Evaluate treatment complexity and interaction potential. 4) Prioritize conditions causing most symptoms/functional impairment. 5) Apply minimally disruptive medicine principles. 6) Reassess priorities regularly as conditions evolve.

The Ariadne principles guide multimorbidity management: assess interactions, prioritize patient preferences, individualize treatment, and reassess regularly. NICE multimorbidity guidelines recommend against applying single-disease guidelines additively. Canadian Family Physician literature emphasizes shared decision-making and treatment burden reduction.

**Q:** What are the key drug-drug interactions to watch for in elderly multimorbid patients?

**A:** QTc prolongation (antipsychotics + SSRIs + domperidone), hyperkalemia (ACEi + spironolactone + potassium supplements), bleeding (warfarin + NSAIDs/SSRIs), serotonin syndrome (SSRI + tramadol + triptans), anticholinergic burden (multiple anticholinergics), hypoglycemia (sulfonylurea + fluoroquinolone).

Anticholinergic burden is a major concern — the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale quantifies cumulative risk. Canadian Geriatrics Society recommends calculating anticholinergic burden and reducing it when possible. Electronic prescribing alerts help but clinical judgment remains essential.

**Q:** What is the evidence-based approach to deprescribing proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)?

**A:** Step 1: Confirm ongoing indication (Barrett's esophagus, severe GERD, chronic NSAID use with risk factors = continue). Step 2: If no ongoing indication, taper over 4-8 weeks (reduce dose by 50% every 1-2 weeks). Step 3: Switch to H2RA or on-demand PPI. Step 4: Non-pharmacologic measures (elevate HOB, avoid triggers).

Canadian Deprescribing Network PPI algorithm is evidence-based and widely used. Long-term PPI risks include C. difficile, hypomagnesemia, osteoporotic fractures, and vitamin B12 deficiency. Rebound acid hypersecretion occurs in ~50%, peaking at 2 weeks — gradual taper minimizes this.

**Q:** When should the frailty index be applied and how does it influence management decisions?

**A:** Apply when considering treatment intensity in elderly patients. Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS, 1-9): 1-3 = fit, 4-5 = vulnerable/mildly frail, 6-7 = moderately/severely frail, 8-9 = very severely frail/terminally ill. Frailty influences BP targets (less aggressive), glycemic targets (relaxed), cancer screening (may discontinue), and surgical risk assessment.

The Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale is widely used in Canadian practice. CFS >=5 is associated with increased mortality, hospitalization, and adverse drug reactions. Canadian Cardiovascular Society uses frailty to modify anticoagulation decisions. Frailty assessment is mandatory in Canadian LTC admission protocols.

**Q:** What are Choosing Wisely Canada recommendations relevant to elderly multimorbid patients?

**A:** Don't prescribe benzodiazepines for insomnia in elderly. Don't order routine screening tests in patients with limited life expectancy. Don't use antipsychotics as first-line for BPSD in dementia. Don't prescribe antibiotics for asymptomatic bacteriuria. Don't order ECG as routine screening in asymptomatic patients.

Choosing Wisely Canada aims to reduce low-value care. The Canadian Geriatrics Society list specifically addresses overtreatment and overinvestigation in elderly. Engaging patients in these conversations using shared decision-making tools improves outcomes and reduces harm from unnecessary interventions.

**Q:** How should chronic kidney disease be managed in the context of multimorbidity in elderly patients?

**A:** Avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, gadolinium if eGFR <30, aminoglycosides). Adjust renally-cleared medications (metformin hold if eGFR <30, DOACs dose-adjusted, gabapentin reduced). ACEi/ARB for proteinuria. SGLT2i for CKD with or without diabetes (if eGFR >=20). BP target <130/80 per Hypertension Canada. Monitor potassium.

KDIGO and Canadian guidelines recommend SGLT2 inhibitors for CKD regardless of diabetes status based on DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY trials. Medication dose adjustment is the most common preventable adverse event in CKD. Finerenone is a newer agent for diabetic CKD with cardiovascular benefit.

**Q:** What are the four principles of family medicine as defined by the College of Family Physicians of Canada?

**A:** 1. The family physician is a skilled clinician (expert generalist, collaborates on management of undifferentiated disease, multimorbidity, chronic disease). 2. Family medicine is a community-based discipline (adapts to community needs, lifespan care, multidisciplinary collaboration). 3. The family physician is a resource to a defined practice population (self-directed learning, health advocacy, resource stewardship). 4. The patient-physician relationship is central (whole-person care in family/social context, continuity, privacy, and longitudinal relationship).

These four principles distinguish family medicine from specialty medicine and underpin the MCCQE1 CanMEDS-Family Medicine competency framework. Family physicians are described as 'experts in generalism' with a commitment to person-centred, holistic, integrated care.

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis of fatigue using the PS VINDICATE mnemonic?

**A:** P-Psychogenic (depression, anxiety, CFS, fibromyalgia); Physiologic (pregnancy, caregiving); S-Sleep disturbance (OSA, BPH, shiftwork); Sedentary lifestyle; V-Vascular (stroke); I-Infectious (mono, hepatitis, HIV, TB); N-Neoplastic; Nutrition (anemia); Neurogenic (MS, MG, Parkinson's); D-Drugs (β-blockers, antihistamines, benzodiazepines); I-Idiopathic; C-Chronic illness (HF, COPD, renal failure); A-Autoimmune (SLE, RA, PMR); T-Toxin (alcohol, heavy metals); E-Endocrine (hypothyroidism, DM, Cushing's, adrenal insufficiency).

Psychogenic causes are the most common (especially if <6 months duration). 50% of patients presenting with fatigue have associated psychological complaints. Physical causes usually have associated signs/symptoms on focused history and exam.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS)?

**A:** Requires ALL 3: (1) Substantial reduction in activity from pre-illness level, lasting >6 months, with new-onset fatigue not alleviated by rest, not from excessive exertion; (2) Post-exertional malaise*; (3) Unrefreshing sleep*. PLUS at least one of: cognitive impairment* OR orthostatic intolerance. (*frequency and severity must be evaluated)

CFS (also called ME/CFS — myalgic encephalomyelitis) is a diagnosis of exclusion. It affects mostly women in their 30s and White individuals. Etiology is unknown but likely multifactorial (infectious, immunological, neurohormonal). Exercise therapy may have positive effects on fatigue but evidence is limited.

**Q:** What are the key investigations for unexplained fatigue?

**A:** CBC and differential, electrolytes, BUN, Cr, ESR, glucose, TSH, ferritin, vitamin B12, serum protein electrophoresis, Bence-Jones protein, albumin, LFTs (AST, ALT, ALP, bilirubin), calcium, phosphate, ANA, β-hCG. Also: urinalysis, CXR, ECG. Additional: serologies (Lyme, hepatitis B/C, HIV, ANA), Mantoux skin test.

Investigation should be guided by history and physical exam. Psychosocial causes are common — minimal investigation is usually warranted unless physical cause is suspected. After 1 year, 40% of patients with fatigue are no longer fatigued.

## Geriatrics & Elder Care

**Q:** What are the five domains of the FRAIL scale used to screen for frailty in older adults?

**A:** Fatigue, Resistance (stair climbing), Ambulation (walking), Illnesses (>5 comorbidities), Loss of weight (>5%)

The FRAIL scale is a validated, quick screening tool. Scores 0=robust, 1–2=pre-frail, 3–5=frail. It predicts falls, hospitalization, and mortality in community-dwelling older adults.

**Q:** A 79-year-old woman falls at home. Which standardized test best assesses her dynamic balance and fall risk?

**A:** Timed Up and Go (TUG) test

TUG measures time to rise from a chair, walk 3 m, turn, and return. >12 seconds indicates fall risk. The Canadian Falls Prevention Curriculum recommends TUG for all community-dwelling older adults after a fall.

**Q:** Which drug class is the most common pharmacological contributor to falls in older adults?

**A:** Psychotropics (benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, antidepressants)

Psychotropics cause sedation, orthostatic hypotension, and impaired coordination. Benzodiazepines double fall risk. Deprescribing is a priority in fall prevention per the AGS Beers Criteria and STOPP criteria.

**Q:** What Mini-Cog score suggests possible cognitive impairment requiring further evaluation?

**A:** Score of 0–2 out of 5 (0–3 recall words + clock drawing)

The Mini-Cog combines 3-word recall (1 point each) and clock drawing (0 or 2 points). A score ≤2 suggests possible dementia and warrants further evaluation with MoCA or formal neuropsychological testing.

**Q:** An 80-year-old man is admitted post-hip fracture. On day 2 he becomes acutely confused, agitated, and disoriented. What is the diagnosis and key precipitant to address?

**A:** Delirium; address pain, urinary retention, constipation, medications, and infection

Delirium affects 14–56% of hospitalized older adults. Post-operative pain under-treatment is a key precipitant in hip fracture patients. Use the CAM tool to confirm. Non-pharmacological multicomponent prevention is first-line.

**Q:** Which calcium and vitamin D daily doses are recommended for fracture prevention in adults over 70 years?

**A:** Calcium 1200 mg/day (dietary preferred) + Vitamin D 800–2000 IU/day

Osteoporosis Canada recommends 1200 mg calcium and at least 800 IU vitamin D daily for those ≥50 years, with up to 2000 IU/day in elderly or institutionalized individuals. Calcium supplements may modestly increase CV risk.

**Q:** What is the DAST-10 used for in geriatric patients?

**A:** Screening for drug/substance use problems (Drug Abuse Screening Test — 10 items)

DAST-10 identifies problematic substance use including prescription opioids, benzodiazepines, and alcohol. Substance misuse is underdiagnosed in older adults and contributes to falls, delirium, and cognitive decline.

**Q:** A 77-year-old woman with a recent hip fracture has a T-score of -2.7. She is on vitamin D and calcium. What pharmacological therapy should be started?

**A:** Bisphosphonate (e.g., alendronate or zoledronic acid)

Post-fracture patients with T-score ≤-2.5 meet criteria for pharmacological osteoporosis therapy. Zoledronic acid IV annually is effective post-hip fracture and reduces mortality. Alendronate 70 mg weekly is an oral option.

**Q:** What is the recommended screening interval for cognitive impairment in adults ≥65 in Canadian primary care?

**A:** Annual assessment using a validated tool (e.g., MoCA, Mini-Cog) in those with risk factors; no routine universal screening recommended by CTFPHC

The Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (CTFPHC) does not recommend routine cognitive screening of asymptomatic adults. However, case-finding in those with memory complaints, vascular risk factors, or functional decline is appropriate.

**Q:** Which scale quantifies severity of functional impairment in activities of daily living (ADLs) in older adults?

**A:** Katz Index of ADLs (bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding)

The Katz ADL Index scores 6 basic self-care functions. It is used to assess functional status, plan care transitions, and monitor disease progression. Lawton-Brody IADL scale assesses instrumental ADLs (cooking, finances, etc.).

**Q:** An 85-year-old nursing home resident is refusing meals and has lost 8% of body weight over 3 months. What is the first step in management?

**A:** Comprehensive assessment for reversible causes (dental problems, dysphagia, depression, medications, pain, constipation)

Malnutrition in older adults is often multifactorial. Reversible causes must be excluded before considering appetite stimulants or feeding tubes (which are rarely appropriate in advanced dementia). MNA (Mini Nutritional Assessment) can quantify risk.

**Q:** Which antihypertensive target is recommended for community-dwelling adults ≥75 years according to Hypertension Canada?

**A:** SBP <130 mmHg if tolerated (based on SPRINT trial data)

SPRINT trial showed benefit of intensive BP control (SBP <120 target) in adults ≥75 without diabetes. Hypertension Canada recommends SBP <130 mmHg in high-CV-risk elderly patients if tolerated, with close monitoring for orthostatic hypotension.

**Q:** What is urge urinary incontinence and what is first-line non-pharmacological management?

**A:** Involuntary urine leakage with sudden urge; first-line is bladder training and pelvic floor exercises

Urge incontinence (overactive bladder) is the most common type in elderly women. Bladder training (scheduled voiding, urge suppression) and pelvic floor physiotherapy are first-line before anticholinergics (which cause cognitive side effects in elderly).

**Q:** A 78-year-old man has a PMR (polymyalgia rheumatica) flare after prednisone taper. He also has osteoporosis. What bone protection should be prescribed?

**A:** Bisphosphonate (alendronate or zoledronic acid) plus calcium and vitamin D

Patients on glucocorticoids ≥7.5 mg/day for ≥3 months require bone protection. Osteoporosis Canada and ACR recommend bisphosphonate therapy with calcium/vitamin D supplementation to prevent glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis.

**Q:** What is advance care planning and when should it be initiated in older adults?

**A:** Ongoing process of discussing future healthcare goals, values, and preferences — ideally started early, before a health crisis

ACP includes identifying a substitute decision-maker, completing advance directives/personal directives, and discussing goals of care. Canadian guidelines recommend initiating ACP with all patients ≥65, especially those with life-limiting illness.

**Q:** What is the approach to polypharmacy in elderly?

**A:** Use STOPP/START criteria or Beers Criteria. Deprescribe: PPIs, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, statins in limited life expectancy. Use deprescribing.org algorithms

Polypharmacy: >=5 medications. Associated with falls, ADRs, hospitalizations, cognitive decline. Medstopper tool helps prioritize. Review at every visit.

**Q:** What distinguishes delirium from dementia?

**A:** Delirium: acute onset, fluctuating course, inattention, reversible. Dementia: insidious onset, progressive, attention preserved (early), irreversible

Delirium causes: infection, metabolic, drugs (anticholinergics, benzos, opioids), urinary retention, constipation. CAM tool for diagnosis. Treat underlying cause. Avoid restraints and benzos if possible.

**Q:** What is the comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA)?

**A:** Systematic evaluation of: medical (comorbidities, medications, nutrition), functional (ADLs, IADLs, mobility), psychological (cognition, mood, behavior), social (support, caregiver, housing, finances)

CGA improves outcomes: reduces mortality, institutionalization, and functional decline. Interdisciplinary team approach. Tools: Katz ADL index, Lawton IADL, MMSE/MoCA, GDS, Timed Up and Go. Canadian Geriatrics Society promotes CGA in acute and community settings.

**Q:** How are delirium and dementia differentiated clinically?

**A:** Delirium: acute onset, fluctuating, inattention, often reversible. Dementia: gradual onset, progressive, attention initially preserved, irreversible. Delirium can be superimposed on dementia

Delirium screening: CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) - requires acute change + inattention + EITHER disorganized thinking OR altered LOC. Dementia screening: MoCA (>=26 normal) or MMSE (>=24). Delirium is a medical EMERGENCY requiring cause identification.

**Q:** What are the common causes of delirium (mnemonic)?

**A:** DELIRIUM(S): Drugs, Electrolytes, Lack of drugs (withdrawal), Infection (UTI, pneumonia), Reduced sensory input, Intracranial event, Urinary retention/constipation, Myocardial/pulmonary event, (Subdural)

Drugs are the most common modifiable cause (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids). Non-pharmacological prevention: orientation, mobilization, sleep hygiene, hydration, avoid restraints. Avoid antipsychotics unless patient is danger to self/others.

**Q:** What is the approach to falls prevention in elderly patients?

**A:** Multifactorial assessment: gait/balance (TUG test), medications (review for culprits), vision, orthostatic BP, home hazards, footwear, vitamin D, strength/balance exercise (Otago program)

Falls affect 30% of >=65 annually, leading cause of injury-related death in elderly. High-risk meds: benzodiazepines, antihypertensives, anticholinergics, opioids, antipsychotics. Tai chi and Otago exercise program have best evidence. Home safety assessment by OT reduces falls.

**Q:** What are the stages and types of dementia?

**A:** Alzheimer (60-80%): memory first, gradual. Vascular: stepwise, executive dysfunction. Lewy body: visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, fluctuations. Frontotemporal: personality/behavior changes, younger onset. Staging: mild/moderate/severe by function

Diagnosis: clinical history + cognitive testing (MoCA) + labs to exclude reversible causes (TSH, B12, calcium, CBC, electrolytes, glucose) + CT/MRI. Alzheimer treatment: cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild-moderate, memantine for moderate-severe.

**Q:** What medications are used for dementia and what are their side effects?

**A:** Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil 5-10mg, rivastigmine, galantamine): nausea, diarrhea, bradycardia, syncope. Memantine (NMDA antagonist): for moderate-severe, better tolerated. Neither reverses or halts progression

Start low go slow. Modest benefit: ~2 points MMSE. Discontinue if no benefit after 3-6 months or severe stage. Monitor for bradycardia (caution with beta-blockers). Anticholinergic medications worsen cognition and should be discontinued.

**Q:** What is the approach to urinary incontinence in elderly?

**A:** Classify: stress (cough/sneeze), urge (detrusor overactivity), overflow (retention), functional (mobility/cognition). Assess: bladder diary, PVR, UA. Treat: pelvic floor exercises (stress), bladder training + antimuscarinics (urge)

Avoid oxybutynin in elderly (crosses BBB, cognitive impairment). Mirabegron (beta-3 agonist) preferred for urge incontinence in elderly. Timed voiding for functional incontinence. Address contributing factors: constipation, medications, caffeine, mobility.

**Q:** What is frailty and how is it assessed?

**A:** Frailty phenotype (Fried): >=3 of: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow gait, weak grip. Pre-frail: 1-2. Also: Clinical Frailty Scale (Rockwood) 1-9

Frailty predicts adverse outcomes: falls, disability, hospitalization, mortality. CFS is widely used in Canadian healthcare (used in COVID triage). Frailty is potentially modifiable with exercise, nutrition optimization, medication review, and social support.

**Q:** What are the principles of prescribing in elderly patients?

**A:** Start low, go slow, but get there. Review all medications regularly. Use Beers/STOPP/START criteria. Avoid anticholinergic burden (ACB score). Consider life expectancy in preventive therapy. Deprescribe proactively

Pharmacokinetic changes: decreased renal clearance, hepatic metabolism, increased body fat (longer half-life lipophilic drugs), decreased albumin (higher free drug). Pharmacodynamic: increased sensitivity to CNS depressants, anticholinergics, anticoagulants.

**Q:** How is elder abuse screened for and managed?

**A:** Types: physical, emotional, financial, sexual, neglect. Screen: direct questioning, look for unexplained injuries, poor hygiene, fearfulness. Report to APS (Adult Protective Services) per provincial legislation. Mandatory reporting varies by province

Elder abuse affects ~10% of Canadian seniors. Risk factors: social isolation, dementia, caregiver stress, financial dependence. Physicians should screen in private without caregiver present. Document findings carefully. Connect with community resources and social work.

**Q:** What is the approach to malnutrition in elderly patients?

**A:** Screen with MNA (Mini Nutritional Assessment). Causes: poor dentition, depression, medications, poverty, functional limitations, malabsorption. Target protein 1-1.2 g/kg/day. Supplement vitamin D. Address reversible causes

Malnutrition affects 30-50% of hospitalized elderly. Consequences: poor wound healing, immune dysfunction, muscle wasting (sarcopenia), increased mortality. Oral nutritional supplements improve outcomes. Dietitian referral. Address social determinants (food insecurity, Meals on Wheels).

**Q:** What is the approach to driving assessment in elderly patients with cognitive impairment?

**A:** MoCA <18 or significant functional impairment: consider unfit. Refer for comprehensive driving evaluation. Mandatory reporting of medically unfit drivers varies by province (mandatory in AB, ON; discretionary in BC, QC)

CMA Physician Guide to Determining Fitness to Drive. Dementia per se does not preclude driving - functional assessment needed. Provincial reporting obligations: physicians may/must report. Consider progressive nature. Patient safety vs autonomy. Discuss transportation alternatives.

**Q:** What is the palliative approach to care in geriatrics?

**A:** Early integration of palliative principles alongside disease-directed therapy. Symptom management (pain, dyspnea, nausea, delirium). Goals of care conversations. Caregiver support. Hospice referral when appropriate

Palliative approach differs from end-of-life care - can be concurrent with curative treatment. In Canada hospice and palliative care is publicly funded. ESAS (Edmonton Symptom Assessment System) for symptom monitoring. Opioids for dyspnea and pain are appropriate at end of life.

**Q:** What are the criteria for and management of behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)?

**A:** Non-pharmacological first: identify triggers, routine, music therapy, redirection, caregiver education. Pharmacological only for severe agitation/psychosis: risperidone 0.25-1mg (only approved antipsychotic for BPSD in Canada). Time-limited trial

Antipsychotics carry black-box warning for increased mortality in elderly dementia (1.6-1.7x). Attempt taper after 3 months stability. Avoid benzodiazepines (paradoxical agitation, falls). Trazodone for sleep. Assess for pain, constipation, infection as causes.

**Q:** What is the Choosing Wisely Canada approach to geriatric care?

**A:** Key recommendations: do not use antipsychotics first-line for BPSD, avoid benzodiazepines for insomnia in elderly, do not screen with PSA >70, avoid screening tests when life expectancy <10y, do not use PPI long-term without reassessment

Choosing Wisely Canada promotes evidence-based reduction of unnecessary tests and treatments. Particularly relevant in elderly where harms of interventions may exceed benefits. Each specialty society contributes top 5 recommendations. Promotes shared decision-making.

**Q:** What are the functional assessments used in geriatric rehabilitation?

**A:** ADLs (Katz Index): bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding. IADLs (Lawton): phone, shopping, cooking, housekeeping, laundry, transport, medications, finances

Functional decline predicts mortality and institutionalization. Comprehensive geriatric assessment includes: medical, functional, cognitive (MoCA/MMSE), mood (GDS), nutrition (MNA), social supports, falls risk, polypharmacy.

**Q:** What is the Timed Up and Go test for falls risk?

**A:** Patient rises from chair, walks 3m, turns, walks back, sits. >=12 seconds = increased falls risk. Part of multifactorial falls assessment

Falls assessment: gait/balance (TUG), vision, medications (sedatives, antihypertensives), orthostatic BP, home hazards, footwear, vitamin D, bone density. Multifactorial intervention reduces falls 20-30%.

**Q:** What are the types and staging of pressure injuries?

**A:** Stage 1: non-blanchable erythema. Stage 2: partial thickness (blister/shallow ulcer). Stage 3: full thickness (subcutaneous visible). Stage 4: full thickness with exposed bone/tendon/muscle. Unstageable: covered by eschar

Braden Scale assesses risk (sensory, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, friction). Prevention: reposition q2h, pressure-redistributing surfaces, nutrition optimization, moisture management.

**Q:** What are the types of urinary incontinence and first-line treatments?

**A:** Stress: leaks with cough/sneeze — pelvic floor exercises (Kegels), pessary, surgery. Urge: sudden urge, frequency — bladder training, antimuscarinic (oxybutynin) or mirabegron. Overflow: incomplete emptying — treat obstruction, intermittent catheterization

Mixed incontinence common. Functional: mobility/cognitive barriers. Avoid antimuscarinics in elderly (cognitive decline, dry mouth, constipation). Mirabegron (beta-3 agonist) safer in elderly.

**Q:** What are the major risk factors for falls in the elderly, and what screening tool is used for fall risk assessment?

**A:** Risk factors: previous falls, muscle weakness, gait/balance impairment, polypharmacy (especially sedatives, antihypertensives), visual impairment, cognitive impairment, orthostatic hypotension, environmental hazards. Screening: Timed Up and Go (TUG) test — abnormal if >12 seconds.

Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death and hospitalization in Canadians over 65. The TUG test assesses functional mobility by timing a patient rising from a chair, walking 3 meters, turning, and returning. A multifactorial approach addressing all identified risk factors is more effective than single interventions.

**Q:** What is the multifactorial intervention approach for fall prevention in elderly patients?

**A:** Exercise program (balance + strength training), medication review (reduce/stop sedatives, antihypertensives), vision correction, home safety assessment, vitamin D supplementation (if deficient), management of orthostatic hypotension, footwear assessment, assistive device fitting.

The Otago Exercise Programme (strength and balance exercises) reduces falls by ~35% in community-dwelling elderly. Vitamin D (at least 800 IU/day) reduces falls in those who are deficient. Deprescribing psychotropic medications is one of the highest-yield interventions. Hip protectors may reduce hip fracture risk in institutionalized patients.

**Q:** An 82-year-old presents after a fall with groin pain, shortened and externally rotated leg. What is the likely diagnosis, and what is the management approach?

**A:** Hip fracture (femoral neck or intertrochanteric). Management: pain control, IV fluids, preoperative medical optimization (within 24-48 hours ideally), surgical fixation (displaced femoral neck: hemiarthroplasty or THA in active patients; intertrochanteric: sliding hip screw or IM nail).

Hip fractures carry 30-day mortality of ~5-10% and 1-year mortality of ~20-30%. Surgery within 48 hours reduces mortality, delirium, and pressure injuries. Nondisplaced femoral neck fractures may be treated with internal fixation (screws). All hip fracture patients should be assessed for osteoporosis and started on treatment.

**Q:** What are the types of urinary incontinence, and how do you differentiate stress from urge incontinence clinically?

**A:** Stress: involuntary leakage with cough, sneeze, exertion (weak pelvic floor/urethral sphincter). Urge: sudden compelling desire to void followed by involuntary leakage (detrusor overactivity). Overflow: incomplete emptying with dribbling (obstruction or detrusor underactivity). Functional: physical or cognitive barriers to reaching toilet.

Stress incontinence is most common in women (post-childbirth, menopause) and treated with pelvic floor exercises (Kegels) first-line, then surgical sling procedures. Urge incontinence is treated with bladder training, antimuscarinic agents (oxybutynin, tolterodine), or beta-3 agonist (mirabegron). Antimuscarinics should be avoided in elderly due to anticholinergic burden.

**Q:** What is the initial workup for urinary incontinence in the elderly?

**A:** History (type, frequency, severity, triggers), voiding diary, medication review (diuretics, anticholinergics, alpha-blockers), urinalysis (rule out UTI, hematuria), post-void residual (rule out overflow/retention), cognitive and functional assessment.

Transient causes of incontinence can be remembered with the mnemonic DIAPPERS: Delirium, Infection, Atrophic vaginitis, Pharmaceuticals, Psychological, Excess fluid input, Restricted mobility, Stool impaction. Addressing reversible causes should precede invasive workup or treatment. Urodynamic studies are reserved for refractory or complex cases.

**Q:** What are the stages of pressure injuries, and what defines an unstageable pressure injury?

**A:** Stage I: intact skin with non-blanchable erythema. Stage II: partial-thickness skin loss (shallow open ulcer or blister). Stage III: full-thickness skin loss (fat visible, no bone/tendon). Stage IV: full-thickness tissue loss with exposed bone, tendon, or muscle. Unstageable: full-thickness loss covered by slough or eschar preventing staging.

Deep tissue pressure injury (DTPI) is a newer category presenting as intact skin with persistent deep red/maroon/purple discoloration that may rapidly deteriorate. The sacrum, heels, and ischial tuberosities are the most common sites. Prevention with repositioning every 2 hours and pressure-redistribution surfaces is more effective than treatment.

**Q:** What is the Braden Scale, and how is it used in pressure injury prevention?

**A:** The Braden Scale assesses 6 risk factors: sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction/shear. Scored 6-23; lower scores = higher risk. Score ≤18: at risk; ≤12: high risk; ≤9: very high risk.

The Braden Scale should be completed on admission and regularly reassessed. Interventions for high-risk patients include repositioning every 2 hours, pressure-redistribution mattresses, nutritional optimization (protein, calories), moisture management, and heel elevation. Incontinence-associated dermatitis increases risk and should be managed with barrier creams.

**Q:** What is the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), and how does it guide clinical decision-making?

**A:** The CFS is a 9-point scale from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill). Score ≥5 (mildly frail) identifies patients at increased risk of adverse outcomes (falls, disability, hospitalization, death). It is used to guide goals of care discussions, surgical risk assessment, and resource allocation.

Frailty is distinct from disability and comorbidity — it represents decreased physiological reserve. CFS ≥5 is associated with higher ICU mortality and is used in some triage frameworks. Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) with targeted interventions can improve outcomes in frail patients, including reduced hospital readmissions and functional decline.

**Q:** What are the components of a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)?

**A:** Medical: comorbidities, medications, nutrition, continence, pain. Functional: ADLs (Katz Index), IADLs (Lawton Scale), mobility, falls risk. Cognitive: MMSE or MoCA, delirium screening. Psychological: depression (GDS), anxiety. Social: caregiver support, living situation, finances, advance directives.

CGA has been shown to increase the likelihood of patients being alive and in their own homes at 12 months following hospitalization. It is most beneficial for frail patients who are not too well (no benefit) or too sick (futile). CGA should be performed by an interdisciplinary team including geriatrician, nurse, social worker, physiotherapist, and occupational therapist.

**Q:** What are the types of elder abuse, and what are the risk factors and screening considerations?

**A:** Types: physical, psychological/emotional, financial/material, sexual, neglect (including self-neglect). Risk factors: social isolation, caregiver stress/substance abuse, cognitive impairment, functional dependence. Screen by interviewing patient alone; ask direct questions in a non-judgmental manner.

Elder abuse affects approximately 10% of older Canadians. Financial abuse is the most common type. All provinces have adult protection legislation, though mandatory reporting requirements vary by jurisdiction. Clinicians should be alert to unexplained injuries, malnutrition, poor hygiene, fearful behavior around caregivers, and unusual financial transactions.

**Q:** What interventions prevent deconditioning in hospitalized elderly patients?

**A:** Early mobilization (within 24 hours of admission), minimize bed rest orders, remove unnecessary tethers (IVs, catheters, telemetry), physical therapy consult, avoid benzodiazepines and anticholinergics, adequate nutrition/protein, maintain day-night orientation, promote independence in ADLs.

Hospitalized elderly lose ~5% of muscle strength per day of bed rest. Deconditioning leads to prolonged hospitalization, falls, institutionalization, and death. The Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) reduces delirium incidence by 40% and includes mobility protocols, cognitive stimulation, sleep enhancement, and nutrition/hydration support.

**Q:** What is polypharmacy, and what tools are used to identify potentially inappropriate medications in the elderly?

**A:** Polypharmacy: use of ≥5 medications concurrently. Tools: Beers Criteria (AGS list of potentially inappropriate medications in older adults), STOPP/START criteria (screening tool for potentially inappropriate prescriptions and for prescriptions that should be started). Common offenders: benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, NSAIDs, first-generation antihistamines.

Polypharmacy increases risk of adverse drug events, drug interactions, falls, cognitive impairment, and non-adherence. Deprescribing should be systematic — prioritize stopping medications with highest risk and lowest benefit. The deprescribing.org algorithms provide evidence-based guidance for tapering PPIs, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, and other commonly overused medications.

**Q:** What is the approach to wound care for Stage III and IV pressure injuries?

**A:** Debride necrotic tissue (sharp, enzymatic, or autolytic), manage bacterial bioburden (topical antiseptics if colonized, systemic antibiotics only for cellulitis/sepsis), maintain moist wound environment (hydrogels, foams, alginates), optimize nutrition (protein 1.25-1.5 g/kg/day, vitamin C, zinc), offload pressure, reassess regularly.

The TIME framework guides wound management: Tissue (debridement), Infection/Inflammation (bioburden control), Moisture (balance), Edge (wound edge advancement). Negative pressure wound therapy (VAC) can promote granulation in large Stage III/IV wounds. Surgical flap closure may be considered for recurrent or non-healing wounds in appropriate candidates.

**Q:** An 85-year-old nursing home resident is found with unexplained bruising on the upper arms in different stages of healing. What should you consider, and what are the next steps?

**A:** Consider physical elder abuse. Next steps: document injuries in detail (size, location, color, stage), interview patient alone, assess for other signs (fear, depression, malnutrition), review for medical causes (coagulopathy, steroid use), report to adult protective services as required by provincial legislation.

Bruising in unusual locations (inner arms, trunk, face) or in different stages suggests non-accidental injury. Patterned injuries (hand, belt, ligature marks) are highly suspicious. Medical causes of easy bruising (anticoagulants, thrombocytopenia, senile purpura) must be excluded. Documentation should be factual and non-judgmental, using body diagrams and photography if possible.

**Q:** What is overflow incontinence, and what are the common causes and management?

**A:** Overflow incontinence: involuntary leakage from an overdistended bladder that cannot empty properly. Causes: BPH (most common in men), diabetic neuropathy, medications (anticholinergics, opioids), spinal cord lesions, strictures. Management: treat underlying cause, intermittent catheterization, alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) or 5-alpha reductase inhibitors for BPH.

Post-void residual >200 mL confirms urinary retention. Acute retention requires catheter decompression. Chronic retention with overflow is often painless and may present with recurrent UTIs or renal impairment. Anticholinergics used for urge incontinence can worsen overflow incontinence, so PVR should be checked before starting these medications.

**Q:** What are the key elements of geriatric assessment in family medicine?

**A:** Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA): Functional: ADLs (bathing, dressing, feeding, toileting, transferring, continence) and IADLs (cooking, cleaning, finances, medications, transportation). Cognitive: screening with MoCA (preferred in Canada) or MMSE. Mood: PHQ-2/GDS (Geriatric Depression Scale). Mobility/Falls: Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, gait speed, fall history and risk factors. Nutrition: weight loss, BMI, MNA. Medications: polypharmacy review (Beers criteria/STOPP-START), deprescribing. Social: caregiver burden, driving safety, advance care planning, living situation, supports.

CGA has been shown to reduce mortality, institutionalization, and functional decline in frail elderly. MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) is preferred over MMSE in Canada — it is more sensitive for mild cognitive impairment and was developed in Montreal. The Canadian Deprescribing Network provides evidence-based deprescribing guidelines for common medications in the elderly.

**Q:** What are the principles of deprescribing in elderly patients and which medications are commonly targeted?

**A:** Principles: 1) Review all medications regularly; 2) Identify potentially inappropriate medications (Beers criteria, STOPP/START); 3) Prioritize deprescribing based on risk-benefit; 4) Taper gradually (avoid abrupt discontinuation); 5) Monitor for withdrawal effects and return of symptoms; 6) Involve patient/family in shared decision-making. Common targets: PPIs (>8 weeks without indication), benzodiazepines/Z-drugs, anticholinergics, antipsychotics (for BPSD without clear indication), statins (in limited life expectancy), sulfonylureas (hypoglycemia risk), NSAIDs (GI/renal/CV risk).

Polypharmacy (≥5 medications) affects ~40% of Canadian seniors and increases falls, adverse drug events, and hospitalization. The Canadian Deprescribing Network (deprescribing.org) provides free, evidence-based algorithms. Benzodiazepine deprescribing should reduce dose by ~10-25% every 2-4 weeks — abrupt discontinuation risks seizures.

**Q:** What are the Canadian guidelines for driving assessment in elderly patients and when must a physician report to the licensing authority?

**A:** Assessment: screen for conditions affecting driving (vision, cognition, medications, physical function); cognitive screen (MoCA <18 suggests impairment); review medications (sedatives, opioids, anticholinergics); DriveABLE or road test referral if uncertain. Mandatory reporting: physicians are legally required to report to provincial licensing authority when a patient has a medical condition that makes driving unsafe (dementia, seizures, vision loss, syncope, severe cardiac/respiratory/metabolic disease). Varies by province — in Ontario, mandatory reporting under Highway Traffic Act; in some provinces, permissive rather than mandatory.

This is a common ethical/legal exam topic. Physician reporting for fitness to drive overrides patient confidentiality and does not breach privacy legislation. Most provinces protect physicians from liability for good-faith reporting. The CMA Driver's Guide provides condition-specific medical standards for driving fitness across Canada.

**Q:** What are the key elements of advance care planning in Canadian family medicine?

**A:** Advance care planning (ACP): 1) Identify substitute decision-maker (SDM) — varies by province (power of attorney for personal care in Ontario, mandate in Quebec, representation agreement in BC); 2) Discuss values, goals, and preferences for care; 3) Document advance directives (living will); 4) Discuss specific scenarios (CPR, intubation, ICU, dialysis, feeding tubes); 5) Review and update regularly (change in health status, new diagnosis); 6) Ensure SDM understands their role (substituted judgment — what the patient WOULD want, not what SDM wants). POLST/Medical Orders for Scope of Treatment: physician-signed medical orders reflecting ACP conversations.

ACP is distinct from advance directives — ACP is the ongoing process of discussion, while advance directives are the written documents. In Canada, health care consent legislation is provincial. Speak Up Canada (advancecareplanning.ca) is the national framework promoted by the Canadian Hospice Palliative Care Association.

**Q:** What is the approach to falls assessment and prevention in elderly patients?

**A:** Assessment (multifactorial): History: number of falls, circumstances, witnesses, LOC, prodromal symptoms. Medications: sedatives, antihypertensives, anticholinergics, polypharmacy. Exam: orthostatic vitals, visual acuity, gait/balance (TUG test — abnormal >12 seconds), lower extremity strength, footwear, cognitive screen, cardiac exam. Investigations: CBC, electrolytes, glucose, vitamin D, ECG, consider Holter/echocardiogram if cardiac cause suspected. Prevention (evidence-based): exercise programs (tai chi, balance training — strongest evidence), vitamin D supplementation (≥1000 IU/day), medication review/deprescribing, home safety assessment, vision correction, management of orthostatic hypotension.

Falls affect ~30% of community-dwelling Canadians ≥65 annually and are the leading cause of injury-related hospitalization in seniors. The Canadian Falls Prevention guidelines recommend multifactorial risk assessment for all seniors who report a fall. Exercise is the single most effective intervention — reduces falls by 20-30%.

**Q:** What are the principles of palliative care in Canadian family medicine and how is Medical Assistance in Dying (MAiD) regulated?

**A:** Palliative care principles: symptom management (pain, dyspnea, nausea, delirium), psychosocial/spiritual support, advance care planning, caregiver support, home-based care when possible. MAiD (Criminal Code of Canada, 2016, amended 2021): Eligibility: ≥18 years, capable of making health decisions, grievous and irremediable medical condition causing enduring suffering, informed consent. Two tracks: Track 1 (natural death reasonably foreseeable): 10-day reflection period, 2 independent assessors. Track 2 (natural death NOT reasonably foreseeable): 90-day assessment period, one assessor must have expertise in the condition, must be informed of and offered alternative treatments. Sole mental illness: eligibility was extended but implementation has been delayed.

Canada has one of the most permissive MAiD frameworks globally. MAiD accounted for ~4.1% of deaths in Canada in 2022. Physicians have the right to conscientiously object but must provide effective referral. The expansion to sole mental illness remains controversial and has been deferred multiple times. Palliative care access remains uneven across Canadian provinces.

**Q:** What are the tips to reduce drug-related adverse events in elderly patients (prescribing principles)?

**A:** 1) Review longstanding medications for age/renal/hepatic adjustments. 2) Reassess drugs started in hospital. 3) Deprescribe unnecessary/harmful medications. 4) Verify adherence before dose escalation. 5) Prefer high therapeutic index drugs. 6) Reconcile medications to avoid duplication.

Elderly patients are at higher risk for ADRs due to polypharmacy, altered pharmacokinetics (reduced renal/hepatic function, increased fat:muscle ratio), and pharmacodynamic changes. Systematic medication review and deprescribing are key Canadian geriatric care principles.

**Q:** What age-related skin changes occur and what conditions do they predispose to?

**A:** With aging: epidermis attenuates (rete ridge effacement), keratinocytes lose proliferative ability, dermis loses volume, hyaluronic acid diminishes. Leads to: senile purpura and androgenic alopecia.

Senile (actinic) purpura presents as non-blanchable red-to-purple patches resolving over 1–3 weeks, leaving brown-yellow haemosiderin discolouration. These changes reflect loss of skin integrity from regenerative decline.

**Q:** What is asteatotic dermatitis and how is it managed?

**A:** Diffuse mild pruritic dermatitis secondary to dry skin ('dried river bed' appearance on shins). Very common in the elderly, especially in winter ('winter itch'). Management: skin rehydration with moisturizing routine (products with urea) + topical corticosteroids (TCS).

Asteatotic dermatitis results from loss of skin surface lipids, leading to a cracked, fissured pattern especially on lower legs. Environmental humidity control and emollients are preventive.

**Q:** What PHE screening is recommended for adults over 65 years, specifically regarding cognitive impairment and falls?

**A:** Adults >65: Follow up on caregiver concern of cognitive impairment (Grade A). Multidisciplinary post-fall assessment (Grade A). Hearing impairment screening (inquiry, whispered voice test, audioscope, Grade C).

Geriatric preventive care focuses on functional outcomes. Cognitive impairment screening is triggered by caregiver concern (not routine MMSE in the absence of concern). Fall prevention is a critical safety issue for older adults, requiring multidisciplinary assessment including medication review, vision, gait assessment, and home safety evaluation.

**Q:** What are the 5 criteria of the Physical Frailty Phenotype (Fried criteria), and how many are needed to diagnose frailty?

**A:** Criteria: (1) Shrinking – unintentional weight loss ≥10 lbs or ≥5% body weight in prior year; (2) Weakness – grip strength in lowest 20% by gender/BMI; (3) Poor endurance – self-reported exhaustion; (4) Slowness – walking time for 15 feet in slowest 20% by gender/height; (5) Low activity – kcals/week in lowest 20% (males <383, females <270 kcal/wk). Frail = ≥3 criteria; Pre-frail/at-risk = 1–2 criteria.

The Fried phenotype is widely used in geriatric assessment. Pre-frail patients are targets for preventive intervention. The MCCQE1 may test the number of criteria required for diagnosis or the specific components.

**Q:** What is the Cumulative Deficit Approach (Rockwood frailty index)?

**A:** The frailty index = number of deficits present / number of deficits possible. It balances assets (health, attitudes, resources, caregiver support) against deficits (illness, disability, dependence, caregiver burden) to determine whether a person can maintain independence in the community.

The Rockwood Cumulative Deficit model conceptualizes frailty as accumulation of deficits across multiple domains rather than a specific phenotype. It complements the Fried phenotype model.

**Q:** What are the key immune and endocrine mechanisms underlying frailty?

**A:** Immune: elevated IL-6, CRP, WBCs, monocytes; elevated clotting markers (factor VIII, fibrinogen, D-dimer) → chronic inflammation → skeletal muscle decline. Endocrine: sarcopenia via decreased GH and IGF-1, increased cortisol, decreased DHEA-S, decreased vitamin D; stress dysregulation of autonomic nervous system; age-related changes in renin-angiotensin system and mitochondria.

Understanding the pathophysiology of frailty helps identify modifiable targets. These inflammatory and hormonal changes contribute to the sarcopenia central to the frailty syndrome.

**Q:** What does CGA stand for in geriatrics, and what are its components?

**A:** CGA = Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment. Components include: past medical history, medications, allergies, social history, function, and geriatric review of systems (cognition, mood, sleep, pain, nutrition, falls, continence, vision/hearing, skin, and safety); physical exam; investigations (CBC, electrolytes, TSH, vitamin B12, vitamin D, LFTs, extended electrolytes).

CGA is the cornerstone of geriatric medicine. It informs management of geriatric syndromes and guides interdisciplinary care. Knowing its components is essential for MCCQE1.

**Q:** What are the DIAPERS transient causes of urinary incontinence in older adults?

**A:** D – Delirium; I – Infection (urinary); A – Atrophic urethritis/vaginitis; P – Pharmaceuticals; E – Excessive urine output; R – Restricted mobility; S – Stool impaction.

DIAPERS is a classic mnemonic for reversible/transient causes of incontinence in older adults. Addressing these causes can often resolve incontinence without further workup.

**Q:** What are the 4 subtypes of urinary incontinence?

**A:** (1) Stress incontinence – leakage with physical strain (e.g., coughing, sneezing); (2) Urge incontinence – leakage with oversensitive bladder and strong urge to urinate; (3) Overflow incontinence – leakage from poor bladder emptying (e.g., BPH, neurogenic bladder); (4) Functional incontinence – leakage due to illness/disability not related to the urinary tract (e.g., immobility, dementia).

Classifying the subtype guides management. Stress responds to pelvic floor exercises; urge to bladder training and antimuscarinics (used cautiously in elderly); overflow to alpha-blockers or catheterization; functional to environmental modifications.

**Q:** What is the prevalence of urinary incontinence in older adults, and what are its morbidities?

**A:** Prevalence: 15–30% community-dwelling older adults; ≥50% institutionalized. Morbidities: cellulitis, pressure injuries, UTIs, falls with fractures, sleep deprivation, social withdrawal, depression, sexual dysfunction. Importantly, UI is NOT associated with increased mortality.

Urinary incontinence is under-reported and under-treated. Its downstream complications (particularly falls/fractures) significantly impact older adult morbidity.

**Q:** What are the 3 subtypes of fecal incontinence?

**A:** (1) Passive incontinence – involuntary discharge of stool or gas without awareness; (2) Urge incontinence – discharge of fecal matter despite active attempts to retain bowel contents; (3) Fecal seepage – leakage of stool following otherwise normal evacuation.

Distinguishing subtypes guides management. Passive incontinence suggests internal sphincter dysfunction; urge incontinence suggests external sphincter or rectal hypersensitivity; seepage may relate to incomplete evacuation or rectal pathology.

**Q:** What is the MEALS ON WHEELS mnemonic for etiology of malnutrition in older adults?

**A:** M – Medications; E – Emotional problems (depression); A – Anorexia; L – Late-life paranoia; S – Swallowing disorders; O – Oral problems; N – Nosocomial infections; W – Wandering/dementia-related activity; H – Hyperthyroid/hypercalcemia/hypoadrenalism; E – Enteric disorders; E – Eating problems; L – Low salt/low fat diet; S – Social isolation/inability to shop.

MEALS ON WHEELS is a useful mnemonic for the many causes of malnutrition in elderly patients. Many causes are reversible, making a thorough assessment essential.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition per the 2018 Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM)?

**A:** Requires combination of ≥1 phenotypic criterion AND ≥1 etiologic criterion. Phenotypic: weight loss (>5% in 6 months or >10% beyond 6 months), low BMI, reduced muscle mass (by DXA, BIA, CT, or MRI). Etiologic: reduced food intake, or inflammation (from acute disease/injury or chronic disease).

The GLIM 2018 criteria standardize the definition of malnutrition and require both phenotypic and etiologic components. BMI concern thresholds in older adults: <23.5 in males and <22.0 in females.

**Q:** What investigations are ordered for malnutrition workup in older adults?

**A:** CBC, electrolytes, Ca²⁺/albumin, Mg²⁺, PO₄³⁻, creatinine, LFTs (INR, bilirubin), vitamin B12, folate, TSH, lipid profile. If indicated: urinalysis, ESR, CXR.

A broad metabolic panel helps identify reversible or contributing causes (e.g., hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, liver disease). Hypoalbuminemia (<38 g/L community, <35 g/L hospital) and hypocholesterolemia (<4.1 mmol/L) also support the diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the NPIAP staging criteria for pressure injuries (Stages 1–4, Unstageable, and Deep Tissue)?

**A:** Stage 1: Nonblanchable erythema of intact skin. Stage 2: Partial thickness skin loss with exposed dermis; viable pink/red moist wound bed ± serum blister. Stage 3: Full thickness skin loss; adipose visible; slough/eschar may be present. Stage 4: Full thickness skin and tissue loss; exposed fascia/muscle/tendon/ligament/cartilage/bone. Unstageable: Full thickness loss obscured by slough/eschar; depth cannot be determined. Deep Tissue Injury: Intact/non-intact skin with nonblanchable maroon/purple discoloration or blood-filled blister; results from intense/prolonged pressure at bone-muscle interface.

Accurate staging guides treatment decisions. Stage 1–2 can often be managed conservatively; Stages 3–4 may require debridement and surgical consultation. Deep tissue injuries may evolve rapidly.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for pressure injuries?

**A:** Extrinsic: friction, pressure, shear force, moisture. Intrinsic: immobility, malnutrition, comorbidities (DM, PVD, vasculitis, immunodeficiency, CHF), sensory loss. Geriatric-specific: age-related skin changes, being bedbound, cognitive impairment, chronic illness.

Identifying risk factors enables targeted prevention. Multiple validated tools (e.g., Braden Scale) assess pressure injury risk on admission.

**Q:** What is the recommended repositioning frequency to prevent pressure injuries in bed vs. chair?

**A:** In bed: reposition every 2 hours (q2h). In chair: reposition every 1 hour (q1h).

Frequent repositioning redistributes pressure and is the cornerstone of pressure injury prevention. Advanced static mattresses or overlays are also recommended by the American College of Physicians for high-risk patients.

**Q:** When is sharp debridement indicated for pressure injuries, and how does it differ from other debridement methods?

**A:** Sharp debridement is performed urgently when there is risk of sepsis or cellulitis. Other forms (mechanical, enzymatic, autolytic) are non-urgent. All ulcers with necrosis warrant debridement.

Debridement removes necrotic tissue to allow healing. The urgency depends on the clinical situation: infected wounds with systemic signs require urgent sharp debridement, while stable wounds can use slower methods.

**Q:** What are the infectious complications of pressure injuries?

**A:** Bacteremia/sepsis, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, sinus tracts, abscess, endocarditis, meningitis.

Pressure injuries can serve as portals of entry for serious infection. Osteomyelitis underlying a pressure injury may be difficult to diagnose and requires bone biopsy for definitive diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the key recommendations for preventing functional decline during hospitalization in older adults?

**A:** Early mobilization (for most complications), medication review (for delirium, falls, ADRs, urinary incontinence), orientation/sensory aids/volume repletion/noise reduction for delirium, daily inspection and q2h repositioning for pressure injuries, incentive spirometry/physiotherapy for respiratory function, structured exercise and progressive resistance training for functional decline, and removal of unnecessary IV lines/catheters/NG tubes for infection prevention.

Hospitalization itself is a risk factor for geriatric decline (Hospital-Associated Disability). Interdisciplinary strategies targeting these sequelae are standard of care in ACE units.

**Q:** What are the healthcare institution types for older adults in Canada, from most to least independent?

**A:** 1. Home and Community Support Services – independent living at home with professional/personal/homemaking support. 2. Retirement Home – fairly independent, minimal ADL/IADL support, often privately owned. 3. Supportive Housing – minimal to moderate assistance, independent living in apartment. 4. Long-Term Care / Skilled Nursing Facility – around-the-clock nursing, on-call physician, OT/PT/RT available. 5. Hospice – care for terminally ill with prognosis <3 months, focus on quality of life.

Matching the level of care to patient needs is a core geriatric competency. Choosing appropriate placement requires assessing functional capacity, social supports, and socioeconomic status.

**Q:** A 78-year-old woman living alone is found to have unintentional weight loss of 12 lbs over 6 months, grip strength in the lowest quartile for her age and BMI, self-reported exhaustion, and walks slowly. She scores 3/5 on the Fried frailty criteria. What is her frailty status and what management approach is indicated?

**A:** She meets criteria for FRAILTY (≥3 Fried criteria). Management: Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA); physical activity programs and nutritional optimization; multicomponent interventions; interdisciplinary primary care; medication optimization; referral to Acute Care for Elders (ACE) unit if hospitalized; caregiver support.

Frail older adults benefit from structured multicomponent interventions addressing physical activity, nutrition, and psychosocial support. Evidence from RCTs shows these reduce incident mobility disability in frail older adults with sarcopenia.

**Q:** What complications of immobility in older adults affect the cardiovascular, respiratory, and musculoskeletal systems?

**A:** Cardiovascular: orthostatic hypotension, venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism. Respiratory: decreased ventilation, atelectasis, pneumonia. MSK: muscle atrophy, contractures, bone loss (osteoporosis).

Immobility is a major contributor to hospital-associated disability. Early mobilization is the primary preventive strategy for most of these complications.

**Q:** What are the psychological and genitourinary complications of immobility in older adults?

**A:** Psychological: sensory deprivation, delirium, depression. Genitourinary: infection (UTI), urinary retention, bladder/renal calculi, incontinence. Also gastrointestinal: anorexia, constipation, incontinence, dehydration, malnutrition. Skin: pressure injuries.

These complications of immobility are interconnected — delirium worsens immobility, which worsens delirium. A multicomponent, interdisciplinary approach is needed to interrupt these cycles.

For patients with one or more first-degree relatives with CRC, colonoscopy should begin at age 50 or 10 years before the relative's diagnosis age.

**Q:** What is the oral temperature threshold for fever in the morning?

**A:** >37.2°C

Fever is defined as oral temperature >37.2°C in the morning or >37.7°C in the evening.

**Q:** What is the oral temperature threshold for fever in the evening?

**A:** >37.7°C

Fever is defined as oral temperature >37.2°C in the AM or >37.7°C in the PM.

**A:** All first-degree relatives should be considered for screening when a case is identified

Given the autosomal recessive inheritance, siblings have a 25% chance of being affected and should be screened.

## Family Medicine

**Q:** According to Canadian screening guidelines, who should be screened for lung cancer with low-dose CT (LDCT)?

**A:** Adults aged 55-74 with >=30 pack-year smoking history who currently smoke or quit <15 years ago should receive annual LDCT screening up to 3 consecutive times. Screening should only occur in settings with expertise in early diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer. Chest X-ray should NOT be used for lung cancer screening.

The Canadian Task Force (2016) makes a weak recommendation for LDCT in this high-risk group based on low-quality evidence. For all other adults regardless of age or smoking history, lung cancer screening is not recommended.

**Q:** What is the PHQ-2 screening tool for depression, and what score warrants further evaluation?

**A:** The PHQ-2 asks 2 questions about the past 2 weeks: (1) little interest or pleasure in doing things, and (2) feeling down, depressed, or hopeless. Each scored 0-3, total out of 6. A score >=3 is positive and should prompt evaluation with the PHQ-9 to determine if criteria for depression are met.

The Canadian Task Force does not recommend routine depression screening. When screening is indicated, PHQ-2 is used as a brief screen, with PHQ-9 for full assessment. All patients with depression should be screened for suicidal ideation. Combination pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy is the most effective treatment.

**Q:** What pharmacotherapy options are available for smoking cessation, and which is most effective?

**A:** Varenicline (Champix) is the most effective: 23% abstinent at 9-52 weeks vs. 16% bupropion vs. 9% placebo. Bupropion SR (Zyban) has similar effectiveness to NRT. NRT (gum, patch, inhaler, nasal spray) achieves 19.7% abstinence at 12 months vs. 11.5% for placebo. Combining counselling with medication is more effective than either alone.

Varenicline acts as a partial nicotinic receptor agonist (reduces cravings) and partial antagonist (reduces response to smoked nicotine). Side effects include nausea and neuropsychiatric symptoms. The quit date should be 10-14 days after starting. The 5 A's framework guides cessation counselling: Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange.

**Q:** A woman presents with vague complaints including headaches, chronic pain, and insomnia, with injuries inconsistent with her history. What diagnosis should you strongly suspect, and what is the strongest predictor of disclosure?

**A:** Intimate partner violence (domestic violence). Asking about abuse is the strongest predictor of disclosure.

Physician recognition rates for intimate partner violence are as low as 5%. Screening tools like WAST and HITS can help identify victims. Safety planning is critical as victims are most at risk for homicide when attempting to leave.

**Q:** A patient presents with fatigue lasting 8 months. Using the PS VINDICATE mnemonic, list the most common causes of fatigue in the family medicine setting.

**A:** Most common: Psychogenic (depression, anxiety, CFS), Sleep disturbance (OSA), Sedentary lifestyle, Infectious (viral), Drugs (beta-blockers, antihistamines), Chronic illness (HF, COPD), Endocrine (hypothyroidism, DM).

Psychosocial causes are the most common etiology of fatigue. About 50% have associated psychological complaints, especially if <6 months duration. One-third of patients have no identifiable cause. Initial workup includes CBC, TSH, ferritin, glucose.

**Q:** What are the three required symptoms for diagnosis of Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS) per the 2015 Institute of Medicine criteria?

**A:** 1) Substantial reduction in pre-illness activity lasting >6 months with fatigue not alleviated by rest; 2) Post-exertional malaise; 3) Unrefreshing sleep. Plus at least one of: cognitive impairment OR orthostatic intolerance.

CFS diagnosis requires all three core symptoms plus at least one additional manifestation. It is found in <5% of patients presenting with fatigue. There are no specific diagnostic lab tests. Exercise therapy may have positive effects.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome (IOM 2015)?

**A:** Three required: (1) substantial reduction in pre-illness activity lasting >6 months with new-onset fatigue not alleviated by rest, (2) post-exertional malaise, (3) unrefreshing sleep. PLUS at least one of: cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance.

CFS is found in <5% of patients presenting with fatigue. It is more common in women, White individuals, and those in their 30s. Treatment focuses on sleep hygiene, physical activity, and symptom management.

**Q:** According to the 2016 Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care, what is the recommended colorectal cancer (CRC) screening strategy for adults ages 60–74?

**A:** FOBT (gFOBT or FIT) every 2 years OR flexible sigmoidoscopy every 10 years. This is a STRONG recommendation with moderate quality evidence.

The 2016 CTFPHC guidelines differentiate between age groups. Ages 60–74 receive a strong recommendation for FOBT q2yr or flexible sigmoidoscopy q10yr, while ages 50–59 receive only a weak recommendation for the same tests. Colonoscopy is NOT recommended as a routine screening test for average-risk individuals.

**Q:** According to the 2016 CTFPHC, what are the CRC screening recommendations for adults ages 50–59?

**A:** FOBT (gFOBT or FIT) every 2 years OR flexible sigmoidoscopy every 10 years. This is a WEAK recommendation with moderate quality evidence.

The strength of recommendation differs between age groups: ages 60–74 get a strong recommendation, while ages 50–59 get only a weak recommendation for the same screening modalities. The lower strength reflects less certainty of net benefit in this younger group.

**Q:** According to the 2016 CTFPHC, what is the recommendation regarding CRC screening in adults ≥75 years?

**A:** Recommend NOT screening adults ≥75 years for CRC. This is a weak recommendation with low quality evidence.

The CTFPHC recommends against screening in adults 75 and older due to low quality evidence. The potential harms of screening (colonoscopy complications, false positives, overdiagnosis) outweigh the benefits in this age group given shorter life expectancy.

**Q:** According to the 2016 CTFPHC, what is the recommendation regarding the use of colonoscopy for CRC screening in average-risk patients?

**A:** Recommend NOT using colonoscopy as a screening test for CRC in average-risk patients. This is a weak recommendation with low quality evidence.

The CTFPHC specifically recommends against colonoscopy as a primary screening tool for average-risk individuals. This contrasts with U.S. guidelines. Instead, the CTFPHC endorses FOBT/FIT or flexible sigmoidoscopy. Colonoscopy is still used for diagnostic evaluation (e.g., symptomatic patients, positive FOBT) and for high-risk individuals (e.g., first-degree relative with CRC).

**Q:** Which CRC screening tests are NOT recommended by the CTFPHC for average-risk patients? List at least 4.

**A:** Tests NOT recommended for CRC screening: (1) Colonoscopy (for average-risk patients), (2) Metabolomic (blood or urine) tests, (3) DNA (blood or stool) tests, (4) CT colonography, (5) Capsule colonoscopy, (6) Double-contrast barium enema, (7) Guaiac FOBT (replaced by FIT).

The CTFPHC provides a clear list of tests that are not recommended for CRC screening. FIT (fecal immunochemical test) has replaced the older guaiac FOBT. CT colonography and capsule colonoscopy lack sufficient evidence for routine screening use. DNA and metabolomic tests are also not recommended.

**Q:** A 52-year-old woman has a father who was diagnosed with colorectal cancer at age 56. What is the appropriate CRC screening recommendation?

**A:** She is NOT at average risk. She should have a colonoscopy starting at age 50 OR 10 years earlier than the age her relative was diagnosed (i.e., age 46), whichever comes FIRST. Since she is already 52, she should have colonoscopy now. A complete family history should be taken; if hereditary cancer syndrome is suspected, refer for genetic assessment.

Having one or more first-degree relatives with CRC places a patient in the 'increased risk' category. The rule is to start colonoscopy at age 50 OR 10 years earlier than the relative's diagnosis age, whichever is first. FIT is NOT appropriate for symptomatic patients; additionally, colonoscopy is the preferred modality for increased-risk patients (not just average-risk screening). Hereditary syndromes (Lynch syndrome, FAP) should be considered.

**Q:** According to the 2013 Canadian Task Force for Preventive Care, what are the Pap smear/cervical cancer screening recommendations by age group?

**A:** • <20 years: DO NOT screen (strong recommendation, high quality evidence)
• 20–24 years: DO NOT routinely screen (weak recommendation, moderate quality evidence)
• 25–29 years: Pap smear or liquid cytology q3 years (weak recommendation, moderate quality evidence)
• 30–69 years: Pap smear or liquid-based cytology q3 years (strong recommendation, high quality evidence)
• ≥70 years, adequately screened (3 consecutive negative Paps in last 10 years): Routine screening may cease
• ≥70 years, not adequately screened: Continue until 3 negative test results obtained

The CTFPHC 2013 guidelines recommend against screening in women under 25 and limit screening to q3 years for those 25–69. This is based on the natural history of HPV infection, which often clears spontaneously in younger women, and the harms of unnecessary colposcopy/treatment in young women. Ontario's 2022 guidelines extend screening to women ≥25 who are or have ever been sexually active (or ≥21 if immunocompromised).

**Q:** According to the Ontario Cervical Screening Program (2022), when should cervical cancer screening begin and what is the recommended interval?

**A:** Women ≥25 years who are or have ever been sexually active: screen every 3 years if cytology is normal. Women ≥21 who are immunocompromised and are or have ever been sexually active may initiate screening earlier.

Ontario's 2022 program differs from the national CTFPHC 2013 guidelines (which recommended starting at 25 for all). Ontario allows screening at 21 for immunocompromised patients. Provincial guidelines can vary from national recommendations; knowing both is important for the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the 2014 CTFPHC recommendation regarding PSA-based prostate cancer screening?

**A:** • Ages <55: Do NOT screen with PSA (strong recommendation, low quality evidence)
• Ages 55–69: Do NOT screen with PSA (weak recommendation, moderate quality evidence); however, clinicians should discuss risks and benefits with each man based on his preferences
• Ages ≥70: Do NOT screen with PSA (strong recommendation, low quality evidence)

The CTFPHC recommends against routine PSA screening across all age groups because the potential small benefit is outweighed by significant harms: bleeding, infection, urinary incontinence, false positives, and overdiagnosis. For men 55–69 the evidence is inconsistent (small possible benefit vs. harms), hence the weaker recommendation that requires shared decision-making.

**Q:** Why does the CTFPHC recommend against PSA screening even for men aged 55–69?

**A:** Because the potential small benefit from PSA screening is outweighed by potential significant harms of screening and associated follow-up treatment, including: bleeding, infection, urinary incontinence, false positive results, and overdiagnosis/overtreatment.

For men <55 or ≥70, there is no evidence that PSA screening reduces mortality, whereas there is evidence of harms (strong recommendation against). For men 55–69, there is inconsistent evidence of a small potential benefit, hence only a weak recommendation against with a requirement for shared decision-making. Overdiagnosis is a major concern as many prostate cancers detected by PSA would never cause symptoms.

**Q:** What are the 6 stages of change in the Transtheoretical (Motivational) Model, and what is the physician's aim at each stage?

**A:** 1. Pre-contemplation: Encourage patient to consider possibility of change; raise issue sensitively, offer neutral information
2. Contemplation: Understand ambivalence, encourage change; discuss pros/cons using reflective listening
3. Preparation: Explore options; offer realistic options and discuss difficulties
4. Action: Help design rewards for success; offer positive reinforcement, explore coping strategies
5. Maintenance: Help maintain motivation; discuss progress and signs of impending relapse
6. Relapse: Help view relapse as a learning experience; non-judgmental discussion, reassess readiness

The Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change) guides counselling for behaviour change. The physician's approach must be tailored to the patient's current stage. In pre-contemplation, imposing change creates resistance; in preparation, the focus shifts to practical planning. Motivational interviewing techniques (reflective listening, avoiding confrontation) are key across all stages.

**Q:** A patient says, 'I know smoking is bad for me, but I'm not ready to quit yet.' What stage of change is this patient in, and what is the most appropriate physician response?

**A:** This patient is in the CONTEMPLATION stage (aware of the problem but ambivalent about change). The most appropriate response is to understand the patient's ambivalence and encourage change by offering an opportunity to discuss the pros and cons of change using reflective listening, without imposing change.

Contemplation is characterized by awareness of the problem and ambivalence — the patient is considering change but has not committed. The physician should use reflective listening to explore both sides, build confidence, and gain commitment. Offering unsolicited advice or pressure is counterproductive and creates resistance (as would occur if you treated them as 'pre-contemplative'). Avoid prescribing medication or creating a quit plan until the patient moves to Preparation.

**Q:** What are Canada's three Food Guide guidelines for healthy eating?

**A:** 1. Nutritious foods (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, proteins — especially plant-based) are the foundation for healthy eating; foods with mostly unsaturated fat should replace those with mostly saturated fat; water should be the beverage of choice.
2. Processed or prepared foods and beverages that contribute to excess sodium, free sugars, or saturated fat undermine healthy eating and should not be consumed regularly.
3. Food skills (cooking, food preparation, and reading food labels) are needed to navigate the complex food environment and support healthy eating.

Canada's Food Guide (2019) moved away from the 'four food groups' model to emphasize: (1) what to eat (healthy foods), (2) what to limit (processed foods), and (3) how to eat (food skills, cooking). It emphasizes plant-based proteins, water as the primary beverage, and mindful eating. It applies to individuals ≥2 years of age.

**Q:** What are the caloric values per gram for carbohydrates, protein, and fat?

**A:** • Carbohydrates: 4 kcal/g
• Protein: 4 kcal/g
• Fat: 9 kcal/g

These basic nutritional values are essential for calculating dietary energy intake and counselling patients on weight management. Fat provides more than twice the calories per gram compared to carbohydrates or protein. Calculating total daily energy expenditure is roughly 35 kcal/kg/day, varying by age, weight, height, sex, and activity level.

**Q:** What are the Vitamin D supplementation recommendations from the Canadian Cancer Society for adults in Canada?

**A:** • All adults living in Canada: Consider taking Vitamin D supplementation of 1000 IU/day during the fall and winter.
• Adults at higher risk of lower Vitamin D levels (older adults, those with dark skin, people who don't go outside often, those who wear clothing covering most of their skin): Consider taking 1000 IU/day year-round.
• Babies exclusively breast-fed: Consider taking Vitamin D supplementation of 400 IU/day.

These CCS recommendations are based on research on Vitamin D and prevention of colorectal and breast cancers. Canada's northern latitude means limited sunlight exposure in fall/winter, reducing endogenous Vitamin D synthesis. Groups with darker skin pigmentation, limited sun exposure, or obesity are at higher risk for deficiency. Note these are for cancer prevention; Osteoporosis Canada has separate recommendations for bone health.

**Q:** What are the Osteoporosis Canada daily Vitamin D and Calcium recommendations?

**A:** Vitamin D:
• 400–1000 IU/day for individuals aged 19–50 years
• 800–2000 IU/day for individuals ≥50 years or younger adults at high risk (osteoporosis, multiple fractures, inadequate Vitamin D absorption)

Calcium:
• 1000 mg/day from all sources for individuals aged 19–50 years and pregnant/lactating women
• 1200 mg/day for individuals >50 years (recommended to obtain from diet whenever possible vs. supplementation)

Osteoporosis Canada recommends higher Vitamin D and calcium for older adults due to decreased absorption efficiency with age. The upper limit of Vitamin D (2000 IU) is used for high-risk patients. Calcium should preferably come from dietary sources (dairy, fortified foods, leafy greens) rather than supplements due to cardiovascular concerns with high-dose calcium supplementation.

**Q:** What dietary guidelines are recommended to reduce cardiovascular disease risk regarding fats and alcohol?

**A:** Fats: Consume mono- and polyunsaturated fats in moderation; minimize trans fat intake; limit saturated fats; use olive and canola oil over butter/margarine/coconut oil; use skim or 1% milk over whole milk.

Alcohol: Limit to <2 drinks/week. Risk continuum:
• Low risk: <2 drinks/week
• Moderate risk: 3–6 drinks/week
• High risk: ≥7 drinks/week

The 2016 CCS Guidelines and 2023 Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (CCSA) guidance inform these recommendations. The CCSA released updated alcohol guidance in 2023 emphasizing that any alcohol carries some risk and recommending <2 drinks/week for low risk. This is significantly more conservative than older guidelines and represents an important update for MCCQE1 purposes.

**Q:** Compare the Mediterranean diet and the DASH diet in terms of their key components.

**A:** Mediterranean diet:
• High: leafy green vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts, legumes, extra virgin olive oil
• Moderate: fish, lean meats, low-fat dairy, poultry
• Low: red meats and sweets

DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension):
• High: vegetables/fruits, no/low-fat dairy, whole grains, poultry, fish, beans, seeds, nuts; high potassium, magnesium, calcium, protein, fibre
• Low: sodium, sweets, added sugars, sugar-sweetened beverages, fats, red meats, saturated fat, trans fat, cholesterol

Both diets have evidence for cardiovascular benefit. The DASH diet was specifically designed to reduce blood pressure and is particularly high in potassium, magnesium, and calcium. The Mediterranean diet has the strongest evidence for reducing all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events. The Portfolio Diet (nuts 45g/day, plant protein 50g/day, viscous fibres, plant sterols 2g/day) is another evidence-based option for cholesterol reduction.

**Q:** What is the BMI classification for obesity according to Health Canada, and what are the associated disease risks?

**A:** • Underweight: BMI <18.5 — Increased risk (different complications)
• Normal: BMI 18.5–24.9 — Least risk
• Overweight: BMI 25.0–29.9 — Increased risk
• Obesity Class I: BMI 30.0–34.9 — High risk
• Obesity Class II: BMI 35.0–39.9 — Very high risk
• Obesity Class III (Extreme Obesity): BMI ≥40.0 — Extremely high risk

BMI is a useful but imperfect screening tool — it does not directly measure adiposity and may misclassify muscular individuals. The Canadian Adult Obesity Clinical Practice Guidelines (2020) recognize that waist circumference (WC) provides additional information about metabolic risk beyond BMI. The guidelines also emphasize that obesity involves weight bias and stigma, which independently increase complications and mortality.

**Q:** What are the 5 steps in the Canadian Adult Obesity Clinical Practice Guidelines (2020) approach to obesity management?

**A:** 1. ASK: Recognize obesity as a chronic disease; obtain patient permission to discuss and help treat it in an unbiased manner
2. ASSESS: Use appropriate measures (height, weight, waist circumference, BMI); identify root causes (biological, psychological, life experiences), complications, barriers, and disease severity
3. ADVISE: Discuss treatment options; create individualized care plans addressing root causes and supporting behaviour change
4. AGREE: Agree on goals focusing on patient's values, ensuring realistic expectations and sustainable behaviour change
5. ASSIST: Follow up and reassess regularly; assist with drivers/barriers; advocate for improved obesity care

The 5A framework (Ask, Assess, Advise, Agree, Assist) is the cornerstone of the 2020 Obesity Canada guidelines. It emphasizes a patient-centred, non-judgmental approach. Obesity is recognized as a chronic, progressive, relapsing disease driven by biological, psychological, and social factors — not simply a failure of willpower. Weight bias and stigma are explicitly acknowledged as contributors to complications.

**Q:** What are the criteria for bariatric surgery candidacy according to Canadian guidelines?

**A:** Consider bariatric surgery for:
• BMI ≥40 kg/m², OR
• BMI ≥35 kg/m² with ≥1 adiposity-related disease (e.g., T2DM)
When optimal medical and behavioural management has been insufficient to produce significant weight loss. The choice of bariatric procedure should be made with an interprofessional team; adjustable gastric banding and single anastomosis gastric bypass are NOT suggested due to unacceptable complications and long-term failure.

Bariatric surgery is the most effective long-term treatment for severe obesity and can induce remission of T2DM. The 2020 Obesity Canada guidelines specify that surgery should only be considered after adequate medical/behavioural management has been attempted. The preferred procedures include Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy. Pharmacotherapy candidates: BMI ≥30 kg/m² or BMI ≥27 kg/m² with ≥1 weight-related condition.

**Q:** What are the 4 prescription medications approved in Canada for obesity treatment, and what are their mechanisms?

**A:** 1. Contrave® (naltrexone + bupropion): Reduces hunger and cravings
2. Saxenda® (liraglutide): Decreases appetite (GLP-1 receptor agonist)
3. Xenical® (orlistat): Reduces dietary fat absorption by inhibiting pancreatic and gastric lipases
4. Wegovy® (semaglutide): Improves satiation and satiety, reduces hunger and cravings (GLP-1 receptor agonist)

Indications: BMI ≥30 kg/m² OR BMI ≥27 kg/m² with ≥1 weight-related condition (e.g., HTN, T2DM, dyslipidemia), used alongside reduced calorie diet and increased physical activity.

As of 2025, these are the 4 approved pharmacotherapies for obesity in Canada. Semaglutide (Wegovy) represents the newest and most effective option. GLP-1 receptor agonists (liraglutide, semaglutide) have additional cardiovascular and glycemic benefits. Orlistat causes gastrointestinal side effects (steatorrhea, oily spotting) due to its mechanism. Naltrexone-bupropion acts centrally on appetite pathways.

**Q:** What are the adverse medical complications associated with obesity?

**A:** • T2DM
• Dyslipidemia
• Coronary artery disease (CAD)
• Osteoarthritis
• Stroke
• Hypertension (HTN)
• Certain cancers (e.g., breast, colon, endometrial)
• Gallbladder disease
• Sleep apnea
• Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)
• Increased total mortality
• Back/joint pain

Obesity is associated with systemic complications affecting virtually every organ system. Metabolic complications (T2DM, dyslipidemia, HTN) form the 'metabolic syndrome.' Mechanical complications include osteoarthritis, sleep apnea, and GERD. Cancer risk is elevated due to inflammatory adipokines and hormonal effects (excess estrogen from adipose tissue). Low BMI is also associated with complications: osteoporosis, infertility, and pregnancy complications.

**Q:** What is the definition of metabolic syndrome and its diagnostic criteria?

**A:** Metabolic syndrome requires: Central obesity (Men: WC >102 cm; Women: WC ≥80 cm) PLUS any TWO of the following four:
1. TG level ≥1.7 mmol/L (≥150 mg/dL)
2. Low HDL-C: Men <1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL); Women <1.3 mmol/L (50 mg/dL)
3. Blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or taking BP medications
4. Fasting glucose ≥5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL)

Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of cardiometabolic risk factors that significantly increase the risk of CVD and T2DM. Central (abdominal) obesity is the central feature. The IDF definition uses lower WC thresholds for South Asians. The presence of metabolic syndrome roughly doubles the risk of CVD and increases T2DM risk 5-fold. Treatment targets lifestyle modification addressing all components.

**Q:** According to the 2021 Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) Dyslipidemia Guidelines, what are the risk categories used to determine who needs statin therapy?

**A:** Statin-indicated conditions (treat regardless of FRS):
• Clinical atherosclerosis
• Abdominal aortic aneurysm
• LDL-C >5 mmol/L or genetic dyslipidemia
• Most diabetes (age ≥40; or age ≥30 with ≥5 year duration; or microvascular disease; or CKD)

Primary prevention (calculate FRS or CLEM):
• Low risk: FRS <10%
• Intermediate risk: FRS 10–19%
• High risk: FRS ≥20%, or LDL-C ≥3.5 mmol/L, or Non-HDL >4.3 mmol/L, or ApoB ≥1.2 g/L, or men ≥50/women ≥60 with ≥1 additional risk factor

The 2021 CCS guidelines emphasize a treat-to-target approach with primary targets of LDL-C <2.0 mmol/L or ≥50% LDL-C reduction, plus ApoB <0.8 g/L or non-HDL-C <2.6 mmol/L. The Framingham Risk Score (FRS) estimates 10-year CAD risk based on age, sex, TC, HDL-C, SBP, smoking, and DM. Family history of premature CVD doubles the FRS.

**Q:** What are the screening recommendations for dyslipidemia in Canada?

**A:** Screen with a standard lipid profile:
• Males ≥40 years
• Females ≥40 years OR postmenopausal
• Any age with risk factors for dyslipidemia (smoking, T2DM, HTN, family history of premature CVD, family history of dyslipidemia, CKD, inflammatory disease, obesity, erectile dysfunction, HIV, COPD, hypertensive diseases of pregnancy, stigmata of dyslipidemia)

Repeat screening every 5 years if FRS <5%, or every year if FRS ≥5%.

The standard lipid panel includes TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, and calculated non-HDL-C. Optional additions include ApoB and urine ACR. Secondary causes of dyslipidemia should always be screened for: hypothyroidism, CKD, DM, nephrotic syndrome, and liver disease. Non-fasting lipids (TC and non-HDL-C) hold the same prognostic value as fasting lipids for Framingham risk assessment.

**Q:** What are the signs of hyperlipidemia on physical examination?

**A:** • Atheromata: plaques in the intimal layer of arterial walls
• Xanthelasmata: sharply demarcated yellowish deposits of cholesterol underneath skin, usually on or around the eyelids
• Tendinous xanthoma: lipid deposit in tendon (especially Achilles tendon)
• Eruptive xanthoma: hypertriglyceridemia-induced reddish-yellow, pruritic, and painful papular or nodular rash
• Lipemia retinalis: creamy, white-coloured retinal blood vessels; occurs only with extreme hypertriglyceridemia
• Corneal arcus (arcus senilis): lipid deposit in cornea

Physical signs of hyperlipidemia are important for identifying patients with severe or longstanding dyslipidemia who may have genetic causes (familial hypercholesterolemia). Xanthelasmata suggest elevated LDL-C but can occur with normal lipids. Tendinous xanthomas are highly specific for familial hypercholesterolemia. Eruptive xanthomas occur with severe hypertriglyceridemia (TG >10 mmol/L) and signal pancreatitis risk. Arcus senilis before age 45 suggests dyslipidemia.

**Q:** What are the treatment targets for dyslipidemia therapy according to the 2021 CCS Guidelines?

**A:** Treat to target approach:
• Primary: LDL-C <2.0 mmol/L OR ≥50% reduction in LDL-C
• Alternative primary targets: ApoB <0.8 g/L OR non-HDL-C <2.6 mmol/L

If target achieved on maximally tolerated statin dose: reassess
If target NOT achieved: discuss add-on therapy (1st line: ezetimibe; alternative: bile acid sequestrants [BAS]; for very high risk: PCSK9 inhibitors as add-on)

The treat-to-target approach was updated in the 2021 CCS guidelines. The primary LDL-C target of <2.0 mmol/L is a hard endpoint target. ApoB is an increasingly recognized alternative target as it directly measures the number of atherogenic lipoprotein particles. PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab, alirocumab) are reserved for high-risk patients unable to reach targets on maximal statin + ezetimibe therapy.

**Q:** What are the first-line pharmacological and non-pharmacological management strategies for dyslipidemia?

**A:** Non-pharmacological (Health Behaviour Interventions — can decrease LDL-C by up to 10%):
1. Smoking cessation (most important for preventing CAD)
2. Dietary modification: reduce saturated fat, red meat, refined sugar, alcohol; consume nuts, fruits/vegetables, poultry, fish
3. Physical activity: ≥150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise in bouts of >10 min

Pharmacological — 1st line: Statins (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors)
• Risks: myopathy (myalgia, rhabdomyolysis)
• If severe side effects: switch to ezetimibe

Lifestyle modifications should be employed for at least 3 months before drug therapy for low/intermediate-risk patients; high-risk patients should start therapy immediately with concurrent lifestyle changes. Statins are the cornerstone of pharmacotherapy. Monitoring: ALT and CK at baseline and 6 weeks later; tolerate CK elevation up to 10x ULN (or 2-3x if symptomatic). LDL cannot be calculated when TG ≥4.5 mmol/L.

**Q:** What are the monitoring requirements for patients on statin therapy?

**A:** • Baseline: ALT and CK
• 6 weeks after starting: repeat ALT and CK
• No routine repeated measures of ALT and CK in asymptomatic patients (only if symptomatic)
• Tolerate CK elevation up to 10× ULN without symptoms; stop if 2–3× ULN if symptomatic
• Tolerate serum Cr rise of <25%
• Once adequate response achieved: evaluate fasting lipids every 6–12 months

Statin monitoring has evolved to avoid over-testing. Routine monitoring of liver enzymes is no longer recommended in asymptomatic patients on stable statin therapy. Myopathy monitoring is clinically driven. Use statin + fibrate combinations with caution due to increased risk of myalgias, CK elevations, myopathy, and rhabdomyolysis. Statins slightly increase the incidence of T2DM, but the absolute risk is small relative to the cardiovascular benefit.

**Q:** What is isolated hypertriglyceridemia, how is it classified, and how is it managed?

**A:** Definition: Elevated TG with normal HDL-C and TC.
Classification:
• Mild: TG ≥2.2 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dL)
• Marked: TG ≥5.6 mmol/L (≥500 mg/dL)
• Severe (pancreatitis risk): typically TG >10 mmol/L

Management:
• Principal therapy: lifestyle modification (weight loss, exercise, avoid smoking and alcohol, effective blood glucose control in DM, increased omega-3 fatty acid intake)
• Drug therapy (lowers pancreatitis risk, NOT CAD risk): fibrates
• Severe hypertriglyceridemia (>10 mmol/L): associated with increased risk of acute pancreatitis

Isolated hypertriglyceridemia itself does not increase cardiovascular risk (this differs from mixed dyslipidemia). However, severe hypertriglyceridemia (>10 mmol/L) carries significant pancreatitis risk. The primary goal of drug therapy in this setting is pancreatitis prevention, not CAD prevention. Fibrates are the drug of choice. Omega-3 fatty acids (prescription doses) can also lower TG. Eruptive xanthomas can appear at very high TG levels.

**Q:** What are the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for adults aged 18–64?

**A:** Physical Activity:
• ≥150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous intensity aerobic physical activity in bouts of ≥10 min
• Muscle/bone strengthening activities using major muscle groups, ≥2 days/week

Sleep:
• 7–9 hours/day of good quality sleep with consistent bed and wake times

Sedentary Behaviour:
• Limit sedentary time to ≤8 hours/day
• No more than 3 hours/day of recreational screen time
• Break up long periods of sitting as often as possible

The 2020 CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology) Guidelines use a 24-hour framework integrating physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep as interconnected health behaviours. Adults 18–64 and older adults ≥65 have the same activity and sleep targets. Older adults additionally need activities that challenge balance to prevent falls.

**Q:** What are the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for children aged 5–17?

**A:** Physical Activity:
• ≥60 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic physical activity
• Muscle/bone strengthening exercises ≥3 days/week
• A variety of structured/unstructured light physical activities for several hours/day

Sleep:
• 9–11 hours/day (ages 5–13 years)
• 8–10 hours/day (ages 14–17 years)
• Uninterrupted, with consistent bed and wake times

Sedentary Behaviour:
• No more than 2 hours/day of recreational screen time
• Limit sitting for extended periods

Children and youth have higher physical activity requirements than adults (60 min/day vs. 150 min/week). Moderate activities for children include bike riding and playground play; vigorous activities include running and swimming. Children also need more sleep than adults. Screen time limit of 2 hours/day applies to recreational screen time (not educational).

**Q:** What are the benefits of regular physical exercise?

**A:** • Reduces risk of premature death
• Reduces risk of heart disease, stroke, and hypertension
• Reduces risk of certain types of cancer
• Reduces risk of T2DM
• Reduces risk of osteoporosis
• Reduces risk of overweight/obesity
• Improves fitness, strength, mobility, and functional independence
• Improves mental health (morale and self-esteem)
• Improves cardiometabolic risk factors
• Improves health-related quality of life

Exercise is one of the most powerful preventive interventions available. Approximately 25% of Canadians exercise regularly, 50% occasionally, and 25% are sedentary. When counselling patients on exercise: Ask about current activity level, Assess readiness, Advise about guidelines, Agree on a goal, Assist and Arrange follow-up (the 5A approach applied to exercise). Patients with CAD should have cardiac assessment before starting exercise. DM patients risk hypoglycemia with exercise.

**Q:** What is the epidemiology of smoking in Canada and why is it clinically important?

**A:** • Smoking is the single most preventable cause of premature illness and death
• 70% of smokers see a physician each year
• 2022 Canadian Tobacco and Nicotine Survey (CTNS, ages ≥15): 10.9% are current smokers
• 4% of youth ages 15–19, 8% of young adults ages 20–24, and 12% of adults ≥25 smoke
• More males (13%) than females (9%) smoke

The high physician visit rate among smokers (70%) means every clinical encounter is an opportunity for cessation counselling. Smoking cessation is the single most important intervention for preventing CAD. Even brief advice from a physician can increase quit rates. The CAN-ADAPTT guidelines mandate that all healthcare providers update tobacco use status, advise patients to quit, and assess willingness to quit at every interaction.

**Q:** What is the 2-3 Pattern of Smoking Cessation withdrawal timeline?

**A:** • 2–3 hours after last cigarette: Onset of withdrawal
• 2–3 days: Peak withdrawals
• 2–3 weeks: Expect improvement of withdrawal symptoms
• 2–3 months: Resolution of withdrawal; highest relapse rate within first 2–3 months

Understanding the withdrawal timeline helps set patient expectations and timing of pharmacotherapy. Nicotine withdrawal symptoms include: low mood, insomnia, irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, restlessness, decreased heart rate, and increased appetite. Physicians should warn patients about these expected symptoms and have a plan for managing them. The highest relapse risk is within the first 2–3 months.

**Q:** What is the '5 A's' framework for smoking cessation counselling for patients willing to quit?

**A:** 1. ASK: Ask if the patient currently smokes and is willing to discuss cessation
2. ADVISE: Advise the patient to quit
3. ASSESS: Assess willingness to quit
4. ASSIST: Assist in quit attempt(s)
5. ARRANGE: Arrange follow-up

The 5 A's is the standard framework for brief smoking cessation counselling. Combining counselling/behavioural therapies with pharmacotherapy is more effective than either alone. Minimum effective counselling: ≥4 sessions, >10 min each, with 6–12 month follow-up. 14% abstinent with counselling vs. 10% without. The STAR plan helps patients: Set a quit date, Tell family and friends, Anticipate challenges, Remove tobacco-related products.

**Q:** Compare the efficacy of the three main pharmacotherapies for smoking cessation.

**A:** • Placebo: ~8–11.5% abstinent at 12 months
• NRT (Nicotine Replacement Therapy): 19.7% abstinent at 12 months (vs. 11.5% for placebo); combination NRT (fast-acting form + patch) is superior to single-form NRT
• Bupropion SR (Zyban®): 21% abstinent at 12 months (vs. 8% placebo); similar effectiveness to NRT; start 7–14 days before quit date
• Varenicline (Champix®): 23% abstinent from 9–52 weeks (vs. 16% bupropion vs. 9% placebo); most effective option; start 10–14 days before quit date

Varenicline (Champix) is the most effective single pharmacotherapy for smoking cessation. It acts as a partial nicotinic receptor agonist (reduces cravings) and partial competitive antagonist (reduces response to smoked nicotine). Significant side effects (nausea, headache, unusual dreams, neuropsychiatric symptoms) can limit compliance. Bupropion's mechanism for cessation appears independent of its antidepressant effect. Combination NRT (patch + fast-acting form) is superior to single-form NRT.

**Q:** What are the different forms of Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT), their dosages, and key points?

**A:** 1. Nicotine Gum (OTC): 2 mg if <25 cigarettes/day; 4 mg if ≥25 cigarettes/day; 1 piece q1–2h for 1–3 months (max 24 pieces/day); chew until 'peppery' taste, then 'park' between gum and cheek
2. Nicotine Patch (OTC): 8 weeks total; 21 mg/day × 6 wk → 14 mg/day × 2 wk → 7 mg/day × 2 wk; start with lower dose if <10 cigarettes/day; change q24h, alternate sides
3. Nicotine Inhaler (OTC): 6–16 cartridges/day up to 12 weeks; nicotine absorbed in mouth and throat (not lungs)
4. Nicotine Nasal Spray (Rx): newer form

NRT provides nicotine without other harmful substances in cigarettes, reducing cravings and withdrawal symptoms. No significant difference in abstinence rates between different NRT forms as monotherapy. Combination NRT (patch + fast-acting form like gum/inhaler) is superior to single-form NRT with high certainty evidence. Use with caution immediately post-MI, worsening angina, or arrhythmia. Advise patients not to smoke while using NRT.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of varenicline (Champix®) and how does it achieve its effect on smoking cessation?

**A:** Varenicline is a partial nicotinic receptor agonist AND a partial competitive nicotinic receptor antagonist:
• As a partial agonist: stimulates nicotine receptors enough to reduce cravings and withdrawal symptoms
• As a partial competitive antagonist: blocks nicotine from binding to receptors, thereby reducing the rewarding effects of smoked nicotine (reduces the 'pleasure' from cigarettes)

Start 10–14 days before quit date. 23% abstinent from 9–52 weeks vs. 16% with bupropion vs. 9% with placebo.

Varenicline's dual mechanism makes it uniquely effective: it satisfies nicotine cravings while simultaneously preventing the reinforcing effects of continued smoking. Important side effects include nausea, headache, drowsiness, unusual dreams, and neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression, suicidality — though the evidence for this has been debated). FDA and Health Canada require monitoring for mood/behaviour changes. Lower patient compliance due to side effects can limit real-world effectiveness.

**Q:** According to Canadian CAN-ADAPTT guidelines, what are the 4 key responsibilities of healthcare providers regarding tobacco cessation?

**A:** 1. Update tobacco use status for all patients regularly
2. Clearly advise patients to quit
3. Assess patient willingness to quit and offer assistance to those who express interest
4. Conduct regular follow-up to assess response and monitor mental health status/other addictions while quitting

The 2012 CAN-ADAPTT guidelines emphasize that tobacco cessation is a core responsibility of all healthcare providers, not just specialists. The guidelines also emphasize monitoring mental health during cessation (as withdrawal can precipitate mood disorders) and monitoring for other addictions (patients may substitute substances). Social support, community resources, self-help, group therapy, helplines, and web-based strategies should be offered to all patients willing to quit.

**Q:** What is the Framingham Risk Score (FRS), and how is it used in clinical practice for dyslipidemia?

**A:** The FRS estimates the 10-year risk of developing CAD and is calculated based on: gender, age, HDL-C, total cholesterol (TC), systolic BP, smoking status, and diabetes.

Key uses:
• To be completed for men and women ages 40–75 every 5 years
• Family history of premature CVD (<55 in male 1st-degree relative; <65 in female 1st-degree relative) DOUBLES the FRS
• Risk categories: Low <10%, Intermediate 10–19%, High ≥20%
• Used to determine when to initiate pharmacotherapy
• CV age can be calculated to increase patient adherence
• LDL cannot be calculated when TG ≥4.5 mmol/L (use non-HDL-C instead)

The FRS is the primary risk stratification tool in Canadian dyslipidemia guidelines. Non-fasting TC and non-HDL-C hold the same prognostic value as fasting lipids for FRS calculation. Repeat screening every 5 years if FRS <5%, or annually if FRS ≥5%. The Cardiovascular Life Expectancy Model (CLEM) is an alternative risk assessment tool. High-risk patients warrant immediate statin therapy without a 3-month lifestyle modification trial.

**Q:** What are the risk factors that indicate screening for dyslipidemia at any age (before the standard age thresholds of 40 years)?

**A:** • Men and women aged ≥40 (or South Asian individuals or individuals of Asian origin)
• Current cigarette smoking
• T2DM
• Arterial HTN
• Family history of premature CVD (men <55, women <65 in 1st-degree relative)
• Chronic kidney disease (CKD)
• Family history of dyslipidemia
• Erectile dysfunction
• Inflammatory disease (lupus, RA, psoriatic arthritis, IBD)
• HIV infection
• COPD
• Clinical evidence of atherosclerosis or carotid artery disease
• Stigmata of dyslipidemia (arcus cornea, xanthelasma, xanthoma)
• Obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²)
• Hypertensive diseases of pregnancy

The standard screening age is 40 years for both sexes, but the presence of risk factors indicates earlier screening. Notably, erectile dysfunction is an independent cardiovascular risk factor and a marker of subclinical atherosclerosis (penile arteries are smaller and affected earlier than coronary arteries). HIV and COPD are recognized as increasing cardiovascular risk. Hypertensive diseases of pregnancy (pre-eclampsia) are associated with long-term cardiovascular risk.

**Q:** A 45-year-old man has been on atorvastatin 40 mg for 3 months. He complains of muscle aches. His CK is 5× the upper limit of normal. What is the appropriate management?

**A:** Since CK is 5× ULN with symptoms (myalgia), this is concerning for statin-induced myopathy. The threshold to stop is CK >2–3× ULN IF symptomatic. With CK at 5× ULN AND symptoms, the statin should be STOPPED. If asymptomatic, tolerate CK up to 10× ULN. After resolution, consider re-challenging with a lower-dose statin or switching to an alternative (e.g., ezetimibe as monotherapy, rosuvastatin at lower dose, or alternate-day dosing).

Statin-induced myopathy ranges from myalgia (muscle pain without CK elevation) to rhabdomyolysis (severe CK elevation with myoglobinuria and renal failure). The CCS monitoring guidelines specify: tolerate CK up to 10× ULN in asymptomatic patients; stop if 2–3× ULN if symptomatic. Rhabdomyolysis risk increases with statin-fibrate combinations. Risk of any adverse event from statins is OR 1.4 (95% CI 1.09–1.80), but serious events are rare (NNH ~400 for serious adverse events).

**Q:** What is the physical activity recommendation for older adults (≥65 years) according to the 2020 CSEP Guidelines, and what additional consideration applies?

**A:** Same as adults 18–64:
• ≥150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous intensity aerobic physical activity in bouts of ≥10 min
• Muscle/bone strengthening activities ≥2 days/week
• 7–9 hours/day of sleep
• Limit sedentary time ≤8 hours/day

Additional consideration: Physical activities that challenge BALANCE (those with poor mobility should perform activities to enhance balance and prevent falls).

Fall prevention is a key priority for older adults. Activities that improve balance (tai chi, single-leg standing, yoga) should be incorporated in addition to aerobic and resistance training. Falls are the leading cause of injury-related hospitalization in older Canadians. The balance component of the guidelines specifically targets mobility-impaired older adults to prevent falls and fractures.

**Q:** What are the key differences between a patient in the 'Pre-Contemplation' and 'Preparation' stage of change, and how should the physician approach each differently?

**A:** Pre-Contemplation:
• Patient does NOT consider changing behaviour a possibility
• Physician aim: raise the issue sensitively, offer neutral exchange of information (to AVOID resistance), increase awareness of the problem and its risks
• Do NOT prescribe action steps yet

Preparation:
• Patient has decided to change and is choosing how
• Physician aim: explore realistic options, discuss inevitable difficulties, identify high-risk situations, develop relapse prevention strategies, strengthen commitment and confidence
• NOW is the time to create a concrete plan (e.g., quit date, STAR plan for smoking)

Matching the physician's response to the patient's stage prevents resistance and builds therapeutic alliance. Pushing action steps on a pre-contemplative patient creates resistance ('Why should I listen to you?'). Providing only awareness-raising to a patient in preparation wastes an opportunity. The Transtheoretical Model underpins motivational interviewing, a key skill tested on the MCCQE1 for chronic disease management and preventive counselling.

**Q:** In Canada, what is the prevalence of osteoporosis in women and men?

**A:** 1 in 4 women and 1 in 8 men are affected by osteoporosis in Canada.

Osteoporosis is a common age-related disease characterized by decreased bone mass and increased fracture susceptibility. Canadian prevalence data highlights the disproportionate burden in women, particularly post-menopausal women, compared to men.

**Q:** Which two validated Canadian tools are used for 10-year fracture risk assessment in osteoporosis?

**A:** FRAX (WHO Fracture Risk Assessment Tool) and CAROC (Canadian Association of Radiologists and Osteoporosis Canada) — both validated in the Canadian population and available at osteoporosis.ca.

FRAX and CAROC are the two recommended fracture risk assessment tools in Canada. Both incorporate clinical risk factors and BMD values to estimate 10-year probability of a major osteoporotic fracture, guiding treatment decisions per the 2023 Canadian clinical practice guidelines.

**Q:** According to the 2023 Canadian osteoporosis guidelines, what 10-year fracture risk threshold or T-score triggers pharmacotherapy recommendation?

**A:** Pharmacotherapy is suggested when 10-year fracture risk >20% OR T-score <-2.5 AND age >70 years.

The 2023 Canadian Clinical Practice Guidelines for osteoporosis specify that pharmacotherapy should be suggested for patients with high fracture risk (>20% at 10 years) or those aged >70 with T-score <-2.5. Intermediate risk (10–20%) warrants BMD reassessment in 3 years if on therapy.

**Q:** List 5 secondary causes of osteoporosis (conditions that cause secondary osteoporosis).

**A:** Primary hyperparathyroidism, Type 1 DM, osteogenesis imperfecta, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, hypogonadism or premature menopause (<45 yr), Cushing's disease, chronic malnutrition/malabsorption, chronic liver disease, COPD, and chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., IBD).

Secondary osteoporosis arises from identifiable underlying conditions. When these are identified, specialist referral for co-management should be considered. Recognizing secondary causes is important as they may require specific treatments beyond standard osteoporosis pharmacotherapy.

**Q:** What are the three physical exam findings that suggest possible vertebral fracture in the assessment of osteoporosis?

**A:** 1. Prospective height loss >2 cm or historical height loss >6 cm; 2. Rib-to-pelvis distance <2 finger-breadths in the midaxillary line; 3. Occiput-to-wall distance >5 cm.

These clinical signs are inexpensive bedside tests that suggest undiagnosed vertebral fractures. A finding of vertebral fracture(s) upgrades fracture risk and can guide appropriate choice and duration of osteoporosis therapy, often prompting lateral spine imaging.

**Q:** What is the recommended daily calcium intake for adults aged 19–50 years versus those aged >50 years?

**A:** Adults 19–50: 1000 mg/day. Adults >50: 1200 mg/day.

Adequate calcium intake is fundamental to bone health. Dietary calcium is preferred (e.g., 1 cup milk = 300 mg, 1/2 cup yogurt = 332 mg), with supplementation added only to meet the target. Requirements increase after age 50 due to decreased calcium absorption and increased bone turnover.

**Q:** What is the recommended daily vitamin D supplementation for adults 19–50 years versus those >50 years (or those with risk factors)?

**A:** Adults 19–50: 400–1000 IU/day. Adults >50 or younger adults with risk factors (osteoporosis, multiple fractures, or conditions affecting vitamin D absorption): 800–2000 IU/day.

Vitamin D is critical for calcium absorption and bone mineralization. Dietary sources (fortified milk, fatty fish) provide limited amounts, so supplementation is usually necessary. Most multivitamins contain only 400 IU, which may be insufficient for older adults or those at risk.

**Q:** How often should BMD be reassessed in a patient who was started on osteoporosis pharmacotherapy?

**A:** Reassess BMD in 3 years if initiated on pharmacotherapy. Reassess earlier if the patient develops secondary causes, a new fracture, or other risk factors for rapid bone loss.

Per the 2023 Canadian osteoporosis guidelines, patients on pharmacotherapy should have BMD measured at 3 years to assess response. Those NOT on pharmacotherapy should be reassessed at 5–10 years if risk <10%, 5 years if risk 10–15%, and 3 years if risk ≥15%.

**Q:** What investigations should be ordered in the workup of osteoporosis?

**A:** CBC, creatinine, corrected calcium, ALP, TSH, 25-hydroxyvitamin D (after 3–4 months of adequate supplementation), and serum protein electrophoresis if vertebral fractures are present.

These investigations help identify secondary causes of osteoporosis and establish a baseline. 25-hydroxyvitamin D should be measured after adequate supplementation to assess true vitamin D status. Protein electrophoresis screens for multiple myeloma, which can cause vertebral fractures.

**Q:** Which STI is the most common reportable STI in Canada, and which is the second most common?

**A:** Most common: chlamydia (Chlamydia trachomatis). Second most common: gonorrhea (Neisseria gonorrhoeae).

Chlamydia is the most prevalent reportable STI in Canada, often asymptomatic especially in women, leading to underdiagnosis if not screened. Gonorrhea is second most common and has increasing antibiotic resistance. Both are mandatorily reportable to public health.

**Q:** What are the 5 P's of sexual history taking in STI assessment?

**A:** Partners (numbers, gender), Practices (vaginal, oral, anal insertive/receptive), Protection, Past history of STIs, Pregnancy prevention.

A thorough sexual history using the 5 P's framework is essential for STI risk assessment. It guides appropriate screening, counseling, and partner notification. Information about practices helps determine which anatomical sites to screen.

**Q:** What are the STI screening recommendations in Canada for gonorrhea and chlamydia?

**A:** Annual screening for all sexually active people under age 30. Individuals at increased risk should also be screened for hepatitis B, HIV, and syphilis. Pap test every 3 years for anyone with a cervix aged 25–69 who has ever been sexually active.

Canadian STI guidelines emphasize opportunistic screening, particularly in young sexually active individuals. Screening is the cornerstone of STI control since both chlamydia and gonorrhea are frequently asymptomatic, preventing transmission and complications such as PID and infertility.

**Q:** Which STIs are mandatorily reportable in Canada?

**A:** Chlamydia, gonorrhea, hepatitis B, HIV, syphilis, and chancroid.

Mandatory reporting to public health allows for contact tracing, surveillance, and outbreak control. A patient with a bacterial STI or trichomoniasis should abstain from sexual activity until treatment is complete and for 7 days after treatment for both partners (or until test of cure).

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for uncomplicated gonococcal urethritis/cervicitis?

**A:** Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM single dose PLUS azithromycin 1 g PO single dose. Always treat for both gonococcal AND non-gonococcal (i.e., chlamydia) simultaneously.

Dual therapy is recommended because co-infection with chlamydia is common, and combined treatment reduces antibiotic resistance development. Test of cure with cultures 3–7 days post-treatment is required for pharyngeal infections, ongoing symptoms, treatment failure, or pregnancy.

**Q:** What is the treatment for non-gonococcal urethritis/cervicitis (usually Chlamydia trachomatis)?

**A:** Doxycycline 100 mg PO BID x 7 days plus gonococcal urethritis/cervicitis prescription (dual therapy). Repeat screening at 3 months post-treatment.

Chlamydia treatment requires a 7-day course of doxycycline or alternative regimens. Because co-infection with gonorrhea is common, dual treatment is initiated simultaneously. Follow-up screening at 3 months is recommended due to high rates of reinfection.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for primary infectious syphilis (Treponema pallidum)?

**A:** Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose. For late latent syphilis: 2.4 million units IM penicillin G once weekly for 3 weeks.

Benzathine penicillin G remains the treatment of choice for syphilis at all stages. Partners from the last 3–12 months must be notified. Continuous follow-up and serological testing is required until patients are seronegative to confirm treatment success.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for a first episode of genital herpes (HSV-1 or HSV-2)?

**A:** Acyclovir 400 mg PO TID x 7–10 days, OR Famciclovir 250 mg PO TID x 7–10 days, OR Valacyclovir 1000 mg PO BID x 7–10 days.

Antiviral therapy for primary genital herpes reduces severity and duration of symptoms. It does not cure the infection or prevent future recurrences. Recurrent episodes are typically milder and shorter. Antivirals do not reliably prevent transmission.

**Q:** What HPV vaccine (Gardasil) is offered in Canada and to which groups?

**A:** Gardasil is offered to: females aged 9–27, women ≥27 at risk of ongoing exposure, males aged 9–27 (to prevent anogenital cancer and genital warts), and individuals ≥27 at risk of ongoing exposure.

HPV vaccination is highly effective at preventing cervical, anal, and genital cancers as well as genital warts. Gardasil 9 protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. Vaccination is most effective before exposure (i.e., before sexual debut).

**Q:** What is the PODS mnemonic for sinusitis symptoms?

**A:** Facial Pain/pressure/fullness, nasal Obstruction, nasal Purulence or discoloured postnasal Discharge, Smell disorder (hyposmia/anosmia).

PODS captures the cardinal symptoms of sinusitis as per Canadian guidelines. For a diagnosis of acute bacterial rhinosinusitis (ABRS), the patient must have nasal obstruction or nasal purulence/discharge AND at least one other PODS symptom.

**Q:** What are the red flags in sinusitis that warrant urgent referral?

**A:** Altered mental status, severe headache, systemic toxicity, swelling of the orbit or change in visual acuity or extraocular muscles, hard neurological findings, signs of meningeal irritation, suspected intracranial complications (meningitis, intracranial abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis), involvement of associated structures (periorbital/preseptal cellulitis, Pott's puffy tumour).

These red flags indicate potential life-threatening complications of sinusitis including intracranial extension or orbital complications. Urgent assessment and imaging (CT) are required. Pott's puffy tumour is a subperiosteal abscess of the frontal bone — a rare but serious complication.

**Q:** What is the most common viral etiology of sinusitis and what is the most common bacterial etiology?

**A:** Most common viral cause: rhinovirus (also influenza, parainfluenza). Most common bacterial causes: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis.

Viral sinusitis is far more common than bacterial. ABRS should be suspected when symptoms worsen after 5–7 days (biphasic illness), persist >7 days without improvement, or purulence is present for 3–4 days with high fever. Antibiotic use should be reserved for ABRS.

**Q:** What is the first-line antibiotic for acute bacterial rhinosinusitis in adults, and what are alternatives for penicillin allergy?

**A:** First-line: amoxicillin 500 mg PO TID x 5–10 days. Penicillin allergy: doxycycline 100 mg PO BID x 5–10 days. Second-line or antibiotic resistance risk: amoxicillin-clavulanate 500 mg TID or 875 mg BID, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin 750 mg once daily or moxifloxacin 400 mg daily) x 5–10 days.

Antibiotics are indicated for severe ABRS or those failing to improve with intranasal corticosteroids. Mild-to-moderate ABRS is managed with intranasal corticosteroids alone. ENT referral is warranted for anatomic defects, failure of second-line therapy, or ≥4 episodes per year.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate acute bacterial rhinosinusitis versus severe ABRS?

**A:** Mild-to-moderate: intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) alone. Severe: INCS plus antibiotics.

Per Canadian sinusitis guidelines, the severity of ABRS guides treatment. Mild symptoms are occasional and easily tolerated; moderate symptoms are steady but manageable; severe symptoms are hard to tolerate and may interfere with activity or sleep. Watchful waiting with symptom relief is appropriate for mild disease.

**Q:** What is the prevalence of sleep problems in the primary care setting?

**A:** 1/3 of patients in a primary care setting have occasional sleep problems; 10% have chronic sleep problems.

Sleep disorders are highly prevalent in primary care and are frequently underdiagnosed. The three main sleep complaints are insomnia (difficulty falling/staying asleep, early wakening, non-refreshing sleep), parasomnias (night terrors, restless leg syndrome, somnambulism), and excessive daytime sleepiness.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for primary insomnia?

**A:** Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), which includes: sleep hygiene education, relaxation therapy (deep breathing, meditation, biofeedback), stimulus control therapy, and sleep restriction therapy.

CBT-I is the evidence-based first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, superior to pharmacotherapy for long-term outcomes. Pharmacologic treatment (benzodiazepines, non-benzodiazepines) is used only as a short-term supplement (<14 days) with appropriate follow-up in 7–14 days.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)?

**A:** Male gender, obesity (BMI high), large tonsils and adenoids in children, aging (decreased muscle tone), craniofacial abnormalities, and positive family history (FMHx).

OSA results from partial or complete upper airway obstruction due to collapse of the base of the tongue, soft palate with uvula, and epiglottis during sleep. Understanding risk factors guides appropriate screening and referral for nocturnal polysomnography.

**Q:** What is the diagnostic criterion for OSA on nocturnal polysomnography?

**A:** Greater than 15 apneic/hypopneic episodes per hour of sleep.

This is the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) threshold for diagnosing OSA. Apneas are defined as cessation of breathing for ≥10 seconds. Apneic episodes can last 20–180 seconds and occur 100–600 episodes per night in severe cases.

**Q:** What are the cardiovascular consequences of untreated OSA?

**A:** Hypertension (2x increased risk), coronary artery disease (3x increased risk), stroke (4x increased risk), cardiac arrhythmias, pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular dysfunction, and cor pulmonale. OSA is an independent risk factor for CAD.

Chronic hypoxemia and sympathetic activation from repetitive apneas drive cardiovascular complications. These significant risks make treatment of OSA critical beyond just improving sleep quality and daytime function. Other consequences include morning headache (hypercapnia), sexual dysfunction, and cognitive impairment.

**Q:** What is the primary treatment for OSA, and what is the physician's reporting obligation if OSA is not controlled?

**A:** Primary treatment is CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure), which maintains patent airway in 95% of OSA cases. If OSA is not controlled by CPAP, the physician must report the patient to the Ministry of Transportation (driving restriction).

CPAP is the gold-standard treatment for moderate-to-severe OSA. Reporting to the Ministry of Transportation is required because uncontrolled OSA impairs driving due to excessive daytime sleepiness, creating a significant public safety risk. Surgical options (UPPP, tonsillectomy) are alternatives for those intolerant of CPAP.

**Q:** What is the Modified Centor Score used for, and what are its criteria?

**A:** The Modified Centor Score estimates the probability of Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus (GABHS) in pharyngitis. Criteria: +1 for absent cough, +1 for fever >38°C history, +1 for tonsillar exudate, +1 for swollen/tender anterior cervical nodes, +1 for age 5–14, 0 for age 15–44, -1 for age >45.

In communities with moderate strep prevalence (10–20%), a score of 0–2 carries <1–15% probability of GABHS and no further testing or antibiotics are needed. A score ≥3 warrants a Rapid Antigen Detection Test (RADT) and treatment with antibiotics if positive. The score is not applicable to children <5 years.

**Q:** What is the gold standard for diagnosing GABHS pharyngitis, and what is the sensitivity and specificity of rapid antigen detection testing (RADT)?

**A:** Gold standard: throat culture. RADT: high specificity (95–99%) but moderate sensitivity (85%). Nucleic acid detection has high sensitivity (92%).

RADT provides rapid results but can miss cases (false negatives) due to moderate sensitivity. For this reason, a negative RADT in patients <20 years should be followed by throat culture. In patients >20 years with a negative RADT, antibiotics should not be given.

**Q:** What are the non-suppurative complications of Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcal (GABHS) pharyngitis?

**A:** Acute rheumatic fever and acute glomerulonephritis.

Suppurative complications include abscess, sinusitis, otitis media, cervical adenitis, and pneumonia. Importantly, antibiotic treatment reduces the risk of rheumatic fever but does NOT reduce the incidence of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. A 10-day antibiotic course is required for strep treatment.

**Q:** What is the antibiotic of choice for streptococcal pharyngitis in adults, and what is used in penicillin allergy?

**A:** First-line: penicillin V 300 mg PO TID or 600 mg BID x 10 days. Mild penicillin allergy: cefuroxime 500 mg PO BID x 10 days. Severe/anaphylactic penicillin allergy: azithromycin (12 mg/kg/day, max 500 mg/dose) x 5 days.

A full 10-day course of antibiotics is essential for GABHS pharyngitis to prevent rheumatic fever. Penicillin remains the drug of choice due to excellent efficacy and lack of resistance. There is no increased risk of rheumatic fever with a 48-hour delay in treatment pending culture confirmation.

**Q:** What is infectious mononucleosis (EBV), and how is it managed?

**A:** EBV (Epstein-Barr virus) causes infectious mononucleosis: pharyngitis, tonsillar exudate, fever, lymphadenopathy, fatigue, and rash. Diagnosis: peripheral blood smear and heterophile antibody test (monospot). Management: self-limiting; symptomatic treatment (acetaminophen/NSAIDs); avoid heavy physical activity and contact sports for at least 1 month or until splenomegaly resolves (risk of splenic rupture); corticosteroids and ENT consult if acute airway obstruction.

EBV-associated mononucleosis is important because antibiotics are NOT indicated and may cause a maculopapular rash (especially with amoxicillin/ampicillin). The key danger is splenic rupture from trauma, requiring activity restrictions. Monospot test has high specificity but lower sensitivity in the first week.

**Q:** What are the principles of palliative care?

**A:** Early identification of patients who could benefit; support and treat both patient and family; address physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs; establish a multidisciplinary team; focus on symptom management and comfort; offer therapeutic environment and bereavement support; ensure maintenance of human dignity.

Palliative care is not limited to end-of-life care but should begin early in a serious illness trajectory. It complements curative treatment and aims to improve quality of life through holistic, patient-centred care. The multidisciplinary team may include physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, and others.

**Q:** What is the Cockcroft-Gault formula for creatinine clearance in elderly patients, and why is serum creatinine alone unreliable in the elderly?

**A:** CrCl (mL/min) = (weight in kg × (140 – age) × 1.23) / serum creatinine in μmol/L. Multiply by 0.85 for females. Serum creatinine is unreliable because elderly patients have reduced muscle mass, leading to less creatinine production — normal serum creatinine can mask significantly reduced renal function.

In the elderly, age-related muscle loss means creatinine production is reduced; serum creatinine may appear normal even with significant GFR decline. CrCl calculation helps identify true renal function for dosing medications. It overestimates CrCl when muscle mass is disproportionately reduced beyond normal aging.

**Q:** What is the approach to medication review in elderly patients?

**A:** Ask patient to bring all current medications; review Beers Criteria (2019) for potentially inappropriate medications; consult deprescribing.org; screen for adverse drug effects and interactions; eliminate unnecessary medications (duplicates or redundant pharmacologic effects); simplify dosing regimen; discuss goals of care to guide pharmacologic management; ensure patient understands medications and directions.

Polypharmacy is a major issue in the elderly, leading to adverse drug events, falls, and hospitalizations. The Beers Criteria identifies medications with risk/benefit profiles that are unfavourable in older adults. Deprescribing is an active, evidence-based process of tapering or stopping inappropriate medications.

**Q:** What is the prevalence of complementary and integrative medicine (CAM) use among Canadians, and what are the most common reasons for use?

**A:** 50–75% of Canadians report some CAM use over their lifetime, but only half disclose this to their physician. Most common uses: gynecological problems, anxiety, headaches, digestive problems.

Non-disclosure of CAM use is common and clinically significant due to potential herb-drug interactions and false assurance of treatment. Physicians should routinely ask about CAM use, especially herbal products, using a non-judgmental approach. Examples of CAM include chiropractic, acupuncture, naturopathy, and homeopathy.

**Q:** What are the potential drug interactions of St. John's Wort?

**A:** Interacts with: antidepressants/SSRIs (serotonin syndrome risk), cyclosporine, digoxin, irinotecan (cancer medication), anticoagulants (warfarin), and is contraindicated with indinavir (HIV drug). Also affects CYP450 enzyme system.

St. John's Wort (Hypericum perforatum) is used for mild-to-moderate depression but is a potent CYP450 inducer, reducing plasma levels of many medications. It is one of the most clinically important herb-drug interactions. Adverse effects include photosensitivity, anxiety, dry mouth, GI symptoms, and sexual dysfunction.

**Q:** What are the drug interactions and adverse effects of Ginkgo Biloba?

**A:** Drug interactions: anticoagulants and ASA (increased bleeding risk). Adverse effects: headache, stomach upset, bleeding, severe allergic reactions (whole plant/seeds/pulp). Raw or roasted seeds/pulp are poisonous if ingested.

Ginkgo biloba is used to increase peripheral circulation for conditions like Alzheimer's dementia, intermittent claudication, and tinnitus. The bleeding risk from interaction with anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents (ASA) is clinically significant and should be assessed preoperatively.

**Q:** Which herbal product is used for BPH as an adjunct to finasteride, and what are its known drug interactions?

**A:** Saw palmetto. Drug interactions: none known. Adverse effects include mild GI distress and headache.

Saw palmetto (Serenoa repens) is commonly used for benign prostatic hyperplasia. While some evidence supports modest symptom improvement, clinical evidence is mixed. It is often used alongside finasteride (5-alpha reductase inhibitor) in managing lower urinary tract symptoms.

**Q:** What are the key drug interactions of garlic supplements?

**A:** Interactions with anticoagulants (increased bleeding risk) and saquinavir (HIV drug — reduces plasma levels). May increase postoperative bleeding.

Garlic has antiplatelet effects and can reduce the efficacy of HIV medications like saquinavir. Patients should be counselled to disclose garlic supplement use, especially before surgery or when on anticoagulant therapy. Raw garlic contact dermatitis is a recognized adverse effect.

**Q:** What is the first-line antibiotic treatment for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in an outpatient without comorbidities?

**A:** Amoxicillin 1000 mg PO TID x 5–7 days (for patients >50, where mycoplasma is less likely). If atypical organisms suspected, add clarithromycin 500 mg PO BID or azithromycin 500 mg PO on day 1 then 250 mg daily x 4 days. Second-line: doxycycline 200 mg PO on day 1 then 100 mg PO BID x 5–7 days.

CAP outpatient treatment covers typical organisms (S. pneumoniae) as first priority. Adding a macrolide or using doxycycline covers atypical organisms (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia pneumoniae). Fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) are reserved for treatment failure or patients with comorbidities.

**Q:** What are the two regimens recommended for H. pylori eradication in peptic ulcer disease (non-NSAID related)?

**A:** Option 1 (Triple therapy): PPI PO BID + amoxicillin 1000 mg PO BID + clarithromycin 500 mg PO BID + metronidazole 500 mg PO BID x 7 days. Option 2 (Bismuth quadruple therapy): PPI PO BID + bismuth subsalicylate 525 mg PO QID + metronidazole 500 mg PO QID + tetracycline 500 mg PO QID x 7–14 days.

H. pylori eradication is essential for healing peptic ulcers and preventing recurrence. PPIs include lansoprazole 30 mg, omeprazole 20 mg, pantoprazole 40 mg, or rabeprazole 20 mg. Bismuth quadruple therapy is preferred in regions with high clarithromycin resistance.

**Q:** What is the treatment for Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) diarrhea — mild-to-moderate versus severe disease?

**A:** Mild-to-moderate (WBC <15 x 10⁹/L and creatinine <1.5x baseline): vancomycin 125 mg PO QID x 10–14 days. Severe (WBC ≥15 x 10⁹/L or creatinine ≥1.5x baseline): vancomycin 125 mg PO QID x 10–14 days (same regimen, but higher vigilance and possible fidaxomicin or surgical consult).

C. difficile infection most commonly follows antibiotic use (especially clindamycin). Oral vancomycin is preferred over metronidazole for all disease severity per current guidelines. IV vancomycin is not effective as it does not reach the colon; oral route is essential.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for acute otitis media (AOM) in children under 6 months, and what is appropriate for children aged 6–24 months?

**A:** Under 6 months: treat with antibiotics. Ages 6–24 months: watchful waiting is appropriate if parents can observe child for 48 hours with a plan to reassess in 24–48 hours or return if child worsens; OR provide an antimicrobial prescription to fill if no improvement. First-line: amoxicillin 75–90 mg/kg/day PO divided BID (max 3 g/day) x 10 days if <24 months, 5 days if ≥24 months.

AOM management involves balancing antibiotic benefits against antimicrobial resistance. Young infants (<6 months) require treatment due to immature immune systems. Watchful waiting for 6–24-month-olds reduces unnecessary antibiotic use while maintaining safety through close follow-up.

**Q:** What is the treatment for otitis externa (swimmer's ear), and what is used for necrotizing otitis externa?

**A:** Otitis externa (with or without TM perforation): Ciprodex (ciprofloxacin-dexamethasone) otic suspension 4 drops BID x 7 days. Necrotizing (malignant) otitis externa with bone involvement: ciprofloxacin 750 mg PO BID x 4–8 weeks.

Otitis externa is most commonly caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, coliforms, and S. aureus. Necrotizing otitis externa is a severe form that involves the temporal bone and surrounding structures, occurring mainly in immunocompromised or diabetic patients. Systemic antibiotics with prolonged courses are required for the necrotizing form.

**Q:** What is the preferred antibiotic for traveller's diarrhea, and why?

**A:** Azithromycin is the preferred agent (1000 mg PO single dose OR 500 mg PO daily x 1–3 days) due to growing quinolone resistance, particularly in Southeast Asia. Ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are alternatives.

Traveller's diarrhea is most commonly caused by enterotoxigenic E. coli, Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, viruses, and protozoa. Azithromycin has become preferred due to increasing fluoroquinolone resistance worldwide, especially for Campylobacter. Prevention involves safe food and water precautions.

**Q:** When an STI is detected in a child, what evaluation is mandatory?

**A:** Evaluation for sexual abuse is mandatory.

STIs in children should always raise the suspicion of sexual abuse. Physicians have a mandatory duty to report suspected child abuse to child protective services. A thorough and sensitive forensic evaluation should be conducted by trained professionals.

**Q:** What are the main uses and key drug interaction of Valerian Root as a natural health product?

**A:** Uses: sedative, anxiolytic, depression, menopausal symptoms. Adverse effects: drowsiness, dizziness, headache, itching, digestive problems. Drug interactions: CNS depressants, antihistamines, and other sedatives (additive sedation).

Valerian root (Valeriana officinalis) acts on GABA receptors to produce sedative effects. The interaction with other CNS depressants and antihistamines can lead to excessive sedation. Patients should be counselled not to drive or operate machinery when taking valerian with other sedating substances.

**Q:** What are the adverse effects and contraindications of Feverfew?

**A:** Adverse effects: nausea, GI discomfort, irritation of mouth/skin, 'post-feverfew syndrome' (headaches, sleep disturbances, myalgias, arthralgias after discontinuation), may cause miscarriage (affects uterine contractions). Drug interactions: anticoagulants. Contraindicated in pregnancy.

Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium) is used primarily for migraine prevention. The post-feverfew syndrome on discontinuation is analogous to withdrawal and occurs after prolonged use. Pregnant women must avoid feverfew due to uterotonic effects that can cause miscarriage.

**Q:** What type of pharyngitis causes coxsackievirus (hand, foot, and mouth disease), and what are its distinguishing features?

**A:** Coxsackievirus causes hand, foot, and mouth disease: sudden onset in late summer/early fall; fever, pharyngitis, headache, abdominal pain, vomiting; small vesicles that rupture and ulcerate on soft palate, tonsils, pharynx (pale grey, several mm diameter, surrounding erythema); ulcers may appear on hands and feet.

Hand, foot, and mouth disease is primarily a pediatric illness caused by coxsackievirus A16 and enterovirus 71. It is distinguished from herpangina (coxsackievirus only in posterior oropharynx) and genital herpes (which has larger, fewer ulcers). Treatment is symptomatic only.

**Q:** What natural health products must be avoided in immunosuppressed patients?

**A:** Echinacea should be avoided in immunosuppressed patients (hypersensitivity and potential immune stimulation that may interfere with immunosuppressive therapy).

Echinacea is commonly used for common cold and URTI. Because it stimulates the immune system, it can interfere with immunosuppressive medications used in transplant recipients, autoimmune disease, or cancer therapy. This contraindication is clinically important to screen for in these populations.

**Q:** What is the approach to diagnosis and management when a Modified Centor Score is 3 or more in a patient with sore throat?

**A:** Score ≥3: perform Rapid Antigen Detection Test (RADT). If RADT positive → treat with antibiotics. If RADT negative AND patient <20 years → perform throat culture and treat if positive. If RADT negative AND patient >20 years → no antibiotics should be given.

This stepwise approach balances appropriate antibiotic use with minimizing unnecessary prescribing. In communities with moderate strep prevalence, a score of ≥3 carries a 15–53% probability of GABHS. Age-stratified decisions reflect lower complication risk and lower pretest probability in older adults.

**Q:** What is the non-pharmacologic management of snoring?

**A:** Positional therapy (sleep on side), weight loss, nasal dilators, tongue-retaining devices, mandibular advancement devices. Investigations only if severely symptomatic: nocturnal polysomnography and airway assessment (CT/MRI).

Snoring results from soft tissue vibration at the back of the nose and throat due to turbulent airflow through narrowed air passages. Physical exam findings to assess include obesity, nasal polyps, septal deviation, hypertrophy of nasal turbinates, and enlarged uvula and tonsils. Snoring can be a marker of OSA.

## Family Medicine Smoking Cessation

**Q:** What are the two main pharmacologic agents used for smoking cessation (beyond NRT), and what is the mechanism of each?

**A:** 1. Bupropion: inhibits reuptake of dopamine and/or norepinephrine (NE). 2. Varenicline: partial nicotinic receptor agonist AND partial competitive antagonist of the nicotinic receptor.

Bupropion was originally an antidepressant (Wellbutrin®) repurposed for cessation; therapeutic levels are reached in ~1 week. Varenicline (Champix® in Canada) acts on the same receptor as nicotine, reducing cravings while also blocking nicotine's rewarding effects. Both are available by prescription in Canada.

**Q:** What are the key contraindications to bupropion for smoking cessation?

**A:** Seizure disorder, eating disorder, MAO inhibitor use in the past 14 days, simultaneous use of Wellbutrin® (bupropion) for depression.

Bupropion lowers the seizure threshold, making any pre-existing seizure disorder an absolute contraindication. Eating disorders (especially bulimia/anorexia) also increase seizure risk. Concurrent MAOIs risk hypertensive crisis. Using bupropion for both cessation and depression simultaneously doubles the dose and risk.

**Q:** What is the dosing schedule for bupropion in smoking cessation?

**A:** 150 mg every AM × 3 days, then 150 mg BID × 7–12 weeks. Continue smoking for first 1–2 weeks (therapeutic levels reached in 1 week). For maintenance, consider 150 mg BID for up to 6 months.

The overlap period (continuing to smoke while starting bupropion) allows the drug to reach therapeutic levels before the quit date. This is a key prescribing detail that differentiates bupropion from NRT, where smoking cessation is expected to begin on day 1 of therapy.

**Q:** What is the dosing schedule for varenicline in smoking cessation?

**A:** 0.5 mg every AM × 3 days → 0.5 mg BID × 4 days → 1 mg BID × 12 weeks, with option for additional 12-week course for maintenance. Quit date is typically set for day 8 (after 1 week of varenicline). Gradual quit approach: reduce smoking 50% by week 4, additional 50% by week 8, complete abstinence by week 12.

The gradual titration minimizes the most common side effects (nausea, vomiting). The Canadian prescribing information also notes caution with pre-existing psychiatric conditions, given the risk of psychosis, depression, and suicidal ideation as side effects.

**Q:** What are the 5 Rs of motivational intervention for patients unwilling to quit smoking?

**A:** 1. Relevance (to patient's disease, family, age, gender). 2. Risks of smoking (short-term, long-term, environmental). 3. Rewards (benefits of quitting). 4. Roadblocks (obstacles to quitting, e.g., withdrawal, weight gain). 5. Repetition (reassure that average number of attempts before success is 7).

The 5 Rs are the evidence-based motivational framework for patients in the pre-contemplation or contemplation stages of change. Simple physician advice alone can increase cessation rates by 1–3% above unassisted quit rates, per systematic review evidence cited in Toronto Notes.

**Q:** When is the highest risk period for relapse after smoking cessation, and what is the average number of attempts before successfully quitting?

**A:** Highest relapse rate is within 3 months of quitting. Average number of attempts before successfully quitting is 7.

This knowledge is critical for counselling patients who relapse — relapse should not be framed as failure but as part of the normal process. Prescriptive interventions for recent quitters should address weight gain, negative mood, withdrawal symptoms, and lack of support.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to NRT in pregnant patients who want to quit smoking?

**A:** Counselling is recommended as first-line. NRT should be made available to pregnant women unable to quit using non-pharmacologic methods. Intermittent NRT (lozenges, gum) is preferred over continuous dosing (patch). No strong evidence of major positive or negative outcomes with gestational use of bupropion or varenicline — use only if benefits outweigh risks.

Smoking during pregnancy carries significant risks (prematurity, low birth weight, SIDS, placental abruption), so cessation is a priority. The preference for intermittent NRT over patch reflects reduced fetal nicotine exposure, which is especially important in the first trimester.

## Family Medicine Alcohol

**Q:** What are the four CAGE questions used to screen for alcohol dependence?

**A:** C – Have you ever felt you should Cut down on your drinking? A – Have people Annoyed you by criticizing your drinking? G – Have you ever felt bad or Guilty about your drinking? E – Have you ever had a drink first thing in the morning (Eye opener) to steady your nerves or get rid of a hangover?

Two or more positive responses on the CAGE questionnaire is considered a positive screen, indicating the need for further exploration of alcohol misuse or dependence. In family practice, 10–15% of patients misuse alcohol, and diagnosis is more commonly missed in women, the elderly, and those with high socioeconomic status.

**Q:** What is the typical AST:ALT ratio in alcohol use disorder, and what other lab values are used for monitoring?

**A:** AST:ALT ratio approaches 2:1 in alcohol use disorder. GGT and MCV are used for baseline and follow-up monitoring. CBC (anemia, thrombocytopenia) and INR (decreased clotting factor production) are also checked.

The 2:1 AST:ALT ratio is a classic finding that differentiates alcoholic hepatitis from other causes of hepatitis (where ALT usually exceeds AST). GGT is a sensitive marker for alcohol use, and macrocytosis (elevated MCV) reflects nutritional deficiency (folate, B12) common in chronic alcohol use.

**Q:** What pharmacologic agents are used in the management of alcohol use disorder in Canada?

**A:** 1. Diazepam (benzodiazepine) for withdrawal. 2. Naltrexone: competitive opioid antagonist that reduces cravings and pleasurable effects of drinking (caution: may trigger withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients). 3. Acamprosate: glutamate receptor modulator that reduces craving. Note: Disulfiram (Antabuse®) is no longer available in Canada (available in U.S.).

Disulfiram blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase, causing acetaldehyde accumulation and an aversive reaction (flushing, N/V, hypotension) if alcohol is consumed. Its unavailability in Canada is a high-yield MCCQE1 fact. Acamprosate is preferred for maintenance of abstinence, while naltrexone is effective at reducing heavy drinking days.

**Q:** What percentage of patients in alcohol withdrawal will develop seizures or delirium tremens?

**A:** 10% of patients in alcohol withdrawal will have seizures or delirium tremens.

Alcohol withdrawal is a medical emergency for the subset who develop delirium tremens (DTs), which typically occurs 48–96 hours after the last drink and carries significant mortality if untreated. Benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam) are the treatment of choice for withdrawal management and seizure prophylaxis.

**Q:** What are the criteria for referral to specialist or inpatient alcohol treatment program?

**A:** 1. Dangerous or highly unstable home environment. 2. Severe medical or psychiatric concern. 3. Addiction to a drug that may require inpatient detoxification. 4. Refractory to other treatment programs.

Most patients with alcohol use disorder can be managed in primary care with outpatient or day programs, Alcoholics Anonymous, individualized counselling, and pharmacotherapy. The prognosis is better than commonly perceived: 25–30% exhibit spontaneous improvement over 1 year, and 60–70% of those with jobs and families have improved quality of life 1 year post-treatment.

## Family Medicine Abdominal Pain

**Q:** What are the red flags in a patient presenting with abdominal pain that warrant urgent investigation?

**A:** Severe pain, fever, peritoneal signs, abdominal distention, pain out of proportion to clinical findings, new onset pain/change in pain/altered bowel habits in elderly, weight loss, blood per rectum/melena, jaundice, supraclavicular lymph nodes, family history of serious bowel disease.

Pain that precedes nausea/vomiting makes a serious cause of abdominal pain more likely. The most common diagnosis in family medicine is 'nonspecific abdominal pain' (28%), which is self-limited, but red flags must be systematically excluded. Only 10% of abdominal pain cases are referred to specialists.

**Q:** What is the anatomic basis for pain localization in abdominal pathology? Match each region to the relevant embryologic gut segment.

**A:** Epigastric (foregut): distal esophagus, stomach, proximal duodenum, biliary tree, pancreas, liver. Periumbilical (midgut): distal duodenum to proximal 2/3 of transverse colon. Hypogastric (hindgut): distal 1/3 of transverse colon to rectosigmoid. RUQ: biliary, hepatic, colonic, pulmonary, renal. LUQ: cardiac, gastric, pancreatic, renal, vascular. RLQ: colonic, appendix, gynecological, renal. LLQ: colonic, gynecological, renal.

Visceral pain from abdominal organs is poorly localized and follows embryologic gut segments. Somatic pain from parietal peritoneum is sharp and well-localized. Understanding referred pain patterns (e.g., appendicitis initially periumbilical then migrating to RLQ) is fundamental to abdominal diagnosis.

**Q:** What investigations should be ordered for a patient presenting with acute abdominal pain, and what does each imaging modality best evaluate?

**A:** Blood work: CBC, electrolytes, BUN, Cr, amylase, lipase, AST/ALT/ALP/bilirubin, glucose, INR/PTT, toxicology, β-hCG. Imaging: CXR (free air under diaphragm if perforation), AXR/KUB (gas pattern, free air, renal stones, constipation), U/S (renal stones, gallbladder, gynecological, liver, pancreatitis, appendicitis), CT scan (AAA, appendicitis), non-contrast helical CT (first choice for renal stones). Other: urinalysis, endoscopy, H. pylori testing.

The imaging choice should be guided by the clinical differential. Non-contrast helical CT is preferred for renal stones because stones are radio-dense and contrast is unnecessary. CXR is rapid and identifies free air (pneumoperitoneum) indicating perforation, which would warrant emergent surgical consultation.

## Family Medicine Allergic Rhinitis

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of allergic rhinitis, and how does it differ from vasomotor rhinitis?

**A:** Allergic rhinitis: increased IgE levels to allergens → excessive mast cell degranulation → release of inflammatory mediators (histamine) and cytokines → local inflammatory reaction causing rhinorrhea, sneezing, congestion, and red/watery/itchy eyes. Vasomotor rhinitis: non-allergic, non-IgE-mediated rhinitis triggered by environmental stimuli (cold air, odors, weather changes) without an immune mechanism.

The IgE-mediated mechanism distinguishes allergic rhinitis from other causes and is the rationale for using antihistamines and immunotherapy. Allergic rhinitis affects ~40% of children and 20–30% of adults and is associated with asthma, eczema, sinusitis, and otitis media.

**Q:** What is the stepped management approach for allergic rhinitis?

**A:** 1. Conservative: minimize allergen exposure (most important, may take months), maintain hygiene, saline nasal rinses. 2. Mild symptoms: oral second-generation antihistamines first-line (cetirizine, fexofenadine, loratadine). 3. Moderate/severe or persistent: intranasal corticosteroids ± intranasal antihistamines (expect >1 month for results). 4. Intranasal decongestants: limited to <5 days (avoid rhinitis medicamentosa). 5. Allergy skin testing: for uncontrolled chronic rhinitis. 6. Immunotherapy (allergy shots): severe cases unresponsive to pharmacologic therapy.

Rhinitis medicamentosa (rebound congestion) occurs with prolonged use (>5–7 days) of vasoconstrictive intranasal medications. Immunotherapy uses periodic subcutaneous injections of allergen-specific antigens to desensitize the patient — an important option for severe allergic rhinitis.

**Q:** What is rhinitis medicamentosa and how is it prevented?

**A:** Rhinitis medicamentosa is rebound nasal congestion caused by prolonged use (>5–7 days) of vasoconstrictive intranasal medications (e.g., oxymetazoline). Prevention: limit use of intranasal decongestants to <5 days.

With repeated use, alpha-adrenergic nasal decongestants cause tachyphylaxis — patients become dependent, requiring more frequent dosing to achieve the same decongestant effect. The resulting rebound congestion can be worse than the original symptoms. Treatment involves gradual discontinuation, often with intranasal corticosteroids.

## Family Medicine Anxiety

**Q:** What are the GAD-7 mnemonic symptoms of Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)?

**A:** F-A-C-R-E-S-T: Feeling anxious/nervous (excessive worry), Ability to control worry is absent, Concentration impairment, Restlessness, Energy decreased, Sleep impairment, Tension in muscles. Duration must be >6 months.

The GAD-7 tool is used to both screen and monitor anxiety severity. In primary care, 25–30% of patients have psychiatric disorders, and many are undiagnosed. Organic causes (hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, COPD, arrhythmias) and drug causes (amphetamines, caffeine, benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal) must be excluded before diagnosing GAD.

**Q:** What medical and substance-related causes must be ruled out before diagnosing an anxiety disorder?

**A:** Cardiac: post-MI, arrhythmias. Endocrine: hyperthyroidism, diabetes, pheochromocytoma. Respiratory: asthma, COPD. Psychiatric: somatoform, psychotic, mood disorders (depression, bipolar), personality disorders. Drugs: amphetamines, thyroid preparations, caffeine, OTC cold/decongestants, alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal, beta-agonists.

Pheochromocytoma classically mimics panic disorder with episodic sympathetic surges. Hyperthyroidism presents with anxiety, tremor, palpitations, and weight loss. Beta-agonist inhalers (e.g., salbutamol) can cause tremor and tachycardia resembling anxiety. Thorough history and physical are essential before labeling as primary anxiety.

**Q:** What is the management approach for anxiety disorders in primary care?

**A:** 1. Patient education (emphasize prevalence, good recovery rate). 2. Lifestyle modifications: exercise, reduce caffeine/alcohol (collaborative, person-centered plan). 3. Psychological: CBT, exposure therapy, relaxation techniques, mindfulness. 4. Pharmacotherapy (see Psychiatry). 5. Self-help materials and community resources/support groups.

CBT is the most evidence-based psychotherapy for anxiety disorders. In primary care, a stepped care approach is used — starting with psychoeducation and lifestyle changes before escalating to therapy and medication. Co-existing depression is common and should be screened for concurrently.

## Family Medicine Asthma Copd

**Q:** What are the key differences between COPD and asthma in terms of age of onset, reversibility, role of smoking, and spirometry findings?

**A:** COPD: onset usually 6th decade, >10 pack-year smoking (causal), airflow obstruction is chronic/persistent/not fully reversible, slow progressive worsening, diffusion capacity decreased, hypoxemia chronic in advanced stages. Asthma: any age (50% diagnosed in children), smoking not causal (known trigger), airflow obstruction episodic and usually reversible with therapy, marked improvement with bronchodilators or steroids, diffusion capacity normal, hypoxemia episodic only with severe attacks.

The hallmark symptom of asthma is wheeze, while COPD presents with chronic cough, sputum, and/or dyspnea. Asthma cannot be diagnosed at first presentation. Spirometry can be done starting at age 6. History of allergy is present in >50% of asthmatics but infrequent in COPD.

**Q:** What are the signs of poorly controlled asthma?

**A:** Beta-2 agonist (SABA) use more than 3 times per week, asthma-related absence from work/school, exercise-induced asthma, night-time symptoms more than 1 time per week.

These are key monitoring targets in asthma management. Frequent SABA use signals inadequate control and the need to step up therapy (e.g., add low-dose ICS). Regular use of a peak flow meter helps patients self-monitor at home. All poorly controlled asthmatics should have a written asthma action plan.

**Q:** Outline the stepwise management of COPD.

**A:** Mild – Step 1: SABA as needed (salbutamol). Step 2: SABA as needed + LAAC (tiotropium) OR + LABA (salmeterol). Moderate – Step 3: SABA/SAMA + LABA, or SABA + LABA, or LAAC; consider inhaled vs. oral steroids. Severe – Step 4: As above + pneumococcal vaccination + annual influenza immunization. All steps: smoking cessation, ongoing patient education, environmental control.

LAAC = long-acting anticholinergic (tiotropium/umeclidinium). LABA = long-acting beta-agonist. The backbone of COPD management differs from asthma — ICS are not first-line in COPD (unlike asthma), and anticholinergics play a central role. Vaccinations are particularly important in COPD due to increased risk of exacerbations.

**Q:** Outline the stepwise management of asthma.

**A:** All steps: SABA (salbutamol) as rescue medication + maintenance meds, smoking cessation, patient education. Mild – Step 1: Low-dose ICS. Step 2: Medium/high-dose ICS, or low-dose ICS + LABA, or leukotriene modifier. Moderate – Step 3: Medium/high-dose ICS + LABA or LT modifier. Severe – Step 4: As above + immunotherapy + oral glucocorticosteroids + pneumococcal + annual influenza immunization.

ICS are the cornerstone of asthma maintenance therapy at all levels beyond Step 1. SABA (salbutamol) is rescue only — its frequent use (>3x/week) signals inadequate control. Leukotriene modifiers (e.g., montelukast) are alternatives to LABA in mild-to-moderate asthma. Spacers should always be used with MDI inhalers to reduce local side effects and improve lung deposition.

**Q:** What are the environmental advantages of DPI over MDI inhalers, and what is the key technique difference?

**A:** MDIs have 20–30 times larger carbon footprints than DPIs (e.g., Accuhaler, Ellipta). DPIs require deep and fast breathing (may not be ideal for young children or during acute exacerbations). MDIs should be used with spacers to reduce local side effects, improve lung deposition, and increase efficiency.

There is a large clinical push to transition from MDIs to DPIs to reduce environmental impact of aerosol propellants. Nebulizers are recommended when there are contraindications to MDIs. Salbutamol (MDI) may cause hypokalemia — monitor for lethargy, irritability, paresthesias, myalgias, weakness, palpitations, N/V, polyuria.

## Family Medicine Bph

**Q:** What is the definition of BPH, and how does the prostate feel on DRE in BPH vs. prostate cancer?

**A:** BPH is hyperplasia of the stroma and epithelium in the periurethral transition zone. On DRE: BPH prostate is symmetrically enlarged, smooth, and rubbery. Prostate cancer: typically has asymmetric enlargement, hardness, or nodularity.

The transition zone (not the peripheral zone) is affected in BPH, while adenocarcinoma of the prostate most commonly arises in the peripheral zone (palpable on DRE). This anatomic distinction explains why BPH does not predispose to prostate cancer. DRE is a mandatory part of the BPH assessment.

**Q:** Distinguish the obstructive and irritative symptoms of BPH, and list the late complications.

**A:** Obstructive: hesitancy, diminished stream size and force, stream interruption (double voiding), urinary retention, post-void dribbling, overflow incontinence, nocturia. Irritative: urgency, frequency, nocturia, urge incontinence, dysuria. Late complications: hydronephrosis, loss of renal concentrating ability, systemic acidosis, renal failure.

Obstructive symptoms result from mechanical obstruction of the bladder outlet, while irritative (storage) symptoms result from detrusor instability secondary to chronic obstruction. Nocturia appears in both categories. Late complications reflect back-pressure effects on the upper urinary tract — a urological emergency if bilateral hydronephrosis develops.

**Q:** What is the role of PSA in BPH management, and when is PSA testing inappropriate?

**A:** PSA is no longer used to screen/diagnose BPH due to high false positive rate; instead used as a marker to guide treatment once diagnosis is made. PSA testing is inappropriate in: men with life expectancy <10 years, patients with prostatitis or UTI (can falsely elevate PSA). Increased PSA in a younger man is more often due to cancer than other causes. Discuss PSA testing with men at increased risk (family history, Black men) or those concerned about prostate cancer.

PSA is prostate-specific (not prostate cancer-specific) — it can be elevated by BPH, prostatitis, UTI, and physical manipulation (e.g., DRE, catheterization, cycling). Abnormal DRE or PSA should trigger further assessment (urology referral). The decision to test asymptomatic men should follow shared decision-making.

**Q:** What pharmacologic agents are used for moderate-to-severe BPH, and what are the mechanisms of each?

**A:** 1. Alpha-receptor antagonists (terazosin, doxazosin, tamsulosin, alfuzosin): relax smooth muscle around the prostate and bladder neck, improving urinary flow. 2. 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride): inhibit conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone, reducing prostate growth (only for demonstrated prostatic enlargement; maximum effect at 6–12 months). 3. Antimuscarinics and beta-3 agonists: for storage symptoms (avoid in bladder outlet obstruction/elevated PVR). 4. Long-acting PDE5 inhibitor: for patients with concurrent erectile dysfunction. 5. Desmopressin: for nocturia from nocturnal polyuria (watch for hyponatremia in older adults).

Alpha-blockers provide rapid symptom relief while 5-ARIs provide long-term size reduction. Combination therapy (alpha-blocker + 5-ARI) is often used for large prostates. Finasteride takes 6–12 months for maximum effect and can reduce PSA by ~50% (must be factored into PSA interpretation).

**Q:** What are the absolute indications for surgical intervention (TURP/TUIP) in BPH?

**A:** Absolute indications: failed medical therapy, intractable urinary retention, benign prostatic obstruction leading to renal insufficiency. Complications of TURP include: impotence, incontinence, ejaculatory difficulties (retrograde ejaculation), and decreased libido. TUIP is preferred for prostates <30 g.

TURP (transurethral resection of prostate) is the gold-standard surgical treatment for moderate-to-severe BPH. Retrograde ejaculation (semen enters bladder instead of being expelled anteriorly) occurs in up to 90% of TURP patients and is the most common sexual side effect. It should be discussed pre-operatively.

## Family Medicine Bronchitis

**Q:** What is the etiology of acute bronchitis, and what is the evidence base for antibiotic use?

**A:** 80% viral (rhinovirus, coronavirus, adenovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, RSV), 20% bacterial (M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae). Current literature does NOT support routine antibiotic use in acute bronchitis because it is most often viral. Antibiotics may be useful for those with comorbidities, suspected pneumonia, sepsis, or older adults. Antibiotics show no benefit in children.

Acute bronchitis is the 5th most common diagnosis in family medicine. The most common cause is URTI. A key clinical point: purulent sputum does NOT indicate bacterial infection — viral bronchitis commonly produces mucopurulent sputum. CXR should be ordered only if pneumonia is suspected (abnormal vitals, localized chest findings).

## Family Medicine Chest Pain

**Q:** What are the differential diagnoses of chest pain in primary care, and what clinical finding best distinguishes pericarditis from other causes?

**A:** Differential: acute MI, chest wall pain, GERD, panic disorder/anxiety, pericarditis, pneumonia, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, acute thoracic aortic dissection. Pericarditis: classic triad of pleuritic chest pain (worse with inspiration and lying down, better sitting forward), pericardial friction rub, and ECG changes (diffuse ST elevation and PR depression without T-wave inversion).

The 'sit forward' sign is pathognomonic for pericarditis — the pericardial space enlarges when leaning forward, reducing friction. In contrast, MI pain is typically constant and not positional. Aortic dissection presents with severe tearing chest/back pain and pulse differential in upper extremities (LR+ 5.3).

**Q:** How does MI present differently in elderly women and diabetic patients compared to classic presentation?

**A:** Elderly women: may present with dizziness, back pain, lightheadedness, or symptoms different from the stereotypical presentation (atypical presentation). Diabetics: may present with dyspnea, syncope, and fatigue in the absence of chest pain (due to autonomic neuropathy causing silent ischemia).

Atypical MI presentations are a major source of missed diagnoses. Autonomic neuropathy in long-standing diabetes blunts the perception of ischemic pain (silent MI). Women are also more likely to present with nausea, jaw pain, or fatigue as the dominant symptom. ECG and cardiac enzymes are essential regardless of presentation.

**Q:** What is the immediate management of acute MI in primary care before transfer to emergency?

**A:** ASA 160–325 mg chewed STAT, clopidogrel (Plavix®) or ticagrelor (Brilinta®), LMWH (enoxaparin), morphine, oxygen, NTG. Reperfusion therapy with fibrinolytics (tPA, reteplase, tenecteplase, or streptokinase) if within 12 hours (ideally <30 min), or PCI if time from first medical contact to catheter lab is <30 min. Start beta-blocker (e.g., metoprolol 25 mg PO q6h or BID, titrate to HR 55–60 bpm).

The mnemonic MONA (Morphine, Oxygen, Nitrates, Aspirin) captures the initial steps. ASA should be chewed (not swallowed whole) for faster absorption. Fibrinolytics are used when PCI is not available within the required timeframe. Beta-blockers reduce myocardial oxygen demand and limit infarct size.

## Family Medicine Common Cold

**Q:** What differentiates the common cold from influenza clinically?

**A:** Flu: sudden onset, severe exhaustion, dry/severe/hacking cough, dry and clear nose, achy muscles and head, decreased appetite. Cold: slow onset, mild exhaustion, mild cough (+/-), runny nose, headache-free, normal muscles, normal appetite. Both have chills and can have fever, but fever is more prominent in flu.

Distinguishing flu from cold has management implications — flu may benefit from antivirals (oseltamivir) if given within 48 hours of symptom onset, especially in high-risk patients (elderly, immunocompromised, pregnant). The common cold has no specific antiviral therapy. Common cold is the most common diagnosis in family medicine (adults 2–4 per year, children 6–10 per year).

**Q:** What is the management of the common cold, including evidence on echinacea?

**A:** Patient education: symptoms peak 1–3 days, subside within 1 week (cough may persist weeks). No antibiotics (viral). Prevention: frequent hand washing, avoid mucous membrane contact, surface disinfectants, annual influenza vaccination. Symptomatic relief: rest, hydration, gargling warm salt water, steam, nasal irrigation, analgesics/antipyretics (acetaminophen/ibuprofen), cough suppression (dextromethorphan or codeine; children <6 should not use any cough/cold medications), decongestants, antihistamines. Echinacea: not shown to provide benefits for treating colds; possible weak benefit from some products; 10–20% relative risk reduction in prevention trials but not statistically significant.

The recommendation against cough/cold medications in children <6 reflects safety concerns about serious adverse events with minimal benefit. Secondary bacterial complications (otitis media, sinusitis, bronchitis) can appear 3–10 days after cold onset — a new fever or worsening after initial improvement should prompt reassessment.

## Family Medicine Concussion

**Q:** What assessment tools are used for concussion, and which tool is used for whom and when?

**A:** SCAT6 (Sport Concussion Assessment Tool, 6th edition): for individuals >13 years, used within first 3 days post-injury (acute assessment, e.g., sidelines). SCOAT6 (Sport Concussion Office Assessment Tool-6): for individuals >13 years and >3 days post-injury (clinical evaluation and management). Child SCAT6: for individuals <13 years (acute concussion assessment).

Parachute Canada provides national concussion guidelines including return-to-play/return-to-sports protocols. The distinction between sideline assessment tools (SCAT6) and office tools (SCOAT6) is important for practical application. Mild TBI/concussion management follows a graduated return-to-activity protocol.

## Family Medicine Contraception

**Q:** What are the options for emergency contraception (EC), and how do they compare in effectiveness?

**A:** 1. Hormonal EC (Plan B® / Yuzpe® — 2 doses 12 hours apart): effective within 72 hours (reduces pregnancy risk by 75–85%); most effective within 24 hours; some effectiveness up to 5 days. Plan B® is available OTC at pharmacies across Canada. EC Ella® can be taken up to 5 days post-intercourse. 2. Copper IUD: inserted within 5 days of unprotected intercourse; ~99% effective (significantly more effective than hormonal EC). Note: hormonal EC does NOT affect an established pregnancy. Pregnancy test should be done if no menstrual bleeding within 21 days.

The copper IUD is the most effective form of EC and can also serve as ongoing contraception. Advance provision of hormonal EC increases its use without decreasing regular contraceptive use. Pharmacists across Canada can dispense Plan B® OTC — an important access point for patients.

## Family Medicine Dementia

**Q:** What are the leading causes of dementia in Canada and their relative prevalences?

**A:** Alzheimer's disease: 40–50%. Mixed dementia: 20–25%. Lewy-Body dementia: 5–15%. Vascular dementia: 5–10%. Frontotemporal dementia: 5–10%. Overall: 15% of Canadians ≥65 years are living with dementia; risk doubles every 5 years after age 65.

Alzheimer's disease is the most common form. Vascular and mixed dementias have the highest prevalence of co-morbid depression. Depression prevalence in dementia ranges from 20–60%, with major depression decreasing as dementia severity increases. Screening for depression is an important ongoing component of dementia management.

**Q:** What investigations are used to work up dementia, and what reversible causes must be excluded?

**A:** Best screening tool: MOCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment). Also use MMSE, Dementia Quick Screen, history, physical exam. Investigations to exclude reversible causes: CBC, liver enzymes, TSH (hypothyroidism), renal function, serum electrolytes, serum calcium, serum glucose, vitamin B12, folate, VDRL (syphilis), HIV, head CT. The Mini-Cog (3 words recalled + clock drawing — numbers and hands showing 10:10) is used for quick screening.

Reversible causes of dementia include hypothyroidism, B12/folate deficiency, syphilis, HIV, normal pressure hydrocephalus (triad: gait apraxia, urinary incontinence, cognitive decline), and medication effects. MOCA is more sensitive than MMSE for mild cognitive impairment. The Mini-Cog takes ~3 minutes and is practical in primary care.

**Q:** What pharmacologic agents are used to slow dementia progression and manage behavioral symptoms?

**A:** To slow cognitive decline: N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists (e.g., memantine) and cholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine). For behavioral and emotional symptoms: low-dose antipsychotics and antidepressants.

Cholinesterase inhibitors are first-line for Alzheimer's disease and Lewy-Body dementia (where they improve cognition and hallucinations). Memantine is used for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's and can be combined with cholinesterase inhibitors. Antipsychotics in elderly patients with dementia carry a black box warning for increased mortality from cardiovascular events and infections.

## Family Medicine Depression

**Q:** What are the PHQ-2 screening questions for depression, and what score triggers use of the PHQ-9?

**A:** PHQ-2 questions (over past 2 weeks): 1. 'How often have you been bothered by little interest or pleasure in doing things?' 2. 'How often have you been bothered by feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?' Each scored 0–3; total score out of 6. A score of ≥3 is considered positive → proceed to PHQ-9 for full diagnostic evaluation. The Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (2013) recommends NOT routinely screening for depression in the general population.

PHQ-9 screens for and monitors all 9 DSM criteria for major depressive disorder. GDS (Geriatric Depression Scale) is used for the elderly population. At least 5 out of 9 criteria (including anhedonia or depressed mood for >2 weeks) must be met for diagnosis. Early diagnosis and treatment improve outcomes.

**Q:** What are the phases of depression treatment and their durations?

**A:** 1. Acute phase (8–12 weeks): relieve symptoms, improve quality of life; counsel on risks (sexual side effects, discontinuation syndrome). 2. Maintenance phase (6–12 months after symptom resolution): prevent relapse/recurrence; stress importance of continuing medication for full duration.

Two-thirds of patients may not receive appropriate treatment for depression. Stopping antidepressants too early (when the patient 'feels better') is the most common cause of relapse. Combination therapy (pharmacotherapy + psychotherapy) is synergistic and most effective, showing significantly greater improvements than medications alone (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.38–2.52).

**Q:** What is the first-line SSRI for depression in youth (ages 10–21), and what monitoring is required?

**A:** Fluoxetine is first-line among SSRIs in youth (most evidence). For mild depression: a period of active support and monitoring before initiating treatment is recommended. Monitor closely for adverse effects including suicidal ideation and behavior. Psychotherapy (CBT or IPT alone) can be used for mild depression. Psychotherapy plus medication for moderate to severe depression. Reassess and refer if no improvement after 6–8 weeks. Consider referral for adolescents with moderate/severe depression + psychosis or substance misuse.

The increased risk of suicidal ideation with antidepressants in youth (not actual suicide) is a black box warning in Canada and the US. This risk is highest in the first 1–4 weeks of therapy. Despite this, untreated depression carries its own significant suicide risk. Regular monitoring (especially in the first month) is essential.

**Q:** What must be ruled out before diagnosing depression, and what medical conditions can cause depressive symptoms?

**A:** Must rule out: suicidal/homicidal ideation, psychosis, substance use/misuse/withdrawal, bipolar/manic/hypomanic episodes, postpartum depression. Medical causes: Cancer (especially oropharyngeal, lung, pancreas — 50% develop depression before cancer diagnosis), chronic fatigue syndrome, early dementia, endocrine (hypo/hyperthyroidism, DM, adrenal disorders), infections (mononucleosis), liver/renal failure, medication side effects (beta-blockers, benzodiazepines, glucocorticoids, interferon), menopause, neurological (Parkinson's, MS), vitamin deficiency (pernicious anemia, pellagra).

Pancreatic cancer is classically associated with depression presenting before the cancer is diagnosed — a high-yield MCCQE1 fact. Hypothyroidism mimics depression very closely (fatigue, weight gain, psychomotor slowing, low mood). TSH should be checked in all new presentations of depression.

**Q:** What is Form 1 in Ontario and under what circumstances is it completed by a physician?

**A:** Form 1 is an application by a physician to hospitalize a patient against their will for psychiatric assessment for up to 72 hours. It is completed when a patient with depression (or other psychiatric condition) is deemed high-risk for suicide or homicide.

Form 1 is used under the Ontario Mental Health Act. It allows involuntary psychiatric assessment but NOT treatment — the patient still has the right to refuse treatment after assessment. After 72 hours, the patient must be further certified (Form 3) for continued involuntary admission if necessary. This is an Ontario-specific legal instrument important for the MCCQE1.

## Family Medicine Fever

**Q:** What are the diagnostic temperature thresholds for fever in adults?

**A:** Oral temperature >37.2°C (AM) or >37.7°C (PM); rectal temperature 37.5°C–38.3°C; axillary temperature >37.2°C.

Fever thresholds vary by measurement site and time of day due to normal diurnal variation. Rectal temperature is the gold standard in children <2 years; tympanic membrane (TM) measurement is not accurate until age >5 years.

**Q:** Why is a rectal temperature required for children <2 years old with fever, and when does TM temperature become reliable?

**A:** Rectal temperature is required in children <2 yr for accuracy. Tympanic membrane (TM) measurement is not accurate until the child is >5 years old.

Young children have ear canal anatomy and compliance that makes TM infrared readings unreliable. Rectal measurement directly reflects core temperature, which is the most clinically relevant in febrile young infants where early sepsis detection is critical.

**Q:** List the four main categories of differential diagnosis for fever.

**A:** 1. Infection (bacterial, viral, other)
2. Cancer (leukemia, lymphoma, other malignancies)
3. Medications (e.g., allopurinol, captopril, cimetidine, heparin, INH, meperidine, nifedipine, phenytoin, diuretics, barbiturates, antihistamines)
4. Other (Inflammatory Bowel Disease, Collagen Vascular Disease)

Drug-induced fever is often overlooked; offending agents include many common medications. Always review the medication list in a patient with unexplained fever. Neoplasms cause fever through cytokine release and tumour necrosis.

**Q:** What investigations are appropriate in the workup of a febrile patient?

**A:** CBC and differential, blood culture, urine culture, urinalysis; viral swab (influenza, COVID-19); stool O&P, Gram stain, culture; CXR, Mantoux skin test, sputum culture; lumbar puncture (if meningitis suspected).

Investigation should be guided by history and examination findings to identify the source. Lumbar puncture is reserved for suspected meningitis/encephalitis. A comprehensive initial screen helps narrow the differential between infectious, inflammatory, and malignant causes.

**Q:** What is the management of fever in the outpatient setting?

**A:** Increase fluid intake; general cooling measures (sponge bath, light clothing); acetaminophen or ibuprofen as needed; treat the underlying cause.

Antipyretics provide symptomatic relief and reduce metabolic demand. Treating the underlying cause is essential — antibiotics only if bacterial infection confirmed or strongly suspected. Aspirin is avoided in children due to Reye syndrome risk.

## Family Medicine Headache

**Q:** What are the primary and secondary categories of headache, and which is more common in family medicine?

**A:** Primary headaches (tension, migraine, cluster) are the most common in family medicine. Secondary headaches are caused by underlying organic disease and account for <10% of all headaches — but may be life-threatening.

Secondary causes must be ruled out before diagnosing a primary headache disorder. Key secondary causes include vascular (SAH, aneurysm), neoplastic, infectious (meningitis), traumatic, and drug-related etiologies.

**Q:** What are the POUND criteria for diagnosing migraine?

**A:** P — Pulsatile quality
O — Duration Over 4–72 hours
U — Unilateral
N — Nausea and vomiting
D — Disabling intensity

If ≥4 criteria are present, migraine diagnosis is likely (positive likelihood ratio = 24).

POUND is a validated clinical screening tool. A positive likelihood ratio of 24 is very high, making it clinically useful for confident diagnosis of migraine in primary care without imaging.

**Q:** What are the red flags (SNOOP) in headache evaluation that warrant urgent investigation?

**A:** S — Systemic symptoms (fever, anticoagulation, pregnancy)
N — Neurologic signs/symptoms (impaired mental status, focal neurological deficits, neck stiffness)
O — Onset sudden and severe (thunderclap)
O — Other associated conditions (following head trauma, awakens from sleep, jaw claudication, temporal artery tenderness)
P — Prior headache history change (different pattern, progressive worsening)

Also: new headache after age 50.

Red flag headaches require neuroimaging (CT or MRI) and possibly lumbar puncture to exclude SAH, meningitis, or mass lesion. Thunderclap headache mandates CT head immediately followed by LP if CT is negative.

**Q:** List the causes of secondary headaches by category (vascular, infectious, drug, endocrine, neoplastic, traumatic).

**A:** Vascular: aneurysm, stroke, SAH, HTN, temporal arteritis
Infectious: meningitis, encephalitis, abscess
Drug: withdrawal, medication overuse, side effects, carbon monoxide
Endocrine: thyroid disease, pheochromocytoma
Neoplastic: tumour
Traumatic: TMJ injury, c-spine injury, head injury, subdural hematoma, SAH
Other: ophthalmological, otolaryngological causes

Temporal arteritis (giant cell arteritis) is a vascular cause especially important in patients >50 with new onset headache and elevated ESR/CRP — can lead to irreversible vision loss if not treated promptly with steroids.

**Q:** When are investigations indicated in headache, and what are the options?

**A:** Investigations are indicated only when red flags are present:
- CBC: for suspected systemic or intracranial infection
- ESR or CRP: for suspected temporal arteritis
- Neuroimaging (CT or MRI): to rule out intracranial pathology
- CSF analysis: for suspected intracranial hemorrhage or infection

Routine neuroimaging is not indicated for primary headaches without red flags, as it adds cost without improving outcomes. However, suspected temporal arteritis requires urgent ESR/CRP and same-day ophthalmology/rheumatology referral.

**Q:** What acupuncture evidence exists for migraine prophylaxis?

**A:** Cochrane meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (n=4,985): acupuncture moderately reduced headache frequency compared to no acupuncture. Acupuncture showed a small but statistically significant reduction vs. sham acupuncture post-treatment and at follow-up. Acupuncture is similarly effective to pharmacological prophylaxis for migraine, and superiority is maintained at follow-up.

Adding acupuncture to symptomatic attack treatment reduces headache frequency. The modest but consistent evidence supports offering acupuncture as a non-pharmacological prophylaxis option for patients who prefer to avoid or cannot tolerate medications such as topiramate or propranolol.

## Family Medicine Hearing

**Q:** What are the two main classifications of hearing loss and how do they differ?

**A:** 1. Conductive: external sound does not reach the inner ear (problem with external or middle ear)
2. Sensorineural: involving the inner ear, cochlea, or auditory nerve
3. Mixed: combination of both

Rinne and Weber tuning fork tests distinguish between conductive (Rinne negative; Weber lateralizes to affected ear) and sensorineural (Rinne positive; Weber lateralizes to unaffected ear) hearing loss. 90% of age-related hearing loss (presbycusis) is sensorineural.

**Q:** How is the whispered-voice test performed and what does it assess?

**A:** The examiner stands 0.6 metres (arm's length) behind the patient, whispers a combination of 6 letters/numbers, and asks the patient to repeat the sequence. One ear is tested at a time while the non-test ear is simultaneously masked. It screens for hearing impairment.

The whispered-voice test is a simple, validated screening tool for hearing loss in elderly patients. Inability to correctly repeat the sequence suggests significant hearing impairment warranting formal audiologic assessment.

**Q:** What is the management approach for unexplained unilateral sudden sensorineural hearing loss?

**A:** Treat urgently with steroids. Refer to ENT specialist for ongoing care. Consider investigations: blood glucose, CBC and differential, TSH, syphilis testing.

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is a medical emergency. Prompt systemic corticosteroids (within 2 weeks of onset) improve outcomes. Syphilis and thyroid disease are treatable reversible causes. MRI is indicated if progressive asymmetric SNHL to exclude vestibular schwannoma.

**Q:** When should MRI be ordered for a patient with sensorineural hearing loss?

**A:** MRI should be considered for patients with progressive asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss to exclude vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma).

Vestibular schwannoma is a benign tumour of the 8th cranial nerve that typically presents with unilateral SNHL and tinnitus. Early detection allows for less invasive management. MRI with gadolinium is the gold standard for diagnosis.

## Family Medicine Hypertension

**Q:** What is the Canadian epidemiology of hypertension?

**A:** 22% of Canadian adults have HTN (52% prevalence in ages 60–70). Lifetime risk of developing HTN is approximately 90%. 64% of Canadians with HTN are treated and controlled; 17% are unaware they have HTN. HTN is the 3rd leading risk factor associated with death.

Despite high awareness and treatment rates in Canada, a significant proportion remain uncontrolled. HTN is a major modifiable risk factor for CAD, HF, cerebrovascular disease, renal failure, and PVD, making population-level management critical.

**Q:** Define the BP thresholds for diagnosing hypertension in diabetic vs. non-diabetic patients using office measurement.

**A:** Diabetic patients: OBPM >130/80 mmHg for ≥3 measurements on different days = probable HTN
Non-diabetic patients: AOBP >135/85 mmHg OR OBPM ≥140/90 mmHg = probable HTN (then rule out white coat HTN)
ABPM: daytime mean >135/85 or 24-hr mean >130/80 = HTN
HBPM series mean ≥135/85 = HTN
AOBP or OBPM sBP ≥180 or dBP ≥110 mmHg = HTN regardless

Hypertension Canada uses lower thresholds for diabetic patients given their increased cardiovascular risk. Automated office BP (AOBP) avoids white coat effect by taking multiple readings automatically without a provider present.

**Q:** Distinguish between hypertensive urgency and hypertensive emergency.

**A:** Hypertensive urgency: sBP >210 mmHg or dBP >120 mmHg with minimal or no target-organ damage.
Hypertensive emergency: severe HTN (dBP >120 mmHg) WITH acute target-organ damage — acute heart failure, MI, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury, hypertensive encephalopathy, hypertensive retinopathy.
Accelerated HTN: significant recent BP rise with vascular damage on fundoscopy but without papilledema.
Malignant HTN: BP sufficient to cause papilledema plus other vascular damage (retinal hemorrhages, bulging discs, mental status changes, rising creatinine).

The key distinction is end-organ damage. Hypertensive emergency requires immediate IV antihypertensive therapy (carefully controlled reduction) and hospitalization. Urgency can be managed with oral agents and close follow-up.

**Q:** What is white coat hypertension vs. masked hypertension?

**A:** White coat HTN: high clinic BP with normal home and 24-hr ambulatory BP — caused by anxiety in the clinical setting.
Masked HTN: normal clinic BP with high BP at home and/or ambulatory setting — often provoked by anxiety, job stress, or exercise.

Both conditions are identified by comparing office BP with ABPM or HBPM. White coat HTN carries lower CV risk than sustained HTN but may progress. Masked HTN paradoxically carries higher CV risk than white coat HTN and requires treatment.

**Q:** What are the most common causes of secondary hypertension?

**A:** Renal: renovascular HTN, renal parenchymal disease (glomerulonephritis, pyelonephritis, polycystic kidney disease)
Endocrine: primary hyperaldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, Cushing's syndrome, hyperthyroidism/hyperparathyroidism, hypercalcemia
Vascular: coarctation of the aorta, renal artery stenosis
Drug-induced: estrogens/OCP, steroids, NSAIDs, MAOIs, lithium, decongestants, cocaine, amphetamines, alcohol

Secondary HTN accounts for ~10% of cases but is important to identify as many causes are reversible. Primary hyperaldosteronism is the most common endocrine cause. Suspect renovascular HTN with sudden onset, young age, abdominal bruit, or HTN resistant to ≥3 drugs.

**Q:** When should you suspect primary hyperaldosteronism in a hypertensive patient?

**A:** Suspect when:
- HTN refractory to treatment with >3 drugs
- Spontaneous hypokalemia
- Profound diuretic-induced hypokalemia (<3.0 mmol/L)

Investigate with plasma aldosterone and plasma renin (aldosterone-to-renin ratio) from morning samples taken in the sitting position after 15 min rest.

Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome) is the most common surgically correctable cause of HTN. A high aldosterone-to-renin ratio is the screening test. Discontinue aldosterone antagonists, ARBs, β-blockers, and clonidine prior to testing as they affect results.

**Q:** What baseline investigations are indicated for ALL patients with newly diagnosed hypertension?

**A:** Electrolytes, creatinine, fasting blood glucose and/or HbA1c, lipid profile, 12-lead ECG, urinalysis. Self-measurement of BP at home is also encouraged.

These investigations assess for target organ damage (ECG for LVH; urinalysis for proteinuria), identify contributing metabolic factors (dyslipidemia, DM), and establish a baseline for monitoring treatment effects on renal function and electrolytes.

**Q:** What are the lifestyle modification targets for hypertension management per Canadian guidelines?

**A:** - Diet: DASH diet, limit salt to 5 g/day (2000 mg Na), increase dietary potassium
- Exercise: moderate-intensity dynamic exercise 30–60 min, 4–7×/week
- Smoking cessation
- Low-risk alcohol consumption
- Healthy BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m²) and waist circumference (<102 cm men, <88 cm women)
- Individualized stress reduction
Note: potassium/magnesium/calcium supplements are NOT recommended for HTN

Lifestyle changes can reduce sBP by 2–14 mmHg depending on the intervention (e.g., weight loss of 10 kg reduces sBP ~5–20 mmHg; sodium restriction reduces sBP ~2–8 mmHg). These are first-line and must continue alongside pharmacotherapy.

**Q:** What are the BP treatment targets per Hypertension Canada guidelines?

**A:** <140/90 mmHg for most patients.
<130/80 mmHg for patients with diabetes mellitus.

Lower targets in diabetics reflect their higher baseline cardiovascular risk. High-risk populations (Framingham Risk >20%, age >50) should initiate treatment at sBP ≥130 mmHg. The 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines target <130/80 for most — Canadian guidelines are somewhat less aggressive.

**Q:** What are the indications to initiate antihypertensive pharmacotherapy?

**A:** - sBP ≥140 mmHg or dBP ≥90 mmHg WITH target organ damage or CV risk factors
- sBP ≥160 mmHg or dBP ≥100 mmHg WITHOUT target organ damage or CV risk factors
- sBP ≥130 mmHg for high-risk populations (Framingham Risk >20%, age >50)
- sBP ≥130 mmHg or dBP ≥80 mmHg for patients with DM
Note: caution in elderly patients

Thresholds balance the benefits of BP reduction against risks of overtreatment (hypotension, falls, adverse drug effects) especially in the elderly. Lower thresholds for high-risk patients reflect the disproportionate CV benefit of treatment.

**Q:** What are the first-line antihypertensive drug classes for isolated systolic/diastolic HTN without other compelling indications?

**A:** Monotherapy: thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics (longer-acting preferred), β-blockers, ACEIs, ARBs, or long-acting CCBs.
Single pill combinations: ACEI + CCB, ARB + CCB, or ACEI/ARB + diuretic.
Note: β-blockers not recommended as monotherapy for age ≥60; ACEIs not recommended as monotherapy in Black patients.

β-blockers have less benefit for stroke prevention in older patients. ACEIs have reduced efficacy in Black patients likely due to lower renin activity — ARBs or CCBs are preferred. Combination therapy with a single pill improves adherence.

**Q:** What combination of antihypertensives should be AVOIDED?

**A:** 1. Non-DHP CCB (diltiazem/verapamil) + β-blocker: risk of bradycardia/heart block
2. ACEI + ARB combination: increased risk of hypotension, hyperkalemia, and renal failure without additive CV benefit
Monitor potassium and creatinine when combining ACEI/ARB with a potassium-sparing diuretic.

The ONTARGET trial demonstrated no additional benefit of ACEI+ARB dual blockade but increased harm. Non-DHP CCBs and β-blockers both slow AV conduction, risking severe bradyarrhythmias when combined.

**Q:** What are the preferred antihypertensive agents in each of the following specific comorbidities: (a) heart failure, (b) post-MI, (c) CAD, (d) DM with albuminuria, (e) cerebrovascular disease?

**A:** (a) Heart failure: ACEI (or ARB) + β-blockers + aldosterone antagonists; ARB/neprilysin inhibitor for HFrEF on guideline therapy
(b) Post-MI: β-blocker + ACEI (or ARB if ACEI intolerant)
(c) CAD: ACEI or ARB; β-blocker or CCB for stable angina; avoid short-acting nifedipine
(d) DM with albuminuria: ACEI or ARB (preferred over thiazide if adding DHP CCB)
(e) Cerebrovascular disease: ACEI + thiazide/thiazide-like diuretic combination

Compelling indications drive drug selection beyond BP control. ACE inhibitors reduce mortality in HF and post-MI. ACEI/ARB provide renoprotection in diabetic nephropathy. The PROGRESS trial established ACEI + thiazide combination for secondary stroke prevention.

**Q:** What antihypertensive medications are safe in pregnancy and lactation?

**A:** Pregnancy first-line (oral): Labetalol, Methyldopa, Long-acting oral nifedipine
Pregnancy second-line: Clonidine, Hydralazine, Thiazide diuretics, Other β-blockers (acebutolol, metoprolol, pindolol, propranolol)
AVOID in pregnancy: ACEIs and ARBs (fetotoxic to renal system)
Safe in lactation: Labetalol, Methyldopa, Long-acting nifedipine, Enalapril, Captopril

ACEIs and ARBs are absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy (Category D/X) due to fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, and neonatal renal failure. Labetalol and methyldopa have the longest safety record in obstetric hypertension.

**Q:** What are the follow-up intervals for hypertension management in Canadian primary care?

**A:** Lifestyle modification: follow-up every 3–6 months.
Pharmacological: follow-up every 1–2 months until BP under target for 2 consecutive visits, then every 3–6 months once at target.
Frequent follow-up: for symptomatic/severe HTN, drug intolerance, or target organ damage.
Referral: for refractory HTN, suspected secondary causes, or worsening renal failure.
Hospitalization: for malignant HTN.

Regular follow-up is essential to assess adherence to both lifestyle and pharmacotherapy, monitor for drug side effects, and ensure BP target is maintained. Refractory HTN (uncontrolled on ≥3 drugs including a diuretic) warrants workup for secondary causes.

**Q:** What is isolated systolic hypertension and what population is typically affected?

**A:** Isolated systolic HTN: sBP ≥140 mmHg AND dBP <90 mmHg. Associated with progressive reduction in vascular compliance (arterial stiffness). Usually begins in the 40s.

As arteries stiffen with age, the aorta can no longer effectively dampen the systolic pulse wave, causing elevated sBP with normal or even low dBP. It is the most common form of HTN in older adults and is a stronger predictor of cardiovascular risk in the elderly than diastolic HTN.

**Q:** What is renovascular hypertension and when should it be suspected?

**A:** Suspect renovascular HTN when ≥2 of the following are present:
- Sudden onset or worsening of HTN AND age >55 or <30
- Presence of abdominal bruit
- HTN resistant to 3 or more drugs
- Rise in creatinine ≥30% associated with use of ACEI or ARB
- Other atherosclerotic vascular disease (especially smokers or dyslipidemia)
- Recurrent pulmonary edema associated with hypertensive surges
Investigate: renal ultrasound, captopril renal scan (if GFR >60), MRA/CTA (if normal renal function).

Renovascular HTN from renal artery stenosis causes renin-dependent HTN. ACEI/ARB cause rise in creatinine by reducing glomerular filtration pressure in the stenotic kidney. Fibromuscular dysplasia is the cause in young women; atherosclerosis in older patients.

## Family Medicine Joint Pain

**Q:** What features in the history help distinguish inflammatory from non-inflammatory joint pain?

**A:** Inflammatory (WARM joints): Worse with rest, better with activity; swelling in the latter half of the night; Morning stiffness ≥30 min; Soft tissue swelling and erythema.
Non-inflammatory: Worse with activity, better with rest; morning stiffness <30 min ('gelling').

This distinction guides the diagnostic workup and treatment approach. Inflammatory arthritis warrants serology and DMARDs; non-inflammatory (e.g., OA) is managed with analgesia and mechanical interventions. Morning stiffness duration is a key differentiator.

**Q:** Which autoantibodies are associated with each of the following conditions: RA, SLE, Sjogren's, dermatomyositis/polymyositis, reactive arthritis?

**A:** RA: rheumatoid factor (RhF), anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide)
SLE: ANA (positive), anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm
Sjogren's: anti-SSA/Ro, anti-La, RhF
Dermatomyositis/Polymyositis: anti-Jo1 (anti-histidyl tRNA synthetase)
Reactive arthritis/Ankylosing spondylitis: HLA-B27
Note: negative ANA helps exclude SLE

Anti-CCP is more specific than RhF for RA. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are highly specific for SLE. HLA-B27 is more consistent with reactive arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis than RA. Anti-Jo1 suggests inflammatory myopathy with interstitial lung disease.

**Q:** What are the systemic features in joint pain history that suggest an extra-articular connective tissue disorder?

**A:** Fever (SLE, infection), rash (SLE, psoriatic arthritis), uveitis (psoriatic, reactive arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis), myalgias (fibromyalgia, myopathy), muscle weakness (polymyositis, neuropathy), GU symptoms (reactive arthritis, IBD).

A thorough ROS is essential in joint pain evaluation. Psoriatic skin and nail changes, eye inflammation, and bowel symptoms point to seronegative spondyloarthropathies. Constitutional symptoms (weight loss, fever) may indicate septic arthropathy or malignancy.

## Family Medicine Low Back Pain

**Q:** What is the definition of low back pain by duration, and what is the epidemiology?

**A:** Acute: <6 weeks; Subacute: 6–12 weeks; Chronic: >12 weeks.
Epidemiology: 5th most common reason for visiting a physician; lifetime prevalence 90%; peak age 45–60; largest WSIB category and most common cause of chronic disability for <45 yr; 90% resolve in 6 weeks; <5% become chronic.

Despite the extremely high lifetime prevalence, most episodes of acute LBP are self-limiting. The small proportion that progress to chronic LBP account for the majority of the socioeconomic burden, emphasizing the importance of early yellow flag identification.

**Q:** What is cauda equina syndrome and why is it a surgical emergency?

**A:** Cauda equina syndrome presents with: areflexia, lower extremity weakness, saddle anesthesia (perianal/perineal), fecal incontinence, and urinary retention.
It is a surgical EMERGENCY requiring immediate referral, as compression of the cauda equina nerve roots can lead to permanent bowel, bladder, and sexual dysfunction if not decompressed urgently.

Cauda equina syndrome typically results from large central disc herniation, epidural abscess, or tumour at L2–S1. Urinary retention is the most sensitive finding. Delay in surgical decompression beyond 24–48 hours significantly worsens neurological outcomes.

**Q:** What percentage of low back pain is mechanical and what are the mechanical causes?

**A:** 98% of low back pain is mechanical (worse with movement, better with rest).
Causes:
- Soft tissue: sprain (ligament), strain (muscle)
- Spine: facet joint/disc degeneration, disc herniation, spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, compression fracture
- Other: pregnancy
2% are non-mechanical (neoplastic, infectious, metabolic, rheumatologic, referred pain).

Mechanical LBP is benign and self-limiting in 90% of cases. Non-mechanical causes must be excluded with red flags; these include malignancy (primary or metastatic), infection (osteomyelitis, TB), metabolic bone disease, and referred visceral pain (AAA, pancreatitis, pyelonephritis).

**Q:** How is the straight leg raise (SLR) test performed and interpreted?

**A:** Patient is supine; examiner raises the leg while keeping the knee extended. Test is positive if pain radiates below the knee at <70° of hip flexion, aggravated by ankle dorsiflexion (Bragard's sign).
Positive = indicative of sciatica/lumbosacral nerve root irritation.
Crossed SLR: raising the uninvolved leg elicits pain in the leg with sciatica — more specific than ipsilateral SLR.

The SLR stretches the sciatic nerve and L4–S1 nerve roots. Sensitivity is ~80% but specificity is only ~40% for disc herniation. The crossed SLR has lower sensitivity but higher specificity (~90%), making it more diagnostic when positive.

**Q:** What are the 4 clinical patterns of non-traumatic low back pain and their likely pathology?

**A:** Pattern 1 (Back dominant, worse with flexion): Disc pain
Pattern 2 (Back dominant, worse with extension, never worse with flexion): Facet joint pain
Pattern 3 (Leg dominant, pain changes with back position): Compressed nerve pain / sciatica
Pattern 4 (Leg dominant, worse with activity, improves with rest): Symptomatic spinal stenosis / neurogenic claudication

The CORE Back Tool from the Centre for Effective Practice uses these patterns to guide initial management. Disc pain improves with extension exercises; facet joint pain with flexion; sciatica with prone extension; neurogenic claudication with sustained flexion and abdominal exercises.

**Q:** What are the red flags and yellow flags in low back pain management?

**A:** Red flags (require urgent/emergency action):
- Cauda equina syndrome (EMERGENCY)
- Severe worsening pain especially at night or when lying down (URGENT)
- Significant trauma (URGENT)
- Weight loss, history of cancer, fever (URGENT)
- Age >50, especially >65 (SOON)
- Widespread neurological signs (SOON)

Yellow flags (psychosocial barriers — predict chronicity):
- Belief that pain and activity are harmful
- Sickness behaviours (extended rest)
- Low mood, social withdrawal
- Poor job satisfaction
- Overprotective family or lack of support

Red flags prompt urgent imaging and specialist referral to rule out malignancy, infection, or structural emergency. Yellow flags identify patients at risk for chronic disability and guide psychosocial interventions (CBT, return-to-work programs). Referral urgency: EMERGENCY within hours, URGENT within 24–48 h, SOON within weeks.

**Q:** What does evidence show about spinal manipulative therapy (SMT) for acute low back pain?

**A:** Moderate-quality evidence from 15 RCTs: SMT produces statistically significant improvement in pain (mean -9.95/100 mm VAS) and function at 1–6 weeks. No serious adverse events reported. Minor transient adverse events (increased pain, muscle stiffness, headache) occur in 50–67% of patients. Effect size is modest and heterogeneity is large.

SMT (chiropractic manipulation or physiotherapy mobilization) is a reasonable adjunct for acute LBP. It performs similarly to other active treatments. The transient adverse events are common but resolve quickly, and no serious neurological complications were documented in the systematic review.

**Q:** What is the evidence for massage therapy in non-specific low back pain?

**A:** From a Cochrane systematic review of 13 RCTs: massage is superior for pain and function on both short- and long-term outcomes compared to no treatment; similar to exercise; superior to joint mobilization, relaxation therapy, physical therapy, acupuncture, and self-care education. Benefits last at least 1 year post-treatment. Acupuncture massage was better than classic (Swedish) massage.

Massage therapy is most beneficial for subacute and chronic non-specific LBP, especially in combination with exercise and education. It does not address underlying structural pathology but provides meaningful symptomatic benefit without the risks of pharmacotherapy.

**Q:** What is the femoral stretch test and what does it diagnose?

**A:** The femoral stretch test (also called femoral nerve stretch test): Patient is prone, knee is flexed, examiner extends the hip. A positive test produces anterior thigh pain and is used to diagnose L4 radiculopathy (femoral nerve).

The femoral nerve (L2, L3, L4) is stretched with hip extension. This complements the straight leg raise (which tests L4–S1/sciatic nerve). L4 radiculopathy presents with weakness of knee extension, reduced patellar reflex, and medial leg sensory loss.

**Q:** What are the neurological examination findings corresponding to L4, L5, and S1 nerve root involvement in low back pain?

**A:** L4: knee extension weakness, reduced/absent patellar reflex, medial leg/foot sensation
L5: great toe/ankle dorsiflexion weakness (foot drop), no reflex change, lateral leg/dorsal foot sensation
S1: plantar flexion/toe standing weakness, reduced/absent Achilles reflex, lateral foot/heel sensation

Special tests: SLR for L4–S1; femoral stretch test for L4.

Localizing the level of nerve root compression guides imaging decisions and surgical planning. L4–L5 and L5–S1 are the most common levels for disc herniation. A complete neurological exam (power, reflexes, sensation) is essential in all patients with radicular symptoms.

## Family Medicine Menopause

**Q:** What is the definition of menopause and when does it typically occur?

**A:** Menopause is defined as 12 months of amenorrhea following the final menstrual period. The mean age of menopause in Canada is 51.4 years.

Menopause is a retrospective diagnosis — the 12-month amenorrhea confirms that the final menstrual period has occurred. It results from ovarian follicular depletion and the consequent decline in estrogen and progesterone production.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of menopause and estrogen deprivation?

**A:** Urogenital: atrophy, vaginal dryness/itching, urinary frequency/urgency/incontinence, bleeding.
Vascular: vasomotor instability (hot flashes), increased risk of heart disease.
Bones: bone loss, joint/muscle/back pain, fractures, loss of height.
Brain: depression, irritability, mood swings, memory loss.

Estrogen has widespread protective effects. Vasomotor symptoms affect up to 75% of menopausal women. Bone loss accelerates at menopause (up to 5% per year initially) due to loss of estrogen's anti-resorptive effect, increasing fracture risk significantly.

**Q:** What are the benefits and risks of hormone therapy (HT) for menopause?

**A:** Benefits: decreases risk of osteoporotic fractures and colorectal cancer.
Risks: increases risk of DVT and PE. If initiated >60 years OR >10 years post-menopause: can increase risk of breast cancer and stroke.
HT should NOT be initiated >10 years post-menopause.
Regimens: cyclic or continuous estrogen-progestin; estrogen-only patch/gel/cream/ring/vaginal tablet if no uterus.

The timing hypothesis states HT is most beneficial when started close to menopause onset (the 'window of opportunity'). Late initiation (>60 yr or >10 yr post-menopause) is associated with increased cardiovascular and breast cancer risk, consistent with WHI trial findings.

**Q:** What non-hormonal pharmacological options are available for menopausal vasomotor symptoms?

**A:** Venlafaxine, SSRIs, or gabapentin can be used to ease vasomotor instability (hot flashes).

These agents are particularly useful when HT is contraindicated (e.g., hormone-sensitive breast cancer, prior DVT/PE, uncontrolled CV disease). Venlafaxine and paroxetine have the best evidence for hot flash reduction among non-hormonal options. Gabapentin is especially useful for nocturnal hot flashes.

**Q:** What are the calcium and vitamin D supplementation recommendations for menopausal women?

**A:** Calcium: 1200 mg/day (adequate intake/supplementation)
Vitamin D: 1000 IU/day

These are non-pharmacological cornerstones of osteoporosis prevention in menopausal women. Adequate calcium and vitamin D are necessary for bone mineralization; estrogen deficiency accelerates bone resorption, so ensuring nutritional adequacy is foundational before considering bisphosphonate therapy.

## Family Medicine Osteoarthritis

**Q:** What are the hallmark clinical and radiographic features of osteoarthritis?

**A:** Clinical: joint pain with activity (improved with rest), morning stiffness/gelling <30 min, deformity, bony enlargement, crepitus, limited range of motion, periarticular muscle atrophy.
Common sites: DIP, PIP, 1st CMC joints of hand; hip; knee; 1st MTP; L4-L5/L5-S1; C-spine.
Radiographic (hallmark): joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts, osteophytes.
Note: no laboratory tests diagnose OA.

Almost everyone >65 years shows OA changes on X-ray, but only 33% are symptomatic — radiographic severity does not correlate well with symptoms. The absence of inflammation distinguishes OA from RA (no elevated ESR/CRP, no systemic features).

**Q:** What is the stepwise pharmacological management of osteoarthritis?

**A:** 1st line: Acetaminophen up to 4 g/day (OA is NOT an inflammatory disorder)
2nd line: NSAIDs (COX-2 selective preferred) in low doses for short durations
3rd line: Duloxetine, combination analgesics (acetaminophen + codeine)
Adjuncts: Intra-articular corticosteroid or hyaluronic acid injections; topical NSAIDs (diclofenac); capsaicin cream; oral glucosamine (inconclusive evidence)
Note: medications do NOT alter the natural course of OA.

Acetaminophen is preferred first-line due to its safety profile. COX-2 selective NSAIDs reduce GI risk compared to non-selective NSAIDs but carry cardiovascular risk. Surgery (total joint arthroplasty) is reserved for persistent pain and functional impairment despite optimal pharmacotherapy.

**Q:** What is the epidemiology of osteoarthritis and who is most affected?

**A:** Most common form of arthritis in primary care. Prevalence: 10–12%, increases with age. Results in long-term disability in 2–3% of patients with OA. Almost everyone >65 years shows OA signs on X-ray, but only 33% of these are symptomatic.

OA is primarily a disease of aging and mechanical wear, but genetic, metabolic, and biomechanical factors also contribute. Risk factors include obesity (knee OA), joint injury, repetitive occupational joint use, and female sex (post-menopause).
