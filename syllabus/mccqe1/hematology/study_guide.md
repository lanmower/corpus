# Hematology

## Anemia & Hemoglobin Disorders

**Q:** What is the MCV cut-off that distinguishes microcytic from normocytic anemia?

**A:** MCV <80 fL = microcytic; 80–100 fL = normocytic; >100 fL = macrocytic

MCV-based classification narrows the differential: microcytic (iron deficiency, thalassemia, sideroblastic, ACD in chronic inflammation); normocytic (acute blood loss, hemolysis, ACD, aplastic anemia); macrocytic (B12/folate deficiency, hypothyroidism, alcohol, medications).

**Q:** A 30-year-old vegetarian woman has Hb 88 g/L, MCV 72 fL, low serum ferritin (8 µg/L), and low transferrin saturation. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?

**A:** Iron deficiency anemia; treat with oral ferrous sulfate 325 mg (elemental iron ~65 mg) TID between meals

IDA is the most common nutritional anemia globally. Ferritin <15 µg/L confirms iron deficiency. Oral iron is first-line; IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose) for intolerance or malabsorption. Treat underlying cause (menorrhagia, GI blood loss).

**Q:** What distinguishes thalassemia trait from iron deficiency anemia on CBC?

**A:** Thalassemia trait: low MCV with normal/high RBC count and normal ferritin; iron deficiency: low MCV with low ferritin and low RBC

Mentzer index (<13 suggests thalassemia, >13 suggests IDA). Thalassemia trait is confirmed by Hb electrophoresis (elevated HbA2 >3.5% in beta-thalassemia trait). Iron supplementation does not help thalassemia trait.

**Q:** A 65-year-old woman has Hb 92 g/L, MCV 110 fL, hypersegmented neutrophils, and low serum B12. What is the most likely underlying cause?

**A:** B12 deficiency from pernicious anemia or dietary insufficiency (veganism)

Macrocytic megaloblastic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils (>5 lobes, or any 6-lobed PMN) is characteristic of B12 or folate deficiency. Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies confirm pernicious anemia. Treat with IM B12 (cyanocobalamin) or high-dose oral B12.

**Q:** What is anemia of chronic disease (ACD) and which test best distinguishes it from IDA?

**A:** ACD is caused by inflammatory cytokines reducing EPO and iron utilization; ferritin is normal or elevated in ACD (vs. low in IDA)

In ACD, hepcidin (acute-phase reactant) sequesters iron in macrophages. Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant — elevated in inflammation even with depleted stores. Serum transferrin receptor (sTfR) is elevated in IDA but normal in ACD. sTfR/log ferritin ratio distinguishes them.

**Q:** A 45-year-old man with sickle cell disease presents with sudden-onset pallor, Hb drops from 85 to 55 g/L, reticulocyte count near zero, and fever. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Aplastic crisis due to Parvovirus B19 infection

Parvovirus B19 infects erythroid precursors, halting RBC production. In patients with hemolytic anemias (SCD, thalassemia, spherocytosis), rapid Hb drop occurs. Reticulocytopenia is key. Treatment: supportive (transfusion); parvovirus infection is self-limited.

**Q:** What is the direct antiglobulin test (DAT / Coombs test) used to detect?

**A:** IgG or complement (C3d) coating the surface of red blood cells, indicating immune-mediated hemolysis

Positive DAT indicates warm (IgG) or cold (IgM/complement) autoimmune hemolytic anemia, drug-induced hemolysis, or hemolytic transfusion reaction. Warm AIHA: treat with steroids ± rituximab. Cold agglutinin disease: avoid cold, treat underlying cause.

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman on methyldopa develops anemia with positive DAT, elevated indirect bilirubin, and low haptoglobin. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia (DIIHA) due to methyldopa

Methyldopa (and other drugs: penicillin, cephalosporins, NSAIDs, quinine) causes DIIHA via drug-hapten, immune complex, or autoantibody mechanisms. Discontinue offending drug; hemolysis usually resolves in days to weeks.

**Q:** What is the hallmark peripheral blood finding of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)?

**A:** Schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) on blood film

MAHA occurs in TTP, HUS, DIC, and malignant hypertension. Schistocytes result from mechanical shearing of RBCs at fibrin strands. Combined with thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, and elevated indirect bilirubin.

**Q:** What is hereditary spherocytosis and what is its most common complication?

**A:** Autosomal dominant defect in RBC membrane proteins (ankyrin, spectrin, band 3) causing spherical, osmotically fragile RBCs; most common complication is pigment gallstones (bilirubin cholelithiasis)

HS presents with hemolytic anemia, splenomegaly, and jaundice. Osmotic fragility test and EMA binding test confirm diagnosis. Splenectomy reduces hemolysis but increases infection risk (pneumococcal vaccines mandatory).

**Q:** What is the treatment for aplastic anemia in a 25-year-old with a compatible sibling donor?

**A:** Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) from matched sibling — curative

Severe aplastic anemia (SAA): pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow. Young patients with matched sibling should proceed to HSCT. Older patients or no donor: immunosuppression with anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) + cyclosporine + eltrombopag.

**Q:** What is the threshold for red blood cell transfusion in a hemodynamically stable hospitalized adult without cardiac disease?

**A:** Hb <70 g/L (restrictive transfusion strategy)

TRICC trial and Canadian Blood Services guidelines support restrictive transfusion (Hb <70 g/L) in stable patients. Liberal transfusion (Hb <100 g/L) threshold is used in acute MI, ACS, and post-cardiac surgery. Each unit raises Hb by ~10 g/L.

**Q:** A 70-year-old man with CKD stage 4 has Hb 88 g/L, reticulocyte count 0.5%, and ferritin 450 µg/L. What is the likely cause of his anemia?

**A:** Renal anemia (anemia of CKD) due to decreased erythropoietin production

EPO deficiency in CKD causes normocytic, normochromic anemia with low reticulocyte count. Iron studies: ferritin often elevated due to inflammation. IV iron may be needed if TSAT <20%. If iron replete, ESA (darbepoetin, epoetin alfa) is indicated when Hb <100 g/L.

**Q:** What is the normal range for hemoglobin and what defines anemia in adult females?

**A:** Normal Hb in adult women: 120–160 g/L; anemia defined as Hb <120 g/L (WHO) or <115 g/L (some guidelines)

WHO defines anemia as Hb <120 g/L in non-pregnant women, <130 g/L in men, <110 g/L in pregnant women. Canadian Blood Services uses similar thresholds. Severity: mild 100–119 g/L, moderate 70–99 g/L, severe <70 g/L.

**Q:** A 35-year-old woman from Southeast Asia has Hb 98 g/L, MCV 64 fL, normal ferritin, and HbA2 5.8% on electrophoresis. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Beta-thalassemia trait (carrier state)

HbA2 >3.5% confirms beta-thalassemia trait. These patients have mild microcytic anemia and should not receive unnecessary iron supplementation. Genetic counseling is important if partner is also a carrier (risk of beta-thalassemia major in offspring).

**Q:** How do you interpret iron studies for different anemias?

**A:** Iron deficiency: low ferritin, low iron, high TIBC, low transferrin sat. Anemia of chronic disease: low/normal iron, high ferritin, low TIBC, low transferrin sat

Ferritin is acute phase reactant — may be normal/elevated in ACD even with concurrent iron deficiency. Soluble transferrin receptor elevated in true iron deficiency. Reticulocyte count helps assess marrow response.

**Q:** How is anemia classified by MCV and what are common causes?

**A:** Microcytic (MCV<80): iron deficiency, thalassemia, chronic disease, sideroblastic. Normocytic (80-100): chronic disease, renal, acute blood loss, hemolysis, bone marrow failure. Macrocytic (>100): B12/folate, liver disease, hypothyroid, MDS, drugs

First step: check MCV. Reticulocyte count distinguishes production (low retic) from destruction/loss (high retic). Iron deficiency is most common cause worldwide. Always investigate cause - never just treat the number.

**Q:** How are iron studies interpreted in iron deficiency vs anemia of chronic disease?

**A:** IDA: low ferritin (<30), low iron, high TIBC, low transferrin sat (<20%). ACD: normal/high ferritin (acute phase reactant), low iron, low TIBC, low transferrin sat. Combined: low ferritin settles it

Ferritin <30 is diagnostic of IDA (most useful single test). Ferritin 30-100 is indeterminate (may have coexistent IDA+ACD). Ferritin >100 essentially excludes IDA. Soluble transferrin receptor elevated in IDA, normal in ACD.

**Q:** What is the workup and management of iron deficiency anemia?

**A:** Workup: identify cause (menstrual loss, GI loss - scope if male or postmenopausal). Treatment: oral ferrous sulfate 300mg daily-TID (elemental iron 60-200mg/d) on empty stomach. IV iron if intolerant/malabsorption/severe

Take oral iron with vitamin C, avoid with tea/coffee/calcium/antacids. GI side effects common - try alternate-day dosing (better absorbed anyway per recent evidence). Check reticulocyte response at 1wk, Hb at 4-6wk. Continue 3-6mo to replete stores.

**Q:** How is B12 deficiency diagnosed and treated?

**A:** Diagnosis: low serum B12 (<148 pmol/L), elevated methylmalonic acid (more specific), elevated homocysteine. Causes: pernicious anemia, vegan diet, metformin, malabsorption. Treat: IM B12 1000mcg daily x7, then weekly x4, then monthly

Neurological symptoms (subacute combined degeneration: dorsal columns + corticospinal tracts) may be irreversible if treatment delayed. Check folate simultaneously. Do NOT give folate alone if B12 deficient (worsens neuro). Oral B12 1000-2000mcg daily is acceptable if adherent.

**Q:** What is the approach to hemolytic anemia?

**A:** Suspect if: normocytic anemia + high reticulocytes + high LDH + high indirect bilirubin + low haptoglobin. DAT (Coombs) positive: autoimmune. DAT negative: consider mechanical, intrinsic RBC defects

Intravascular: hemoglobinuria, very low haptoglobin (TTP/HUS, PNH, mechanical valves). Extravascular: splenomegaly (autoimmune, spherocytosis). Peripheral smear: schistocytes (MAHA), spherocytes (AIHA/HS), sickle cells, target cells (thalassemia).

**Q:** What are the causes and management of autoimmune hemolytic anemia?

**A:** Warm AIHA (IgG, 70%): idiopathic, lymphoma, SLE, drugs. Treat: prednisone 1mg/kg, rituximab, splenectomy. Cold agglutinin (IgM): infections (mycoplasma, EBV), lymphoma. Treat: avoid cold, rituximab

DAT positive confirms autoimmune etiology. Warm AIHA: do not transfuse unless life-threatening (crossmatch difficult). Cold AIHA: warm blood products through warmer. Corticosteroids less effective in cold AIHA. Always investigate for underlying lymphoma or SLE.

**Q:** What is thalassemia and how does it differ from iron deficiency?

**A:** Both microcytic. Thalassemia: normal/high ferritin, target cells, elevated RBC count (elevated RDW in IDA), Mentzer index <13 (MCV/RBC). Alpha-thal: Hb electrophoresis may be normal. Beta-thal minor: elevated HbA2 >3.5%

Thalassemia trait: mild anemia, NO iron deficiency. Do NOT give iron (risk of overload). Beta-thal major: transfusion-dependent, chelation needed. Beta-thal intermedia: variable. HbH disease (3 alpha deletions): moderate anemia, Hb Bart in neonates.

**Q:** What is the approach to pancytopenia?

**A:** DDx: aplastic anemia, MDS, leukemia (marrow infiltration), megaloblastic anemia, hypersplenism, infections (HIV, TB), medications. Workup: CBC with smear, reticulocytes, B12/folate, HIV, bone marrow biopsy

Aplastic anemia: hypocellular marrow. MDS: dysplastic marrow. Leukemia: blast infiltration. Megaloblastic: hypersegmented neutrophils, oval macrocytes. Drug causes: MTX, phenytoin, chemotherapy. Refer hematology urgently for suspected marrow failure.

**Q:** What is thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and how is it managed?

**A:** Pentad: MAHA (schistocytes), thrombocytopenia, renal dysfunction, neuro symptoms, fever. ADAMTS13 <10% diagnostic. Treatment: EMERGENT plasma exchange + steroids + caplacizumab. Do NOT transfuse platelets

TTP is a hematologic emergency (untreated mortality >90%). PLASMIC score predicts ADAMTS13 deficiency. Plasma exchange removes autoantibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13. Platelet transfusion WORSENS thrombosis. Caplacizumab (anti-vWF) reduces time to remission.

**Q:** How is sickle cell disease managed in Canada?

**A:** Hydroxyurea (first-line disease-modifying): increases HbF. Voxelotor (Hb S polymerization inhibitor). Crizanlizumab (anti-P-selectin). Transfusion for severe anemia/stroke prevention. Curative: bone marrow transplant

Acute crises: pain (IV fluids, opioids, NSAIDs), chest (antibiotics, transfusion, O2), stroke (exchange transfusion), splenic sequestration (transfusion). Penicillin prophylaxis until age 5. Pneumococcal vaccine. Newborn screening in all Canadian provinces.

**Q:** When should anemia be investigated vs monitored in primary care?

**A:** Investigate immediately: symptomatic, Hb <100, rapid decline, unexpected. Refer hematology: unexplained cytopenias, suspected marrow failure, hemolysis, suspected malignancy. Transfuse: Hb <70 (restrictive strategy)

TRICC trial: restrictive transfusion (Hb<70) equivalent or superior to liberal (Hb<100) in most patients. Exception: ACS threshold Hb<80. Each unit raises Hb ~10g/L. Type and screen before transfusion. Monitor for transfusion reactions.

**Q:** What peripheral blood smear findings are diagnostic?

**A:** Schistocytes: MAHA (TTP/HUS/DIC). Spherocytes: AIHA, hereditary spherocytosis. Target cells: thalassemia, liver disease. Teardrop: myelofibrosis. Rouleaux: myeloma. Hypersegmented neutrophils: megaloblastic

Smear is often the most informative single test in anemia workup. Also look for: blasts (leukemia), Howell-Jolly bodies (asplenia), basophilic stippling (lead, thalassemia), bite cells (G6PD). Always correlate with clinical context.

**Q:** What are the causes and workup of macrocytic anemia?

**A:** Megaloblastic: B12/folate deficiency (hypersegmented neutrophils, oval macrocytes). Non-megaloblastic: liver disease, hypothyroid, MDS, reticulocytosis, drugs (MTX, hydroxyurea, AZT), alcohol

Check B12, folate, TSH, LFTs, reticulocyte count. If B12/folate normal and no obvious cause: bone marrow biopsy to evaluate for MDS. Alcohol causes macrocytosis even without anemia. MCV >115 strongly suggests megaloblastic or MDS.

**Q:** What is G6PD deficiency and how does it present?

**A:** X-linked recessive. Episodic hemolysis triggered by oxidative stress: infections, fava beans, drugs (dapsone, primaquine, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin). Smear: bite cells, Heinz bodies

G6PD level may be NORMAL during acute hemolysis (young RBCs have higher G6PD). Retest 2-3 months after episode. Self-limited episode. Avoid known triggers. Common in Mediterranean, African, Asian descent.

**Q:** What is the approach to an elevated ferritin?

**A:** DDx: iron overload (hereditary hemochromatosis, transfusional), inflammation/infection, liver disease, malignancy, metabolic syndrome, hyperthyroidism. Check transferrin saturation: if >45% suspect hemochromatosis (HFE gene testing)

Ferritin is an acute phase reactant - elevated in inflammation. Hemochromatosis: autosomal recessive C282Y HFE mutation. Screen first-degree relatives. Treatment: phlebotomy targeting ferritin <50. Complications: cirrhosis, DM, cardiomyopathy, arthropathy, bronze skin.

**Q:** What CBC findings suggest specific diagnoses?

**A:** Microcytic anemia: iron deficiency, thalassemia, chronic disease. Macrocytic: B12/folate, alcohol, hypothyroid, MDS. Pancytopenia: aplastic anemia, leukemia, B12 deficiency. Eosinophilia: parasites, allergy, drug reaction

Peripheral smear findings: schistocytes(DIC/TTP), target cells(thalassemia/liver disease), spherocytes(hereditary spherocytosis/autoimmune), Howell-Jolly bodies(asplenia).

**Q:** What are the factors that decrease myocardial oxygen supply?

**A:** Decreased luminal diameter (atherosclerosis, vasospasm), decreased duration of diastole (tachycardia), decreased hemoglobin (anemia), decreased SaO2 (hypoxemia), and congenital anomalies

Myocardial oxygen supply depends on coronary blood flow (which occurs mainly in diastole), oxygen-carrying capacity (hemoglobin), and oxygen saturation.

**Q:** What are less emergent causes of dyspnea?

**A:** Deconditioning, anxiety, respiratory splinting due to unrelated pain, NYD (not yet diagnosed), toxin ingestion, anemia, hemoglobinopathy, CNS lesion, primary muscle weakness

These causes should be considered after ruling out life-threatening conditions

**Q:** In which conditions is HbA1C misleading for diabetes diagnosis?

**A:** Hemoglobinopathies, iron deficiency, hemolytic anemia, severe hepatic or renal disease

**Q:** List 4 disadvantages/limitations of HbA1C

**A:** Cost, misleading in hemoglobinopathies, iron deficiency, hemolytic anemia, severe hepatic or renal disease, altered by ethnicity and aging, not for diagnostic use in children/adolescents, pregnant women, those with CF, or suspected T1DM

HbA1C can be inaccurate in conditions that affect red blood cell turnover or hemoglobin structure.

**A:** Cost, misleading in hemoglobinopathies, iron deficiency, hemolytic anemia, severe hepatic/renal disease, altered by ethnicity and aging, requires standardized validated assay, not for diagnostic use in children/adolescents, pregnant women, CF, or suspected T1DM

HbA1C has limitations in certain populations and clinical conditions.

HbA1C has limitations in certain populations and medical conditions where hemoglobin or red blood cell turnover is affected.

HbA1c can be unreliable in various medical conditions and certain populations, requiring a standardized validated assay.

**Q:** What is the clinical triad of hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS)?

**A:** Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury

HUS is characterized by the triad of hemolytic anemia, low platelets, and renal impairment, often following EHEC infection.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of STEC-related HUS?

**A:** Shiga toxin damages endothelial cells, causing microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury

Shiga toxin from EHEC/STEC binds to endothelial cells in kidneys, causing endothelial damage that leads to the triad of HUS.

**Q:** What are causes of secondary hemochromatosis?

**A:** Parenteral iron overload (transfusions, hemodialysis, IV iron), excessive oral iron intake, other liver diseases (EtOH, NAFLD, viral hepatitis), iron-loading anemias (hemolytic, sideroblastic, aplastic, thalassemia major)

Secondary hemochromatosis results from various conditions causing iron overload, often requiring chelation therapy rather than phlebotomy.

**Q:** What are the causes of secondary hemochromatosis?

**A:** Parenteral iron overload (transfusions, hemodialysis, IV iron), excessive oral iron intake, other liver diseases (EtOH, NAFLD, viral hepatitis), iron-loading anemias (thalassemia, sideroblastic), ineffective erythropoiesis

Secondary hemochromatosis results from conditions that either add iron externally or cause ineffective erythropoiesis with increased intestinal absorption.

**Q:** List the causes of secondary hemochromatosis

**A:** Parenteral iron overload (transfusions, hemodialysis, parenteral iron), excessive oral iron intake, other liver diseases (EtOH, NAFLD, viral hepatitis), iron-loading anemias (hemolytic, sideroblastic, aplastic, thalassemia major), ineffective erythropoiesis

Secondary hemochromatosis results from conditions causing iron overload through repeated blood transfusions, excessive intake, or ineffective erythropoiesis driving increased iron absorption.

**Q:** What is the hallmark finding of sideroblastic anemia on bone marrow examination?

**A:** Ring sideroblasts — iron deposits in mitochondria forming large, abnormal granules that encircle the nucleus of erythroid precursors.

In sideroblastic anemia, defects in heme biosynthesis cause iron to accumulate in mitochondria (which surround the nucleus), creating the ring sideroblast. This is the diagnostic hallmark distinguishing it from other causes of microcytic anemia.

**Q:** What are the reversible causes of sideroblastic anemia?

**A:** Drugs (isoniazid, chloramphenicol), alcohol, lead poisoning, copper deficiency, zinc toxicity, and hypothyroidism.

Reversible sideroblastic anemia results from acquired toxins or metabolic disturbances that disrupt heme biosynthesis. Identifying and removing the precipitating cause leads to resolution. Isoniazid is a classic MCCQE1 cause, as it inhibits pyridoxine (B6), a cofactor in heme synthesis.

**Q:** What is the treatment for X-linked hereditary sideroblastic anemia?

**A:** High-dose pyridoxine (vitamin B6) in some cases.

X-linked sideroblastic anemia is caused by mutations in ALAS2, the pyridoxine-dependent enzyme initiating heme synthesis. High-dose B6 supplementation can partially correct the enzyme deficiency in pyridoxine-responsive patients. Median survival is approximately 10 years.

**Q:** In lead poisoning, at what blood lead level do symptoms typically appear, and what is the diagnostic threshold?

**A:** Symptoms may appear at ≥50 µg/dL; diagnostic threshold is blood lead level >80 µg/dL.

Lead poisoning should be considered in any child with microcytic anemia living in a house built before 1977. Lead inhibits heme biosynthesis at multiple steps, causing sideroblastic (microcytic) anemia with basophilic stippling. The mnemonic LEAD describes key features: Lead lines, Encephalopathy, Abdominal colic/Anemia, Drops (wrist/foot drop).

**Q:** What defines aplastic anemia and what are the key bone marrow findings?

**A:** Destruction of hematopoietic cells leading to pancytopenia and a hypocellular bone marrow (<25% cellularity) with adipose tissue replacement and no evidence of malignant infiltration or fibrosis.

Aplastic anemia is a BM failure syndrome where pancytopenia results from loss of hematopoietic stem cells. The hypocellular marrow is the cornerstone diagnostic finding. Idiopathic aplastic anemia is commonly T-cell mediated. PNH is found in ~50% of aplastic anemia patients and must be excluded.

**Q:** What are the causes of aplastic anemia?

**A:** Congenital (Fanconi syndrome, Shwachman-Diamond, dyskeratosis congenita); acquired: idiopathic (T-cell mediated), ionizing radiation, drugs (dose-related: chemotherapy; idiosyncratic: chloramphenicol, antimalarials, phenylbutazone), toxins (benzene, DDT), viral infections (parvovirus B19, EBV, HBV, HHV6, HIV), autoimmune (SLE, GVHD), PNH, pregnancy, anorexia nervosa, thymoma.

Aplastic anemia has many causes. Idiosyncratic drug reactions (especially chloramphenicol) are classic exam causes. Parvovirus B19 targets erythroid precursors but also causes aplastic crises in patients with chronic hemolysis. Identifying the cause guides management.

**Q:** What is the treatment for idiopathic aplastic anemia?

**A:** Immunosuppressive therapy (horse or rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin + cyclosporine) for non-transplant candidates; allogeneic bone marrow transplant is potentially curative. Eltrombopag (TPO receptor agonist) is also effective. G-CSF and EPO are NOT effective.

Since idiopathic aplastic anemia is T-cell mediated, immunosuppression with ATG + cyclosporine achieves 40-50% response. Allogeneic BMT is preferred in young patients with matched donors. Eltrombopag stimulates residual stem cells. Note that EPO and G-CSF are ineffective in aplastic anemia.

**Q:** What investigations differentiate intravascular from extravascular hemolysis?

**A:** Intravascular: schistocytes on blood film, free Hb in serum, hemoglobinuria, hemosiderinuria (most sensitive delayed marker). Extravascular: positive direct Coombs (DAT) detecting IgG or complement on RBCs, spherocytes on film. Both share: elevated LDH, decreased haptoglobin, elevated unconjugated bilirubin, reticulocytosis.

Haptoglobin mops up free Hb; it decreases in both types but more profoundly in intravascular hemolysis. Hemosiderinuria is the most sensitive delayed marker of intravascular hemolysis. The DAT is key for extravascular (immune) hemolysis. Schistocytes indicate mechanical RBC destruction (MAHA) — an intravascular process.

**Q:** What does the direct antiglobulin test (DAT/direct Coombs) detect and when is it indicated?

**A:** Detects IgG or complement bound to the surface of patient's RBCs. Indicated in: hemolytic disease of the newborn, autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), and hemolytic transfusion reactions.

The DAT adds anti-IgG or anti-complement antibody to the patient's washed RBCs; agglutination = positive. This identifies antibody-coated RBCs undergoing extravascular hemolysis. The indirect Coombs (IAT) detects antibodies in the patient's serum and is used for cross-matching and screening during pregnancy.

**Q:** What Hb electrophoresis pattern is diagnostic of β-thalassemia minor?

**A:** Elevated HbA2 (3.5–5%; normal 1.5–3.5%). About 50% also have a slight increase in HbF.

β-thalassemia minor (trait) results from a single defective β-globin allele. Despite microcytosis (MCV <70 fL), anemia is mild (Hb 100–140 g/L). The compensatory increase in δ-chain synthesis raises HbA2, making it the specific diagnostic marker. No treatment is required; genetic counselling is essential.

**Q:** What are the key features and treatment of β-thalassemia major?

**A:** Severe microcytic anemia (Hb <60 g/L) appearing at 6–12 months; HbF 90–100%, HbA 0–10%. Features: jaundice, gross hepatosplenomegaly, iron overload (hemochromatosis), stunted growth, 'hair-on-end' skull x-ray. Treatment: lifelong RBC transfusions, iron chelation (deferoxamine, deferasirox, deferiprone), folic acid, allogeneic BMT (curative).

β-thalassemia major (Cooley's anemia) is autosomal recessive — both β-globin alleles are defective. HbF persists as compensation. Iron overload from transfusions and ineffective erythropoiesis causes organ damage (ABCDI: Arrhythmia/cardiomyopathy, Bronze skin, Cirrhosis, Diabetes, Hypogonadism). Gene therapy (CRISPR-Cas9) is an emerging curative option.

**Q:** How does α-thalassemia severity relate to the number of defective α-globin genes?

**A:** 1 gene (aa/a-): silent carrier, normal Hb, normal MCV. 2 genes (aa/-- or a-/a-): normal Hb, decreased MCV. 3 genes (a-/--): HbH (β4) disease, decreased Hb, decreased MCV, splenomegaly. 4 genes (--/--): Hb Barts (γ4)/hydrops fetalis, usually incompatible with life.

α-thalassemia results from deletions of α-globin genes (normally 4 copies). Cis 2-gene deletion (--/αα) is more common in Southeast Asia and increases risk of hydrops fetalis in offspring; trans deletion (α-/α-) is more common in Africa, with lower fetal risk. HbH disease is similar to β-thalassemia intermedia in management.

**Q:** What is the molecular basis of sickle cell disease and which populations are most affected?

**A:** Autosomal recessive Glu→Val substitution at position 6 of the β-globin chain (chromosome 11), producing HbS instead of HbA. Most common in Sub-Saharan African, Indian, Middle Eastern, and Mediterranean populations. HbS allele is thought to confer protection against malaria.

SCD occurs when an individual is homozygous (HbSS) or compound heterozygous (HbS-β-thal, HbSC). At low pO2, deoxyHbS polymerizes into rigid rods that distort RBCs into the sickle shape, causing hemolysis and vaso-occlusion. Sickling is worsened by acidemia, increased CO2, 2,3-DPG, fever, and hyperosmolality.

**Q:** What are the four types of acute crises in sickle cell disease (HbSS)?

**A:** 1. Aplastic crisis — BM suppression (especially parvovirus B19) causing Hb drop. 2. Splenic sequestration crisis — acute Hb drop from massive splenic pooling (mainly in children). 3. Vaso-occlusive crisis — microcirculation infarction causing ischemia-reperfusion injury, pain, fever, leukocytosis. 4. Acute chest syndrome — fever/respiratory symptoms + new pulmonary infiltrate (precipitated by infection, fat emboli, pulmonary infarction).

Vaso-occlusive crises are the most common and are precipitated by infections, dehydration, temperature change, pregnancy, menses, and alcohol. Acute chest syndrome is a medical emergency with high mortality. Splenic sequestration occurs mainly in young children as adults develop functional asplenia from repeated infarctions.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease and what are its major short-term adverse effects?

**A:** Mechanism: inhibits repression of the fetal Hb γ-gene, increasing HbF production; HbF reduces polymerization and precipitation of HbS. Short-term adverse effects: dose-related leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, decreased reticulocyte count; decreased sperm production, mucositis, skin ulcers.

Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) is the cornerstone disease-modifying therapy for SCD. Increased HbF dilutes HbS and impairs sickling. RCT evidence (8 trials, 899 patients) shows significant reductions in pain crises, hospitalizations, acute chest syndrome, and need for transfusions. Long-term concerns include potential teratogenicity and cancer risk.

**Q:** What vaccinations and prophylaxis are recommended in sickle cell disease to prevent infection?

**A:** Vaccinations: S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae type b (in childhood). Prophylactic penicillin from age 3 months to 5 years.

Functional asplenia (from repeated infarction) in SCD markedly increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms: S. pneumoniae (most common), H. influenzae, N. meningitidis, and Salmonella (osteomyelitis). Penicillin prophylaxis and vaccination are standard of care per Canadian guidelines.

**Q:** What are the indications for exchange transfusion in sickle cell disease?

**A:** SCD complications: acute chest syndrome, aplastic crises, hepatic or splenic sequestration, stroke; prevention of complications; and preoperative preparation.

Exchange transfusion rapidly reduces HbS percentage and replaces sickled cells with normal RBCs, avoiding the iron overload of simple transfusion. It is preferred over simple transfusion for serious acute complications like stroke and acute chest syndrome. The SWiTCH trial confirmed transfusions + chelation remain preferred for secondary stroke prevention in children.

**Q:** How does warm AIHA differ from cold AIHA in antibody type, Coombs result, and first-line management?

**A:** Warm (75-90%): IgG antibody, agglutination at 37°C, DAT positive for IgG ± complement; first-line = corticosteroids. Cold: IgM antibody, agglutination at 4°C, DAT positive for complement only; first-line = avoid cold + rituximab-based regimen.

Warm AIHA is often primary or secondary to autoimmune disease (SLE), lymphoproliferative disorders, or drugs (penicillin, methyldopa). Cold agglutinin disease is associated with Mycoplasma pneumoniae, EBV, and CLL. Blood film shows spherocytes in warm AIHA and RBC agglutination clumps in cold AIHA.

**Q:** What conditions cause microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)/TMA, and what is the key blood film finding?

**A:** Schistocytes on blood film (mechanical RBC fragmentation). Causes: TTP, HUS, aHUS, DIC, eclampsia/HELLP, malignant hypertension, vasculitis, malfunctioning heart valves, metastatic carcinoma, drugs (calcineurin inhibitors, quinine, simvastatin), catastrophic APS, severe infections (CMV, meningococcus).

MAHA results from fibrin or platelet thrombi in small vessels shearing RBCs into schistocytes. Unlike immune hemolysis, the DAT (Coombs) is negative in MAHA. Recognizing the TMA pentad (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + renal failure + fever + neurological changes) should prompt urgent investigation for TTP.

**Q:** What is the genetic basis of hereditary spherocytosis and what are its key diagnostic features?

**A:** Autosomal dominant (variable penetrance) abnormality in RBC membrane proteins (e.g. spectrin), causing spherocytes with elevated MCHC, increased osmotic fragility, splenomegaly, jaundice, and pigmented gallstones.

Hereditary spherocytosis is the most common hereditary hemolytic anemia. Spectrin deficiency leads to loss of RBC membrane lipids, creating rigid spheres with decreased deformability that are destroyed in the spleen. Splenectomy is effective in severe cases but deferred in early childhood; pre-splenectomy vaccination against pneumococcus, meningococcus, and H. influenzae is mandatory.

**Q:** What triggers hemolysis in G6PD deficiency and what are the key blood film findings?

**A:** Triggers: oxidative stress, drugs (sulfonamides, antimalarials, nitrofurantoin), infections, and fava beans. Blood film: Heinz bodies (denatured Hb clumps) and bite cells (oxidative hemolysis marker).

G6PD deficiency (X-linked recessive; common in African, Asian, Mediterranean populations) impairs the pentose phosphate pathway, reducing glutathione and increasing RBC oxidative damage. G6PD assay should not be done during acute crisis as reticulocytes (high G6PD) give false-normal results. Management: stop offending agent, avoid triggers, transfuse in severe cases.

**Q:** What distinguishes megaloblastic from non-megaloblastic macrocytic anemia on blood film?

**A:** Megaloblastic: large oval macrocytes (ovalocytes) + hypersegmented neutrophils (≥5 lobes); pathophysiology = failure of DNA synthesis causing asynchronous nuclear-cytoplasmic maturation. Non-megaloblastic: large round macrocytes without hypersegmented neutrophils; reflects membrane abnormality/abnormal cholesterol metabolism (e.g. alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism).

Key megaloblastic causes: B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, drugs (methotrexate, hydroxyurea, AZT). Non-megaloblastic causes include the ABCDEF mnemonic: Alcoholism/liver disease, B12 deficiency (early), Compensatory reticulocytosis, Drugs/Dysplasia (MDS), Endocrine (hypothyroidism), Folate deficiency/Fetus (pregnancy). MDS is a non-megaloblastic macrocytic anemia with oval macrocytosis and hypolobated neutrophils.

**Q:** What are the neurological manifestations of vitamin B12 deficiency?

**A:** Peripheral neuropathy (symmetrical, legs > arms); subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (posterior columns: loss of vibration/proprioception/2-point discrimination; pyramidal tracts: spastic weakness, ataxia); cerebral (confusion, delirium, dementia — reversible with B12); rare cranial nerve involvement (optic atrophy).

B12 is essential for myelin synthesis. Neurological damage from B12 deficiency can occur even without significant anemia and may be irreversible if untreated (spinal cord damage). Treating with folate alone (without B12) when both are deficient can correct the anemia but allow neurological deterioration to continue — a critical MCCQE1 concept.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and diagnostic investigation for pernicious anemia?

**A:** Pernicious anemia: auto-antibodies against intrinsic factor (IF) or gastric parietal cells → achlorhydria + lack of IF → decreased ileal absorption of B12. Investigations: anti-parietal cell Ab (more sensitive), anti-intrinsic factor Ab (more specific), elevated gastrin, upper endoscopy (atrophic gastritis; screen for gastric malignancy).

Pernicious anemia is the most common cause of B12 deficiency in adults. Auto-antibodies destroy parietal cells, reducing both acid secretion and IF. IF is required to bind B12 for absorption in the terminal ileum. Associated with other autoimmune conditions (polyglandular insufficiency). Most common in Northern European White populations, median age 60.

**Q:** What is the treatment for vitamin B12 deficiency and what electrolyte complication must be monitored?

**A:** Treatment: B12 1000–1200 µg PO daily (if absorption intact) or 1000 µg IM weekly. Maintenance: 1000 µg PO daily or 1000 µg IM monthly. Monitor for hypokalemia and rebound thrombocytosis when treating severe megaloblastic anemia.

Rapid erythropoiesis with B12 treatment causes high potassium uptake into new RBCs, precipitating hypokalemia. Rebound thrombocytosis can occur as megakaryocyte production normalizes. Route depends on the underlying cause: IM is required when GI absorption is impaired (pernicious anemia, ileal disease).

**Q:** What are the main causes of folate deficiency and why is folate deficiency dangerous in pregnancy?

**A:** Causes: poor diet/alcohol use, malabsorption (celiac, IBD), drugs (methotrexate, phenytoin, trimethoprim, pyrimethamine, OCP, alcohol), increased demand (pregnancy, hemolysis, prematurity, hemodialysis, exfoliative dermatitis). Maternal folate deficiency causes fetal neural tube defects.

Unlike B12 (stored 3–4 years), folate stores deplete within 3–6 months. Folate is essential for DNA synthesis and neural tube closure. Canadian guidelines recommend folate 0.4–1 mg/day pre-conception and in first trimester (higher dose 4–5 mg for high-risk women). Treatment: folic acid 1–5 mg PO daily x 1–4 months.

**Q:** What monitoring is recommended for patients with sickle cell disease?

**A:** Regular CBC, reticulocytes, iron indices, BUN, LFTs, creatinine; urinalysis annually (proteinuria/glomerulopathy); transcranial Doppler annually until age 16 (stroke prevention); retinal exams annually from age 8 (retinopathy); echocardiography once in late childhood/early adulthood (pulmonary hypertension).

Systematic screening prevents SCD complications. Transcranial Doppler detects elevated cerebral blood flow velocity (>200 cm/s) indicating stroke risk — these patients benefit from regular transfusion therapy. Retinal screening detects proliferative retinopathy (more common in HbSC). Pulmonary hypertension is a major cause of death in adults with SCD.

**Q:** What is the significance of cis vs. trans α-globin gene deletions in α-thalassemia?

**A:** Cis 2-gene deletion (--/αα) is more common in Southeast Asia and creates risk of offspring inheriting 4-gene deletion (hydrops fetalis/Hb Barts) if partner is also a carrier. Trans 2-gene deletion (α-/α-) is more common in Africa and has lower risk of producing severely affected offspring.

In cis deletion, both genes are absent from one chromosome; a carrier can pass the chromosome with no α genes to offspring. If both parents carry cis deletions, there is a 1 in 4 chance of Hb Barts (4-gene deletion = lethal). Trans deletions distribute the deleted genes across two chromosomes, making 4-gene deletion in offspring much less likely.

**Q:** What is HbSC disease and how does it differ from HbSS?

**A:** HbSC disease: compound heterozygote with one HbS allele and one HbC allele; most common compound heterozygote SCD. Milder anemia than HbSS; similar complications but less frequent — EXCEPT proliferative sickle cell retinopathy, glomerulonephritis, and avascular necrosis are comparably prevalent. Spleen may not be atrophic in adults.

HbSC occurs in approximately 1 in 833 African-American live births. HbC (Glu→Lys at position 6) has less polymerization tendency than HbS. The milder clinical course can lead to delayed diagnosis. Ophthalmic complications are notably as severe or worse than HbSS, making regular retinal screening essential.

## Coagulation Disorders & Thrombosis

**Q:** A 6-year-old boy has recurrent hemarthroses and deep muscle bleeds. PTT is prolonged, PT is normal, and platelet count is normal. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency)

Hemophilia A is X-linked recessive. Prolonged PTT with normal PT indicates intrinsic pathway defect (factors VIII, IX, XI, XII). Hemophilia A (factor VIII) is 4x more common than B (factor IX). Diagnosis confirmed by factor assay.

**Q:** What is von Willebrand disease type 1 and what is first-line treatment for minor bleeding?

**A:** Partial quantitative deficiency of vWF; first-line is desmopressin (DDAVP) IV/SC/intranasal

DDAVP stimulates endothelial release of stored vWF and factor VIII. Effective for vWD type 1 and mild hemophilia A. Not effective for types 2B (may cause thrombocytopenia) or type 3. vWF concentrate used for major bleeding.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)?

**A:** Systemic activation of coagulation by circulating triggers (e.g., sepsis, trauma, malignancy), leading to consumption of clotting factors and platelets, causing simultaneous bleeding and microvascular thrombosis

DIC laboratory: low platelets, low fibrinogen, elevated PT/PTT, elevated D-dimer/FDPs, and schistocytes. Treat the underlying cause. Transfuse FFP/cryoprecipitate for active bleeding. Low-dose hepcidin for thrombotic-predominant DIC (controversial).

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman on warfarin for AF has an INR of 9.2 but no active bleeding. What is the management?

**A:** Hold warfarin and give low-dose oral vitamin K (1–2.5 mg)

For INR >8 without bleeding: hold warfarin + give oral vitamin K 1–2.5 mg (not IV, which causes erratic correction). For major/life-threatening bleeding with supratherapeutic INR: 4-factor PCC (prothrombin complex concentrate) + IV vitamin K 10 mg.

**Q:** What is the antidote for dabigatran (direct thrombin inhibitor) in life-threatening bleeding?

**A:** Idarucizumab (Praxbind) — humanized monoclonal antibody fragment that binds and neutralizes dabigatran

Idarucizumab (5 g IV) reverses dabigatran within minutes. For factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban): andexanet alfa (Ondexxya) or 4-factor PCC if andexanet unavailable. Dialysis can remove dabigatran (small volume of distribution).

**Q:** Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — what is the pathophysiology and when does it typically occur?

**A:** Immune-mediated: IgG antibodies against PF4-heparin complex activate platelets, causing thrombocytopenia and thrombosis; typically occurs 4–14 days after heparin exposure

HIT is paradoxically prothrombotic. Key: platelet count falls >50% or to <100, with new thrombosis. 4T score assesses probability. Immediately stop all heparin and start non-heparin anticoagulation (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, or DOAC).

**Q:** What thrombophilia is most commonly associated with venous thromboembolism in Canada?

**A:** Factor V Leiden mutation (resistance to activated protein C)

Factor V Leiden is the most common inherited thrombophilia (5% prevalence in Caucasians). Heterozygotes have 3–5x increased VTE risk; homozygotes 50–100x. Does not generally require long-term anticoagulation unless recurrent or unprovoked VTE.

**Q:** What is the appropriate duration of anticoagulation for a first unprovoked proximal DVT?

**A:** Minimum 3 months with reassessment for extended therapy based on bleeding risk and patient preference

Canadian guidelines (Thrombosis Canada): 3 months for provoked VTE; extended anticoagulation (indefinite) for unprovoked proximal DVT if low-moderate bleeding risk. DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban) are preferred. Use PADUA, HASBLED scores to guide decision.

**Q:** What is the treatment of choice for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) with platelet count <20 × 10⁹/L?

**A:** Corticosteroids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/day) ± IVIG for faster response in bleeding or pre-procedure

ITP is autoimmune destruction of platelets by anti-GPIIb/IIIa or anti-GPIb/IX antibodies. Corticosteroids are first-line. IVIG provides faster platelet increase (used before surgery or in active bleeding). Rituximab and thrombopoietin receptor agonists (eltrombopag) for refractory ITP.

**Q:** A 35-year-old pregnant woman develops microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute renal failure in her first trimester. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) — ADAMTS13 deficiency

TTP pentad: MAHA + thrombocytopenia + neurological symptoms + renal failure + fever. ADAMTS13 activity <10% confirms congenital or immune TTP. Treatment: emergent plasma exchange (PEX) + caplacizumab + steroids. Without treatment, 90% mortality.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of clopidogrel and when is dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) indicated?

**A:** Clopidogrel irreversibly inhibits P2Y12 ADP receptor on platelets; DAPT (ASA + clopidogrel) is indicated for 12 months post-ACS or PCI with stent placement

Clopidogrel is a prodrug requiring CYP2C19 activation. Ticagrelor and prasugrel are more potent and reliable P2Y12 inhibitors. CYP2C19 poor metabolizers on clopidogrel have higher MACE rates. DAPT duration depends on stent type and bleeding risk.

**Q:** What is the normal platelet count range and at what level does spontaneous bleeding risk increase significantly?

**A:** Normal: 150–400 × 10⁹/L; spontaneous bleeding risk increases significantly below 10–20 × 10⁹/L

Platelet thresholds for procedures: >50 × 10⁹/L for major surgery, >80 × 10⁹/L for neuraxial anesthesia, >100 × 10⁹/L for neurosurgery. Spontaneous intracranial bleeding risk increases below 10 × 10⁹/L.

**Q:** A 60-year-old man has a persistently elevated PT and aPTT, and a mixing study corrects both. What does this suggest?

**A:** Factor deficiency (correction with mixing study = factor deficiency; failure to correct = inhibitor/lupus anticoagulant)

Mixing study: 1:1 mix of patient plasma with normal plasma. Correction suggests factor deficiency (e.g., liver disease, warfarin, factor deficiency). Non-correction (incubated) suggests factor inhibitor (e.g., acquired hemophilia A) or lupus anticoagulant.

**Q:** What is the primary hemostatic defect in Bernard-Soulier syndrome?

**A:** Deficiency or dysfunction of GPIb-IX-V complex — the platelet receptor for von Willebrand factor

Bernard-Soulier syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive platelet disorder with giant platelets, thrombocytopenia, and prolonged bleeding time. GPIb is critical for platelet adhesion to damaged vascular wall via vWF. Compare with Glanzmann thrombasthenia (GPIIb/IIIa defect — platelet aggregation defect).

**Q:** What are D-dimers and what is their clinical utility in diagnosing VTE?

**A:** Fibrin degradation products elevated in VTE, PE, DIC, and many other conditions; high sensitivity (~95%) but low specificity — useful for ruling OUT VTE when pre-test probability is low

D-dimers should only be ordered when pre-test probability is low (Wells score ≤1 for DVT, ≤4 for PE). A negative D-dimer effectively rules out VTE. Positive D-dimer requires imaging (ultrasound for DVT, CTPA for PE). Do not order with high pre-test probability.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for DIC?

**A:** ISTH scoring: platelets <100k(1), <50k(2) + elevated D-dimer(mild 2, severe 3) + prolonged PT(3-6s=1, >6s=2) + low fibrinogen(<1g/L=1). Score >=5 = overt DIC

DIC causes: sepsis, trauma, malignancy (APL), obstetric emergencies. Treatment: treat underlying cause, replace (FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets if bleeding). Heparin controversial.

**Q:** What are the DIC diagnostic criteria and management?

**A:** ISTH DIC score: platelets, fibrin markers (D-dimer), PT prolongation, fibrinogen level. Score >=5 = overt DIC. Management: treat underlying cause + supportive (platelets if <50k+bleeding, cryo if fibrinogen <1.5, FFP if PT prolonged+bleeding)

DIC is always secondary: sepsis, trauma, malignancy, obstetric (abruption, AFE), transfusion. Schistocytes on smear. Lab: low platelets, low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/PTT, elevated D-dimer. Do NOT give heparin routinely (only for thrombotic-predominant DIC).

**Q:** How is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) diagnosed and managed?

**A:** 4T score: Thrombocytopenia (>50% drop), Timing (5-10d), Thrombosis, no oTher cause. Confirm: anti-PF4/heparin ELISA then serotonin release assay. Manage: STOP all heparin, start non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, fondaparinux, DOAC)

HIT is prothrombotic despite low platelets - do NOT transfuse platelets. Occurs with both UFH and LMWH (more common UFH). 4T score low probability: continue heparin. Intermediate/high: stop heparin and test. Transition to warfarin only after platelets recover >150k.

**Q:** What is the approach to an elevated INR/bleeding on warfarin?

**A:** INR 4.5-10 no bleeding: hold warfarin. INR >10 no bleeding: hold + oral vitamin K 2.5-5mg. Serious bleeding: hold warfarin + IV vitamin K 10mg + PCC (4-factor preferred) or FFP

Kcentra (4-factor PCC) reverses warfarin faster than FFP with lower volume. Fresh frozen plasma: 15mL/kg. Vitamin K IV onset 6-8h, PO 12-24h. For DOAC reversal: idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors, or PCC.

**Q:** What is the approach to a prolonged PTT with normal PT?

**A:** DDx: heparin contamination (most common), factor VIII/IX/XI/XII deficiency, von Willebrand disease, lupus anticoagulant. Mixing study: corrects = factor deficiency; does not correct = inhibitor (LA or factor inhibitor)

vWD is most common inherited bleeding disorder (1%). Type 1 (80%): quantitative. Type 2: qualitative. Type 3: absent. Treatment: DDAVP (type 1), vWF concentrate. Lupus anticoagulant paradoxically causes thrombosis not bleeding.

**Q:** How is von Willebrand disease diagnosed and managed?

**A:** Diagnosis: bleeding history + low vWF antigen and/or activity (ristocetin cofactor) + low factor VIII. Type 1: DDAVP trial (desmopressin 0.3mcg/kg IV). Type 2/3: vWF-containing concentrate. Avoid ASA/NSAIDs

vWD most common inherited bleeding (affects 1%). Symptoms: mucocutaneous bleeding (menorrhagia, epistaxis, bruising, post-procedural). Perioperative: DDAVP 30min pre-procedure for Type 1 or vWF concentrate. Tranexamic acid as adjunct for mucosal bleeding.

**Q:** What is the approach to suspected DVT/PE?

**A:** DVT: Wells score then D-dimer (if low probability) or US. PE: Wells/Geneva score then D-dimer or CTPA. Treatment: DOAC first-line (apixaban or rivaroxaban no bridging needed) x3-6mo. Provoked vs unprovoked determines duration

DOAC preferred over warfarin (no INR monitoring, fewer interactions). Exceptions: APS (warfarin), severe renal (warfarin), pregnancy (LMWH), cancer (LMWH or DOAC). Unprovoked VTE: consider extended anticoagulation. Massive PE: thrombolysis or embolectomy.

**Q:** What are the indications for thrombophilia testing?

**A:** Consider testing: unprovoked VTE <50yr, recurrent VTE, unusual site (cerebral, splanchnic, hepatic), strong family history, recurrent pregnancy loss. Do NOT test: provoked VTE, acute VTE (false results), on anticoagulation

Common thrombophilias: Factor V Leiden (most common), prothrombin G20210A, protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome. Testing rarely changes management. Do not test the general population or before OCP use routinely.

**Q:** How is immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) managed?

**A:** Adults: observation if PLT>30k and no bleeding. Treatment if <30k or bleeding: prednisone 1mg/kg x2-4wk, IVIG for rapid rise (pre-procedure), rituximab, TPO-RA (eltrombopag, romiplostim). Splenectomy for refractory

ITP is diagnosis of exclusion: isolated thrombocytopenia, normal smear (except large platelets), no other cause. Rule out pseudothrombocytopenia (EDTA-dependent clumping). In adults consider secondary causes: HIV, HCV, SLE, CLL. Platelet transfusion only for life-threatening bleeding.

**Q:** What transfusion reactions should be recognized and how are they managed?

**A:** Acute hemolytic: ABO incompatibility, fever/chills/flank pain/hemoglobinuria. STOP transfusion, IV NS, send samples. Febrile non-hemolytic: most common, premedicate. Allergic: urticaria (antihistamine). Anaphylactic: IgA deficiency. TRALI: respiratory distress 6h

TRALI: non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema within 6h, supportive care. TACO: volume overload, diuretics. Delayed hemolytic: 3-28 days, extravascular hemolysis from anamnestic response. Bacterial contamination: platelets > RBCs. Report all reactions to blood bank.

**Q:** What is the perioperative management of anticoagulation?

**A:** Warfarin: stop 5d before, bridge with LMWH if high-risk (mechanical valve, recent VTE). DOACs: stop 2-3d before (renal-dependent). Restart 24-72h post-op depending on bleeding risk. ASA: continue for most procedures

BRIDGE trial: no bridging needed for most AF patients on warfarin (lower bleeding, similar stroke). High-risk bridging: mechanical mitral valve, recent VTE <3mo. PAUSE trial guides DOAC timing by CrCl and procedure bleeding risk.

**Q:** What coagulation studies abnormality patterns indicate specific disorders?

**A:** Elevated PT/INR only: warfarin, vitamin K deficiency, factor VII. Elevated PTT only: heparin, hemophilia A/B, vWD. Both elevated: DIC, liver disease, massive transfusion. Normal with bleeding: platelet disorder

Mixing study: corrects = factor deficiency; doesn't correct = inhibitor (lupus anticoagulant). D-dimer: elevated in PE, DIC, post-surgery — high sensitivity, low specificity.

**Q:** What is the approach to managing anticoagulation perioperatively?

**A:** Low bleed risk surgery: continue anticoagulation. High bleed risk: hold warfarin 5 days (bridge with LMWH if high thrombotic risk — mechanical valve, recent VTE <3mo). DOACs: hold 24-48h based on renal function and bleed risk

CCS/Thrombosis Canada guidelines. Bridge anticoagulation NOT needed for most AF patients (BRIDGE trial). Resume 24-72h post-op based on hemostasis. DOAC-specific reversal: idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa (factor Xa inhibitors).

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)?

**A:** 4T score: Thrombocytopenia (>50% fall=2), Timing (5-10 days=2), Thrombosis (new=2), oTher causes excluded(2). Score >=6 high probability. Confirm with ELISA + serotonin release assay

HIT type II is immune-mediated (PF4-heparin antibodies). Paradoxically causes thrombosis not bleeding. Treatment: stop ALL heparin, start alternative anticoagulant (argatroban, fondaparinux, DOAC). Contraindicated: warfarin (during acute phase).

**Q:** What is the VTE prophylaxis regimen for orthopedic surgery?

**A:** LMWH, rivaroxaban, or apixaban for 14 days (knee) or 35 days (hip) post-arthroplasty. Start 12h post-op (LMWH) or 6-10h (DOAC)

Thrombosis Canada guidelines. ASA alone is inferior to pharmacological prophylaxis. Add mechanical (IPC) in all patients. Extended prophylaxis critical for hip fracture/replacement.

**Q:** What is the management of warfarin over-anticoagulation?

**A:** INR 4.5-10 no bleeding: hold warfarin, recheck. INR >10 no bleeding: hold + vitamin K 2.5-5mg PO. Major bleeding: hold + IV vitamin K 10mg + PCC (4-factor) or FFP

PCC (Octaplex/Beriplex) preferred over FFP: faster onset, smaller volume, no ABO matching needed. Resume warfarin at lower dose when INR therapeutic. Consider switching to DOAC if labile INRs.

**Q:** What distinguishes TTP from HUS?

**A:** TTP: ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%), neurological symptoms predominate, adults. HUS: Shiga toxin (E. coli O157:H7), renal failure predominates, children post-diarrheal illness. Both: MAHA + thrombocytopenia

TTP pentad: MAHA, thrombocytopenia, renal failure, neuro symptoms, fever (full pentad rare). Treatment: TTP = plasma exchange urgently. HUS = supportive (no antibiotics for STEC). Atypical HUS: complement-mediated, treat with eculizumab.

**Q:** A 45-year-old woman undergoes total knee replacement with normal renal function. What is the recommended VTE prophylaxis per Thrombosis Canada guidelines?

**A:** LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily or dalteparin 5000 IU SC daily) starting 12-24 hours postoperatively, continued for minimum 10-14 days with extended prophylaxis up to 35 days. Rivaroxaban 10 mg daily is an accepted alternative.

Thrombosis Canada and CCS guidelines recommend extended-duration LMWH or DOAC for major orthopedic surgery. VTE risk persists beyond discharge. The RECORD trials established rivaroxaban as non-inferior to enoxaparin for orthopedic prophylaxis.

**Q:** A patient on warfarin (INR 8.5) has gross hematuria but is hemodynamically stable with no life-threatening bleeding. How should you manage reversal?

**A:** Hold warfarin and give oral vitamin K 2.5-5 mg. Recheck INR in 24 hours. Do not give PCC or FFP unless major/life-threatening bleeding develops.

For supratherapeutic INR without life-threatening bleeding, oral vitamin K provides gradual sustained reversal. Thrombosis Canada reserves 4-factor PCC for major hemorrhage. IV vitamin K acts faster but carries small anaphylactoid risk.

**Q:** A patient on rivaroxaban presents with massive GI bleed requiring emergent surgery. What is the reversal strategy?

**A:** Administer 4-factor PCC 50 IU/kg as immediate measure. If available, andexanet alfa is the specific reversal agent for factor Xa inhibitors. Supportive care with transfusion, hold rivaroxaban, activated charcoal if last dose within 2 hours.

Andexanet alfa is Health Canada-approved for reversal of rivaroxaban and apixaban in life-threatening bleeding. When unavailable, 4-factor PCC is the recommended alternative. Idarucizumab is specific to dabigatran only.

**Q:** What laboratory findings distinguish DIC from primary fibrinolysis?

**A:** DIC: prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes on smear. Primary fibrinolysis: similar coagulation abnormalities but platelet count is typically normal and no schistocytes.

DIC involves systemic activation of both coagulation and fibrinolysis with microangiopathic hemolysis. The ISTH DIC scoring system uses platelet count, D-dimer, fibrinogen, and PT prolongation. Schistocytes reflect the microangiopathic process unique to DIC.

**Q:** How do you differentiate TTP from HUS clinically and by laboratory findings?

**A:** TTP: severely reduced ADAMTS13 activity (<10%) is the diagnostic hallmark, with thrombocytopenia, MAHA, neurological symptoms, fever, renal impairment. HUS: predominantly renal failure with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, often preceded by bloody diarrhea (STEC-HUS). ADAMTS13 is normal in HUS.

ADAMTS13 assay is the key differentiator. Typical HUS follows Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (O157:H7) infection. Atypical HUS involves complement dysregulation and may respond to eculizumab. Avoid antibiotics and antimotility agents in STEC-HUS.

**Q:** A medical inpatient with pneumonia has a Padua score of 5. What VTE prophylaxis is recommended?

**A:** Pharmacologic prophylaxis with LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily or dalteparin 5000 IU SC daily) for the duration of hospitalization or until fully mobile.

Padua Prediction Score >=4 identifies medical inpatients at high VTE risk warranting pharmacologic prophylaxis. Canadian guidelines align with this risk-stratified approach. Mechanical prophylaxis alone is reserved for patients with contraindications to anticoagulation.

**Q:** A patient on dabigatran develops intracranial hemorrhage. What is the specific reversal agent and dose?

**A:** Idarucizumab 5 g IV (two 2.5 g boluses no more than 15 minutes apart). Provides immediate, complete, and sustained reversal of dabigatran.

Idarucizumab binds dabigatran with 350-fold greater affinity than thrombin. The RE-VERSE AD trial demonstrated complete reversal within minutes. It does not reverse other DOACs. Andexanet alfa or PCC is used for factor Xa inhibitors.

**Q:** What are the components of the ISTH scoring system for overt DIC?

**A:** Score >=5 = overt DIC. Platelet count (>100=0, 50-100=1, <50=2); D-dimer (no increase=0, moderate=2, strong=3); prolonged PT (<3s=0, 3-6s=1, >6s=2); fibrinogen (>1.0=0, <1.0=1). Repeat every 24-48 hours.

The ISTH DIC score should only be applied in patients with an underlying disorder known to cause DIC (sepsis, trauma, malignancy, obstetric complications). Serial monitoring guides response to treatment of the underlying cause. A score of 5 or more is compatible with overt DIC.

**Q:** When should you suspect heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and what is the initial workup?

**A:** Suspect HIT when platelet count drops >50% from baseline or to <150, typically 5-10 days after heparin exposure. Calculate 4Ts score. If intermediate/high probability, send anti-PF4/heparin ELISA and switch to non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban or fondaparinux).

The 4Ts score stratifies pretest probability: low (0-3), intermediate (4-5), high (6-8). A negative PF4 ELISA effectively rules out HIT. All heparin products must be stopped immediately including flushes, and warfarin must not be started until platelets recover.

**Q:** A pregnant woman at 28 weeks develops a DVT. What is the recommended anticoagulation?

**A:** Weight-adjusted LMWH (enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC q12h) for the duration of pregnancy and at least 6 weeks postpartum (minimum total 3 months). Warfarin and DOACs are contraindicated in pregnancy.

LMWH does not cross the placenta. Warfarin is teratogenic (nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses) particularly in the first trimester. DOACs lack safety data in pregnancy. SOGC guidelines recommend switching to warfarin postpartum if desired.

**Q:** A patient with prosthetic heart valves and warfarin is found to have active GI bleeding. Which reversal agent is most appropriate?

**A:** Vitamin K (phytonadione) and/or four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC) for urgent reversal

Warfarin blocks synthesis of clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X via vitamin K antagonism. For urgent reversal, 4F-PCC provides faster INR correction than vitamin K alone; fresh frozen plasma is an alternative per Canadian guidelines.

**Q:** What is the FDA-approved reversal agent for dabigatran in the setting of life-threatening bleeding?

**A:** Idarucizumab (Praxbind)

Idarucizumab is a monoclonal antibody fragment that binds dabigatran with high affinity, rapidly reversing its anticoagulant effect. It is indicated for life-threatening or uncontrolled bleeding in patients on dabigatran.

**Q:** A patient on apixaban for AFib presents with intracranial hemorrhage. What reversal agent should be given?

**A:** Andexanet alfa

Andexanet alfa is an FDA/Health Canada approved reversal agent for apixaban and rivaroxaban (direct factor Xa inhibitors). It acts as a decoy receptor that binds and sequesters the anticoagulant.

**Q:** A patient develops HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) while on unfractionated heparin post-CABG. How does HIT risk compare between UFH and LMWH?

**A:** HIT is less common with LMWHs than with unfractionated heparin

UFH carries a higher risk of HIT than LMWHs due to greater platelet factor 4 binding. Upon HIT diagnosis, all heparin products must be stopped and an alternative anticoagulant (e.g., argatroban) should be initiated per Canadian guidelines.

**Q:** What are the four phases of hemostasis?

**A:** 1. Primary hemostasis — platelet plug formation via adhesion (VWF-collagen), activation (granule release), and aggregation (GPIIb/IIIa). 2. Secondary hemostasis — fibrin clot reinforcement via extrinsic (initiation) and intrinsic (amplification) coagulation pathways converging on thrombin generation. 3. Fibrin stabilization — soluble fibrin converted to insoluble cross-linked clot. 4. Fibrinolysis — clot dissolution by the fibrinolytic system.

Primary hemostasis addresses mucocutaneous bleeding rapidly. Defects in primary hemostasis (thrombocytopenia, VWD, aspirin) cause petechiae/mucocutaneous bleeding. Defects in secondary hemostasis (factor deficiencies, hemophilia) cause deep/delayed bleeding. Both pathways converge at factor X activation leading to thrombin and fibrin generation.

**Q:** What are ABO blood group antigens and antibodies?

**A:** Group O: H antigen on RBC, Anti-A and Anti-B in serum (universal donor). Group A: A antigen, Anti-B in serum. Group B: B antigen, Anti-A in serum. Group AB: A and B antigens, no antibodies in serum (universal recipient).

ABO incompatibility is the most common cause of acute hemolytic transfusion reactions, usually due to clerical errors. Understanding antigen-antibody relationships is essential for safe blood product selection and cross-matching.

**Q:** What are the indications for packed red blood cell (pRBC) transfusion?

**A:** Hb <70 g/L in most patients. Maintain Hb 70-90 g/L during active bleeding. Consider higher threshold (Hb 80 g/L) for: CAD/unstable coronary syndromes, uncontrolled/unpredictable bleeding. 1 unit of pRBC raises Hb by approximately 10 g/L. Transfuse minimum units necessary to relieve symptoms or achieve safe Hb range.

Canadian guidelines (Canadian Society of Hematology Choosing Wisely) advocate restrictive transfusion strategies (target Hb 70-80 g/L) in stable non-cardiac patients, as liberal transfusion does not improve outcomes and may cause harm (TACO, transfusion reactions).

**Q:** What are the indications for platelet transfusion by platelet count threshold?

**A:** <10 × 10⁹/L: non-immune thrombocytopenia (prophylactic). <20 × 10⁹/L: procedures not associated with significant blood loss. <50 × 10⁹/L: procedures associated with blood loss or major surgery (>500 mL EBL). <100 × 10⁹/L: pre-neurosurgery or head trauma. Any count: platelet dysfunction (or antiplatelet agents) with marked bleeding.

Platelet transfusion thresholds vary by clinical context. Relative contraindications include TTP, HIT, post-transfusion purpura, and HELLP syndrome, where platelet transfusion may worsen thrombotic microangiopathy or platelet consumption.

**Q:** What coagulation factor products are available and what are their indications?

**A:** Fresh Plasma (FP/FFP): multiple factor depletion (sepsis, DIC, liver disease), emergency warfarin reversal. Cryoprecipitate (enriched fibrinogen, VWF, VIII, XIII): hemophilia A, VWD (emergencies), hypofibrinogenemia. Fibrinogen concentrate: hypofibrinogenemia. Factor VIII concentrate: hemophilia A. Factor IX concentrate: hemophilia B. Recombinant factor VIIa: factor VII deficiency, hemophilia A/B with inhibitors, Glanzmann's thrombasthenia. PCC (Octaplex, Beriplex): warfarin reversal, vitamin K deficiency, urgent reversal of direct Xa inhibitors.

PCC contains factors II, VII, IX, X and is preferred over FFP for urgent warfarin reversal due to faster administration and smaller volume. Cryoprecipitate is particularly useful for fibrinogen replacement in massive hemorrhage. Factor concentrates are preferred over cryoprecipitate for hemophilia when available.

**Q:** What is an Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHTR) and how is it managed?

**A:** ABO incompatibility → intravascular hemolysis via complement activation; occurs immediately post-transfusion. Most commonly due to incorrect patient identification. Risk: <1/40,000. Presentation: fever, chills, hypotension, back/flank pain, dyspnea, hemoglobinuria, acute renal failure, DIC. Management: STOP transfusion immediately, notify blood bank, check clerical error, send hemolysis labs (CBC, bilirubin, LDH, reticulocytes, DAT), maintain BP (IV fluids + inotropes), maintain urine output (diuretics, crystalloids, dopamine).

AHTR is a potentially fatal emergency. The most common cause is clerical error (wrong blood, wrong patient). Hemoglobinuria ('port-wine' urine) indicates massive intravascular hemolysis. Early and aggressive IV fluid resuscitation prevents acute tubular necrosis from hemoglobin precipitation.

**Q:** What is a Febrile Nonhemolytic Transfusion Reaction (FNHTR) and when should you stop the transfusion?

**A:** Due to alloantibodies to donor WBCs/platelets or cytokine release. Occurs within 6 hours. Risk: 1/100 (minor) to 1/10,000-40,000 (severe). Presents with fever, rigors, flushing, headache, myalgia. If temperature <39°C and no serious symptoms: continue transfusion at slower rate + antipyretics. If temperature ≥39°C or serious symptoms (shaking, chills, hypotension, tachycardia, dyspnea, back/chest pain, N/V): STOP transfusion, investigate, supportive measures.

FNHTR is the most common transfusion reaction. Leukodepletion (standard in Canada) reduces its incidence by removing the WBCs responsible for cytokine release. The critical step is ruling out a more serious hemolytic reaction before attributing fever to FNHTR.

**Q:** What is TRALI (Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury) and how does it differ from TACO?

**A:** TRALI: new-onset acute lung injury within 6 h of transfusion. Features: profound hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 <300), bilateral pulmonary edema on imaging, NO evidence of left atrial hypertension. Pathogenesis: donor Abs bind recipient WBCs → mediator release → increased pulmonary capillary permeability. Occurs 2-4 h post-transfusion, resolves in 24-72 h. Risk: 1/10,000. LEADING cause of transfusion-related morbidity/mortality. Treatment: supportive (oxygen). TACO: due to cardiac dysfunction or rapid transfusion → hypertensive pulmonary edema; treated with diuretics.

TRALI vs TACO distinction is key: TRALI is noncardiogenic (normal or low pulmonary wedge pressure) while TACO is cardiogenic (elevated filling pressures, hypertension, responds to diuretics). TRALI treatment is supportive only; diuretics are NOT indicated.

**Q:** What are the risk factors and features of Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO)?

**A:** Due to impaired cardiac function or excessively rapid transfusion. Incidence: 1/100. Risk factors: age >70, heart failure, history of MI, renal failure, positive fluid balance. Presentation: dyspnea, orthopnea, hypertension, tachycardia, crackles at lung bases, increased venous pressure. Treatment: stop transfusion, diuretics, oxygen; transfuse at lower rate with diuretics to prevent in high-risk patients.

TACO is the second most common serious transfusion complication after TRALI. Prevention in high-risk patients includes slower transfusion rates and pre-treatment with furosemide. Unlike TRALI, TACO responds to diuresis.

**Q:** What are irradiated blood products and when are they indicated?

**A:** Irradiated blood products prevent proliferation of donor T-cells in recipients at risk of transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GvHD). Indicated for: severe T-cell immunodeficiency, patients on purine analogue chemotherapy, Hodgkin lymphoma patients, candidates for BM transplant, directed transfusions from first-degree relatives, HLA-matched products, intrauterine transfusions.

TA-GvHD occurs when donor T-cells engraft and attack the immunocompromised host, with near-100% fatality. Irradiation renders donor T-cells incapable of proliferation. Leukodepletion (standard in Canada) is NOT sufficient to prevent TA-GvHD — irradiation is required.

**Q:** What is the difference between a Group & Screen (G&S) and a cross-match?

**A:** Group & Screen: determines ABO blood group + Rh status, plus screens for auto- or alloantibodies against major/minor blood group antigens in patient plasma. Cross-match: mixes recipient blood with potential donor blood to look for agglutination (takes 30-45 min). Emergency blood selection hierarchy: (1st) fully crossmatched blood; (2nd) same ABO/Rh group; (3rd) O- for reproductive-age females, O+ for all others.

Electronic cross-match is increasingly used where available. O-negative blood is reserved for reproductive-age females to prevent Rh sensitization that could cause hemolytic disease of the newborn. For males and post-menopausal females, O-positive is acceptable in emergencies.

## Lymphoma & Leukemia

**Q:** What is the most common presenting symptom of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL)?

**A:** Painless cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy

HL affects young adults (bimodal: 15–35 and >55 years) and presents with painless lymphadenopathy in cervical/mediastinal region. B symptoms (fever >38°C, night sweats, >10% weight loss) are adverse prognostic factors. Reed-Sternberg cells are pathognomonic.

**Q:** What is the Ann Arbor staging system for lymphoma?

**A:** Stage I: one lymph node region; II: two or more regions same side of diaphragm; III: both sides; IV: extranodal dissemination; A=no B symptoms, B=B symptoms present

Ann Arbor staging guides treatment intensity. For HL, stages I-II are early disease (curable with combined chemo-RT); stages III-IV require ABVD chemotherapy (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) × 6 cycles.

**Q:** What distinguishes diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) from follicular lymphoma?

**A:** DLBCL is aggressive (grows rapidly, often symptomatic) and potentially curable; follicular lymphoma is indolent (slow-growing, often asymptomatic) but generally incurable with conventional therapy

DLBCL is the most common NHL. Treatment: R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) × 6 cycles. Follicular lymphoma: watch-and-wait if asymptomatic, rituximab-based therapy when treatment indicated.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of rituximab in lymphoma treatment?

**A:** Anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that causes complement-dependent cytotoxicity, ADCC, and direct apoptosis of B cells

CD20 is expressed on mature B cells and >90% of B-cell NHLs. Rituximab + chemotherapy (R-CHOP) is standard for most B-cell lymphomas. Side effects: infusion reactions, hepatitis B reactivation (must screen all patients before rituximab).

**Q:** A 65-year-old man with CLL develops autoimmune hemolytic anemia. What is the treatment?

**A:** Corticosteroids (prednisone) ± rituximab for AIHA; CLL treatment with ibrutinib or venetoclax if CLL itself requires therapy

AIHA complicates 10–25% of CLL. Warm AIHA responds to steroids. Rituximab is effective for steroid-refractory cases. Avoid fludarabine in CLL patients with AIHA as it can worsen it.

**Q:** What is the recommended initial workup for newly diagnosed lymphoma?

**A:** Excisional lymph node biopsy (not FNA), CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET scan for staging, bone marrow biopsy (for NHL), CBC, LDH, uric acid, hepatitis B/C serology, HIV

FNA is insufficient for lymphoma subtyping — architectural pattern assessment requires excisional or core needle biopsy. PET-CT is standard for HL and DLBCL staging. LDH is a prognostic marker in NHL.

**Q:** What is the International Prognostic Index (IPI) used for in lymphoma?

**A:** Predicting prognosis in aggressive NHL (DLBCL); risk factors: age >60, elevated LDH, ECOG PS >1, Stage III/IV, >1 extranodal site

IPI scores 0–5: low (0–1), low-intermediate (2), high-intermediate (3), high (4–5) risk. 5-year OS ranges from ~70% (low) to ~30% (high). Adapted R-IPI is used in rituximab era.

**Q:** What is the cure rate for early-stage (Stage I-II) classical Hodgkin lymphoma with combined modality therapy?

**A:** >90% cure rate with ABVD chemotherapy + involved-field radiation therapy

HL is one of the most curable cancers. Long-term survivorship issues include secondary malignancies (breast cancer in women irradiated for mediastinal disease), cardiac disease, pulmonary toxicity (bleomycin), and infertility.

**Q:** A patient on long-term immunosuppression post-renal transplant develops lymphadenopathy and B symptoms. Biopsy shows EBV-positive large B-cell proliferation. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD)

PTLD occurs in 1–3% of solid organ transplants, driven by EBV-positive B-cell proliferation in the setting of impaired T-cell immunity. First step: reduce immunosuppression. Rituximab for CD20+ PTLD. Chemotherapy for aggressive forms.

**Q:** Which lymphoma subtype is strongly associated with H. pylori infection?

**A:** Gastric MALT lymphoma (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma)

H. pylori-driven chronic antigenic stimulation leads to gastric MALT lymphoma. Eradication of H. pylori with triple therapy results in regression of early-stage gastric MALT in 70–80% of cases. H. pylori eradication is first-line therapy.

**Q:** What is Ann Arbor staging for lymphoma?

**A:** I: single node region. II: >=2 node regions same side of diaphragm. III: both sides of diaphragm. IV: diffuse extranodal. A=no B symptoms, B=fever/night sweats/weight loss >10%

Hodgkin: bimodal (20s and 60s), Reed-Sternberg cells, contiguous spread. Treatment: ABVD chemotherapy +/- radiation. NHL: more heterogeneous, often presents at advanced stage. R-CHOP for DLBCL.

**Q:** What is the Ann Arbor staging system for lymphoma?

**A:** Stage I: single lymph node region. II: >=2 regions same side of diaphragm. III: both sides of diaphragm. IV: disseminated extranodal (bone marrow, liver). B symptoms: fever >38, night sweats, >10% weight loss 6mo

Staging determines treatment approach. PET-CT is standard for FDG-avid lymphomas (most HL, DLBCL). CT for low-grade NHL. B symptoms confer worse prognosis. Lugano classification has replaced Ann Arbor for response assessment.

**Q:** How is Hodgkin lymphoma diagnosed and treated?

**A:** Diagnosis: Reed-Sternberg cells (owl-eye) on biopsy, CD15+/CD30+. Treatment: early favorable: ABVD x2 + radiation. Early unfavorable/advanced: ABVD x4-6. PET-adapted: escalate to BEACOPP if PET+ after 2 cycles

HL: bimodal age (20s and >60). Excellent prognosis: early stage >90% cure. Contiguous spread pattern. ABVD: doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine. Bleomycin pulmonary toxicity: monitor PFTs. Long-term: secondary malignancies, CV disease from radiation.

**Q:** What are the major subtypes of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and their management?

**A:** Aggressive: DLBCL (most common NHL) - treat with R-CHOP x6, curable in ~60%. Indolent: follicular lymphoma - watch-and-wait if asymptomatic, BR or R-CHOP when symptomatic. Very aggressive: Burkitt - urgent intensive chemo + CNS prophylaxis

NHL is much more common than HL. Non-contiguous spread (any stage at presentation). Aggressive NHL is potentially curable with chemo. Indolent NHL is generally incurable but has long natural history. Transformation of indolent to aggressive is a feared complication.

**Q:** What is the approach to a patient presenting with lymphadenopathy?

**A:** Red flags: >2cm, supraclavicular, fixed/hard, >4-6wk, B symptoms, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly. Workup: CBC, LDH, imaging (CT), excisional biopsy (NOT FNA for suspected lymphoma)

Most lymphadenopathy is reactive/infectious. Supraclavicular (especially left/Virchow) highly suspicious for malignancy. Excisional biopsy preserves architecture for diagnosis. FNA insufficient for lymphoma subtyping. Generalized: consider HIV, SLE, lymphoma, infections.

**Q:** What is tumor lysis syndrome and how is it prevented?

**A:** Lab: hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, elevated LDH, elevated Cr. Prevention: IV hydration + allopurinol (moderate risk) or rasburicase (high risk). Treat: aggressive hydration, rasburicase, cardiac monitoring, dialysis if needed

TLS risk highest in rapidly proliferating tumors: Burkitt, ALL, high tumor burden. Can be spontaneous. Cairo-Bishop criteria for diagnosis. Rasburicase (recombinant urate oxidase) rapidly reduces uric acid. CI in G6PD deficiency. Monitor labs q6-8h during treatment.

**Q:** What is the approach to chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)?

**A:** Diagnosis: lymphocytosis >5x10^9/L with clonal B-cells (CD5+/CD19+/CD23+). Rai/Binet staging. Many patients watch-and-wait. Treat indications: cytopenias, B symptoms, rapid doubling, bulky nodes. First-line: BTKi (ibrutinib) or venetoclax-based

CLL most common adult leukemia. Smudge cells on smear. Richter transformation to DLBCL in 5-10%. Hypogammaglobulinemia increases infection risk. AIHA and ITP can complicate CLL. Del(17p)/TP53 mutation: poor prognosis, BTKi or venetoclax preferred.

**Q:** What is multiple myeloma: diagnosis, staging, and treatment?

**A:** Diagnostic criteria: clonal BM plasma cells >=10% + myeloma-defining event (CRAB: Calcium elevated, Renal failure, Anemia, Bone lesions) or biomarker (>=60% BM plasma cells, FLC ratio >=100, >1 MRI lesion)

SPEP/UPEP, serum free light chains, BM biopsy. Staging: R-ISS. Treatment: induction with triplet (VRd: bortezomib, lenalidomide, dex) then autologous SCT if eligible. Bone protection: zoledronic acid. Avoid nephrotoxins. Skeletal survey or low-dose whole-body CT (not bone scan).

**Q:** What oncologic emergencies should be recognized in hematologic malignancies?

**A:** Febrile neutropenia (ANC<0.5+fever): blood cultures + empiric broad-spectrum abx immediately (piperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem). Tumor lysis syndrome. Hyperviscosity (myeloma): plasmapheresis. Leukostasis (WBC>100k): leukapheresis

Febrile neutropenia: mortality risk. Do NOT wait for cultures to start antibiotics. Door-to-antibiotic time <60min. SVC syndrome: urgent radiation or stenting. Spinal cord compression: urgent MRI + dexamethasone + radiation/surgery.

**Q:** What is the approach to an incidental finding of elevated WBC?

**A:** Reactive leukocytosis (most common): infection, stress, steroids, smoking. If persistent or very high (>50k): check peripheral smear for blasts. CLL: mature lymphocytes >5k. CML: left shift with all stages of myeloid maturation, BCR-ABL+

CML: Philadelphia chromosome BCR-ABL. Treatment: TKI (imatinib) with excellent prognosis. AML: blasts >20% in marrow, Auer rods. ALL: blasts, more common in children. Leukemoid reaction: very high WBC from infection/inflammation, LAP score high (low in CML).

**Q:** What is monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS)?

**A:** MGUS: M-protein <30g/L, BM plasma cells <10%, no CRAB criteria, no amyloidosis. 1% per year risk of progression to myeloma. Monitor annually: SPEP, CBC, calcium, creatinine

MGUS is common (3% of >50yr). Smouldering myeloma: M-protein 30g/L or 10-60% BM plasma cells, no CRAB. Higher risk of progression (10%/yr for first 5yr). Risk stratification: M-protein size, non-IgG type, abnormal FLC ratio predict progression.

**Q:** What is the staging workup for Hodgkin lymphoma and how do the common NHL subtypes differ?

**A:** Hodgkin lymphoma staging (Ann Arbor): I: single node region; II: ≥2 node regions same side of diaphragm; III: nodes on both sides; IV: extranodal involvement (liver, bone marrow). Suffix: A (no B symptoms), B (fever >38°C, night sweats, >10% weight loss in 6 months). Workup: PET-CT (gold standard for staging), bone marrow biopsy (being replaced by PET), excisional lymph node biopsy (Reed-Sternberg cells), CBC, LDH, ESR, LFTs, hepatitis B/C, HIV. NHL subtypes: DLBCL (most common aggressive NHL — 'curable' with R-CHOP); Follicular (most common indolent — 'watch and wait' if asymptomatic, rituximab-based when treatment needed); Burkitt (highly aggressive, starry-sky pattern, associated with EBV/HIV, t(8;14) c-myc translocation — intensive chemo, high cure rate); Mantle cell (intermediate, t(11;14) cyclin D1).

PET-CT has revolutionized lymphoma staging and response assessment (Deauville criteria). Hodgkin lymphoma is one of the most curable cancers (>85% cure rate with ABVD chemotherapy). Lymphoma Canada and Canadian Cancer Trials Group coordinate lymphoma research and provide patient resources. R-CHOP (rituximab + cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) is the standard of care for DLBCL.

**Q:** What is the approach to non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) treatment and how does DLBCL management differ from follicular lymphoma?

**A:** DLBCL (aggressive): R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) × 6 cycles — goal is CURE (~60-70% cure rate); PET-CT for response assessment (Deauville criteria); CNS prophylaxis (intrathecal methotrexate) for high-risk patients (testicular, breast, kidney involvement, high IPI score); relapsed: salvage chemo + autologous stem cell transplant, CAR-T cell therapy. Follicular lymphoma (indolent): if asymptomatic (low tumor burden): watch and wait (no survival benefit from early treatment); if symptomatic/bulky: rituximab + bendamustine or R-CHOP, maintenance rituximab; NOT curable with standard therapy — characterized by relapsing-remitting course; transformation to DLBCL occurs in ~3%/year (biopsy any rapidly growing node).

The IPI (International Prognostic Index) stratifies DLBCL risk: age >60, elevated LDH, ECOG ≥2, stage III-IV, >1 extranodal site. CAR-T cell therapy (axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel) has revolutionized relapsed/refractory DLBCL treatment and is available at select Canadian centres. Lymphoma Canada coordinates patient support and clinical trial access.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of Mycosis Fungoides (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma)?

**A:** Slightly atrophic scaling, erythematous patches/plaques/nodules/tumours, possibly pruritic and poikilodermic (atrophy, telangiectasia, hyper/hypopigmentation); common sites: trunk, buttocks, proximal limbs.

Mycosis fungoides is the most common primary cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. Early disease has an excellent prognosis. The hypopigmented subtype is most common in children with deeper skin tones.

**Q:** What distinguishes Sézary syndrome from mycosis fungoides?

**A:** Sézary syndrome is a widespread systemic variant with erythroderma, lymphadenopathy, and WBC >20×10⁹/L with Sézary cells; it is associated with intense pruritus, alopecia, and high mortality.

Sézary syndrome can evolve from mycosis fungoides or arise de novo. Flow cytometry showing CD4:CD8 ratio >10 is characteristic. It has a significantly worse prognosis than localized mycosis fungoides.

**Q:** What is the cell of origin in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (mycosis fungoides/Sézary syndrome)?

**A:** Clonal proliferation of skin-homing CD4+ T-cells.

The malignant cells in CTCL are memory T-helper (CD4+) cells that aberrantly home to the skin. Skin biopsy with T-cell receptor gene rearrangement studies is key to diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the key morphologic differences between AML blasts and ALL blasts?

**A:** AML: large blasts, abundant cytoplasm, 3-5 nucleoli, granules and Auer rods, myeloperoxidase/Sudan black positive. ALL: small blasts, scant cytoplasm, 1-3 nucleoli, no granules or Auer rods, PAS positive.

The mnemonic 'Big and SmALL' helps: AML has big blasts (adults, more cytoplasm, more nucleoli, Auer rods) while ALL has small blasts (children, less cytoplasm, fewer nucleoli). Myeloperoxidase positivity confirms myeloid lineage in AML.

**Q:** What are the Ann Arbor stages for lymphoma?

**A:** Stage I: single lymph node region or extralymphatic site (IE). Stage II: ≥2 lymph node regions or extralymphatic site + >1 node region on SAME side of diaphragm. Stage III: lymph node regions on BOTH sides of diaphragm. Stage IV: diffuse involvement of ≥1 extralymphatic organs including bone marrow.

The Ann Arbor system stratifies disease extent. Suffix 'E' denotes extralymphatic involvement, 'A' denotes absence of B-symptoms, 'B' denotes presence of B-symptoms. Stage dictates treatment approach and prognosis.

**Q:** What chromosomal translocations are associated with common lymphoid malignancies?

**A:** t(2;5) → ALK mutation → Anaplastic large cell lymphoma. t(8;14) → Myc activation → Burkitt lymphoma. t(14;18) → Bcl-2 activation → Follicular lymphoma. t(11;14) → Cyclin D1 overexpression → Mantle cell lymphoma. t(11;18) → MALT1 activation → MALT lymphoma.

Each translocation activates a specific oncogene. Bcl-2 prevents apoptosis in follicular lymphoma; Myc drives rapid proliferation in Burkitt; Cyclin D1 promotes cell cycle entry in mantle cell lymphoma.

**Q:** What are the hallmark features of Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL)?

**A:** Malignant proliferation of lymphoid cells with Reed-Sternberg cells. Bimodal age distribution (peaks at 20 yr and >50 yr). Associated with EBV in up to 50% of cases. Classically presents with non-tender, rubbery cervical/supraclavicular lymphadenopathy (60-80%). Can present with B-symptoms, mediastinal mass, pruritus.

Reed-Sternberg cells (large binucleated cells with prominent 'owl-eye' nucleoli) are pathognomonic for HL. HL spreads contiguously from one node to adjacent nodes, unlike NHL which often presents at multiple non-contiguous sites.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for Hodgkin Lymphoma by stage?

**A:** Stage I-II: ABVD chemotherapy (adriamycin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) followed by involved-field or involved-site radiotherapy (XRT). Stage III-IV: ABVD, brentuximab-AVD, or BEACOPP + XRT for bulky disease. Relapsed/refractory: high-dose chemo + autologous stem cell transplant, anti-CD30 Ab, pembrolizumab.

ABVD is the standard first-line regimen. BEACOPP is more intensive and used for advanced disease. PET scan is essential for assessing response. Treatment complications include cardiac disease (adriamycin), pulmonary toxicity (bleomycin), and secondary malignancies.

**Q:** What are the Hasenclever adverse prognostic factors for advanced Hodgkin Lymphoma?

**A:** Seven adverse factors: (1) serum albumin <40 g/L, (2) Hb <105 g/L, (3) male sex, (4) stage IV disease, (5) age ≥45 yr, (6) leukocytosis (WBC >15 × 10⁹/L), (7) lymphocytopenia (<0.06 × 10⁹/L or <8% of WBC). Each additional factor decreases 5-year freedom from progression (FFP).

With 4 adverse factors, 5-year FFP is approximately 51%; with ≥5 factors, it drops to ~60% reduction from baseline. This international prognostic score guides risk stratification and intensity of therapy for advanced-stage HL.

**Q:** What complications can result from treatment of Hodgkin Lymphoma?

**A:** Cardiac disease (secondary to XRT or adriamycin cardiomyopathy in ~1%). Pulmonary disease (bleomycin-induced interstitial pneumonitis). Infertility (<3% with ABVD; discuss sperm banking/egg retrieval before treatment). Secondary malignancies: solid tumours of lung or breast (>8 yr after treatment), non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Hypothyroidism (post-XRT).

Long-term survivors of HL face significant treatment-related morbidity. Bleomycin pulmonary toxicity is cumulative and dose-dependent. Secondary breast cancers are especially relevant for young women receiving chest irradiation, requiring enhanced screening.

**Q:** How is Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL) classified by natural history?

**A:** WHO/REAL classification: (1) Indolent (35-40%): e.g. follicular lymphoma, SLL/CLL, mantle cell lymphoma — slow growing, rarely curable. (2) Aggressive (~50%): e.g. diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) — rapidly progressive but potentially curable. (3) Highly aggressive (~5%): e.g. Burkitt lymphoma — very rapid growth, requires intensive treatment.

Most NHL (85%) originates from B-cells. Indolent lymphomas have long natural histories but are rarely cured with standard therapy; the goal is symptom management. Aggressive lymphomas are potentially curable with combination chemotherapy.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for aggressive Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (e.g., DLBCL)?

**A:** Goal is curative. CHOP (cyclophosphamide, hydroxydoxorubicin/adriamycin, vincristine/Oncovin, prednisone) + rituximab (anti-CD20) if B-cell lymphoma (R-CHOP). XRT for localized/bulky disease. CNS prophylaxis with high-dose methotrexate if testicular or >2 extranodal sites involved. Relapse: high-dose chemo + autologous SCT or CAR T-cell therapy.

R-CHOP is the standard of care for DLBCL. Rituximab targets CD20 on B-cells. CAR T-cell therapy (e.g. axicabtagene ciloleucel) is approved for relapsed/refractory DLBCL after ≥2 lines of therapy. 5-year survival ranges from 26-73% based on IPI score.

**Q:** What are the International Prognostic Index (IPI) adverse factors for DLBCL?

**A:** Five adverse factors: (1) age >60 yr, (2) Ann Arbor stage III-IV, (3) performance status ECOG 2-4, (4) elevated LDH, (5) >1 extranodal site. Based on number of factors, 5-year survival ranges from 26-73%. ~40% cure rate overall.

IPI is the standard prognostic tool for aggressive NHL. Low-risk (0-1 factors) has ~73% 5-year OS; high-risk (4-5 factors) has ~26% 5-year OS. Higher-risk patients may warrant more intensive upfront therapy or clinical trial enrollment.

**Q:** What are the distinguishing features of follicular lymphoma versus mantle cell lymphoma?

**A:** Follicular lymphoma: t(14;18) Bcl-2 activation, 22-30% of NHL, indolent, widespread painless LAD + BM involvement, 3% annual risk of transformation to DLBCL, 5-year survival 53-91%. Mantle cell lymphoma: t(11;14) cyclin D1 overexpression, 6% of NHL, indolent but poor prognosis (5-year survival 25%), often stage IV at presentation with GI involvement (lymphomatous polyposis).

Despite both being classified as indolent, mantle cell lymphoma has a significantly worse prognosis than follicular lymphoma. GI involvement (lymphomatous polyposis) and Waldeyer's ring involvement are characteristic of mantle cell lymphoma.

**Q:** What are the features and treatment of Burkitt lymphoma?

**A:** t(8;14) → Myc activation. Very aggressive (<5% adult NHL, 30% childhood NHL). Endemic form: massive jaw LAD, EBV-associated, African origin. 'Starry-sky' histology (macrophages engulfing apoptotic tumour cells). High risk of tumour lysis syndrome (TLS). Treatment: short bursts of intensive chemotherapy (CODOX-M + IVAC with rituximab) + CNS and TLS prophylaxis.

Burkitt lymphoma is one of the fastest-growing human tumours (doubling time ~24 h). The 'starry-sky' pattern reflects high apoptotic rate. Early aggressive hydration and allopurinol/rasburicase are critical to prevent fatal TLS upon treatment initiation.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic features of Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL)?

**A:** Most common leukemia in Western world; median age 70, M>F. CBC: clonal B-lymphocytes >5 × 10⁹/L. Peripheral blood: small mature lymphocytes + smudge cells. Flow cytometry: CD5+, CD20dim, CD23+, light chain restriction. Cytogenetics by FISH for prognosis (e.g. 17p deletion = poor prognosis, p53 mutation).

Smudge cells in CLL result from fragile malignant lymphocytes rupturing during slide preparation. CD5 co-expression with B-cell markers (CD20, CD23) distinguishes CLL from other lymphoproliferative disorders. 17p deletion/TP53 mutation predicts resistance to standard chemoimmunotherapy.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for CLL based on cytogenetic status?

**A:** Observation if early, stable, asymptomatic. For fit patients age <65 without 17p deletion: venetoclax + obinutuzumab (fixed-duration), or FCR (fludarabine + cyclophosphamide + rituximab, now less commonly used). For patients with unmutated IgVH and/or 17p deletion/p53 positivity: ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, or zanubrutinib (BTK inhibitors). Autoimmune phenomena: corticosteroids, rituximab.

17p deletion (involving TP53) confers resistance to alkylating agents and purine analogues, necessitating BTK inhibitor-based therapy. Venetoclax (Bcl-2 inhibitor) with obinutuzumab offers a fixed-duration option. Novel targeted therapies have largely replaced traditional chemoimmunotherapy.

**Q:** What is Richter's transformation in CLL?

**A:** Approximately 5% of CLL patients undergo aggressive transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Associated with chromosomal abnormalities. Presents with rapid enlargement of nodes, systemic symptoms, elevated LDH. Poor prognosis; requires aggressive chemotherapy.

Richter's transformation represents a clonal evolution from indolent CLL to an aggressive lymphoma. It should be suspected when a CLL patient develops rapidly progressive adenopathy, B-symptoms, or markedly elevated LDH. Diagnosis confirmed by lymph node biopsy showing DLBCL morphology.

**Q:** What defines Multiple Myeloma (MM) by the SLiM CRAB criteria?

**A:** SLiM CRAB criteria — any ONE is sufficient for diagnosis: S: ≥60% clonal plasma cells on BM. Li: Light chain ratio (involved/uninvolved) ≥100. M: MRI with >1 bone lesion ≥5mm. CRAB: hyperCalcemia, Renal failure (Cr >176 μmol/L), Anemia, Bone lesions (lytic lesions or osteoporosis with fracture).

SLiM criteria represent biomarkers of malignancy that indicate near-certain progression, justifying treatment even before overt organ damage (CRAB) develops. The updated criteria allow earlier intervention to prevent irreversible end-organ damage.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and complications of Multiple Myeloma?

**A:** Bone disease: back pain, bony tenderness, pathologic fractures, lytic lesions (skull, spine, proximal long bones). Anemia (BM suppression). Infections (S. pneumoniae, Gram-negatives — suppression of normal plasma cells). Hypercalcemia (N/V, confusion, polyuria). Renal failure (cast nephropathy). Hyperviscosity (rare, more common in Waldenstrom's). Amyloidosis (cardiac, GI, neurologic). Neurologic: spinal cord compression (10-20%, medical emergency).

MM causes end-organ damage through multiple mechanisms: osteoclast activation (bone disease), M-protein deposition (renal cast nephropathy, amyloidosis), and marrow infiltration (cytopenias). Bone scans are NOT useful as they detect osteoblast activity; skeletal surveys and MRI/PET are preferred.

**Q:** What investigations confirm Multiple Myeloma and detect M protein?

**A:** CBC: normocytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia; rouleaux on peripheral film. SPEP: M-protein spike in 80%. UPEP: Bence-Jones protein (light chains in urine) in 15% who secrete only light chains. Immunofixation: confirms M protein and identifies Ig type. Serum free light chain quantification (kappa/lambda ratio). BM aspirate/biopsy: >10% plasma cells. Skeletal series (X-ray), MRI, PET. Note: routine urinalysis does NOT detect light chains (dipstick detects albumin only).

Routine urinalysis misses Bence-Jones proteinuria — a critical MCCQE1 teaching point. 24-hour urine collection with UPEP is required. Decreased anion gap can occur due to positively charged M-protein. Elevated β2-microglobulin and LDH with low albumin indicate poor prognosis.

**Q:** How is Multiple Myeloma treated?

**A:** Non-curative. Goals: improve QoL, prevent progression. Transplant-eligible (<70 yr, no comorbidities): 4-6 months cytoreductive therapy (steroid + novel agents: IMIDs or proteasome inhibitors (PI)) followed by autologous SCT. Transplant-ineligible: combinations of prednisone, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib (PI), revlimid (IMID), daratumumab (anti-CD38). Supportive: bisphosphonates (bone lesions), XRT (bone pain, cord compression), kyphoplasty, hydration for hypercalcemia, EPO, DVT prophylaxis. All patients relapse.

Bisphosphonates require renal dose adjustment. Daratumumab (anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody) has become an important component of frontline and relapsed therapy. PIs (bortezomib, carfilzomib) and IMIDs (thalidomide, lenalidomide) are cornerstones of myeloma treatment.

**Q:** What is Monoclonal Gammopathy of Unknown Significance (MGUS) and when does it require treatment?

**A:** MGUS: M protein in serum WITHOUT clinical/lab evidence of plasma cell dyscrasia. Diagnosed by: M protein <30 g/L, <10% plasma cells in BM, absence of SLiM CRAB criteria. Incidence: 5% in people >70 yr. Rate of malignant transformation: 0.3-1% per year. Higher risk if: M protein ≥15 g/L, abnormal free light chain ratio, or IgA/IgM MGUS. Monitoring: history + labs q6-12 months. No treatment required.

MGUS is a pre-malignant condition that does not require treatment but warrants surveillance. Abnormal serum free light chain ratio is an independent risk factor for progression (landmark Blood 2005 study). Patients with high-risk features need more frequent monitoring.

**Q:** How does Multiple Myeloma differ from CLL and Lymphoplasmacytic Lymphoma in a comparison table?

**A:** MM: plasma cells, IgG/A (rarely IgM), rare lymphadenopathy, rare hepatosplenomegaly, COMMON bone lesions + hypercalcemia + renal failure. CLL: lymphocytes, IgM if present, very common LAD + hepatosplenomegaly, rare bone/Ca/renal. LPL: plasmacytoid lymphocytes, IgM, common LAD + hepatosplenomegaly, rare bone/Ca/renal, HYPERVISCOSITY common.

The cell of origin determines the clinical phenotype. MM is a plasma cell disorder causing osteolytic lesions via osteoclast activation. LPL (Waldenstrom's) secretes large IgM molecules that stay intravascular, causing hyperviscosity. CLL is a circulating B-cell malignancy with nodal/splenic involvement.

**Q:** What is Waldenstrom's Macroglobulinemia (Lymphoplasmacytic Lymphoma) and how is it treated?

**A:** LPL/Waldenstrom's: proliferation of lymphoplasmacytoid cells secreting monoclonal IgM. Median age 64 yr. Features: anemia, hyperviscosity (headache, vertigo, visual changes, bleeding), splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, peripheral neuropathy, cold hemagglutinin disease (Raynaud's, hemolytic anemia). Accounts for 85% of hyperviscosity syndrome cases. Treatment: chemotherapy + rituximab (bendamustine + rituximab). Plasmapheresis for acute hyperviscosity. If HCV positive, treat HCV first.

IgM is a large pentameric molecule confined mainly to the intravascular space, making hyperviscosity more common than in MM. Plasmapheresis rapidly reduces IgM levels for acute viscosity crises. Bone lesions and hypercalcemia are rare (unlike MM).

**Q:** What is hyperviscosity syndrome and how is it treated?

**A:** Clinical sequelae of increased blood viscosity (relative serum viscosity >5-6 units). Caused by increased serum Igs (MM, Waldenstrom's) or cellular components (leukemia, polycythemia vera). Waldenstrom's accounts for 85% of cases. Features: CHF (hypervolemia), headache, vertigo, ataxia, stroke (decreased cerebral blood flow), retinal venous engorgement/hemorrhages, bleeding diathesis (impaired platelet function). ESR usually very low (paradoxically). Treatment: plasmapheresis + chemotherapy.

Paradoxically low ESR in hyperviscosity is due to the high protein concentration hindering RBC rouleaux formation needed for sedimentation. Plasmapheresis removes the pathologic Ig and is the acute intervention of choice before definitive chemotherapy.

**Q:** What is Tumour Lysis Syndrome (TLS) and how is it prevented and treated?

**A:** Metabolic complications from spontaneous or treatment-related cancer cell lysis. Common in high-grade lymphoma and acute leukemia (large tumour burden, high proliferative rate). Metabolic abnormalities: elevated K⁺, uric acid, PO₄³⁻; decreased Ca²⁺ (PO₄ binds Ca). Complications: lethal cardiac arrhythmia (hyperkalemia), AKI. Prevention: aggressive IV hydration, allopurinol (prevents uric acid accumulation) or rasburicase (degrades existing uric acid), correct pre-existing metabolic abnormalities. Alkalinization NOT recommended (risk of calcium phosphate/xanthine precipitation). Treatment: dialysis if needed.

TLS prevention is critical before initiating therapy for Burkitt lymphoma or acute leukemia. Rasburicase (recombinant urate oxidase) is preferred for high-risk cases as it actively degrades uric acid, whereas allopurinol only prevents new formation. Rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency.

**Q:** What are the IgM-specific features of Waldenstrom's Macroglobulinemia that make it clinically distinct from Multiple Myeloma?

**A:** IgM is large and pentameric, confined mainly to intravascular space → causes hyperviscosity syndrome (85% of cases). Bone lesions are RARE (unlike MM). Hypercalcemia is RARE. Cold hemagglutinin disease possible (Raynaud's, hemolytic anemia in cold weather). IgM can cause peripheral neuropathy. No lytic bone lesions on skeletal survey. Bone scans and skeletal survey less relevant.

The intravascular confinement of IgM (due to its large size) distinguishes LPL/Waldenstrom's hyperviscosity from MM hyperviscosity. In MM, hyperviscosity is rare because IgG and IgA distribute into extravascular spaces. This difference explains why plasmapheresis is particularly effective in Waldenstrom's.

## Hematology

**Q:** What are the normal values for hemoglobin, MCV, and platelets?

**A:** Hemoglobin: 115-155 g/L (female), 125-170 g/L (male). MCV: 80-100 fL. Platelets: 130-380 × 10⁹/L. RDW: 11.5-15.5%. Reticulocyte count: 23-90 × 10⁹/L (0.5-1.5%).

MCV classifies anemias as microcytic (<80), normocytic (80-100), or macrocytic (>100). RDW measures variation in RBC size (anisocytosis). A high RDW with microcytosis suggests iron deficiency (variable cell sizes), while normal RDW suggests β-thalassemia minor (uniform small cells).

**Q:** How do you use RDW to distinguish iron deficiency anemia from β-thalassemia minor?

**A:** Iron deficiency: increased RDW (anisocytosis) because cells are of varying sizes as the deficiency progresses. β-thalassemia minor: normal RDW with high RBC count, because the red cell abnormality is not progressive and cells are uniformly small.

Both conditions present with microcytic anemia (low MCV). Iron deficiency shows low ferritin, low serum iron, high TIBC, and low transferrin saturation. β-thalassemia minor shows normal/elevated iron studies, elevated HbA2 on hemoglobin electrophoresis, and target cells on smear.

**Q:** What RBC shapes are seen on peripheral smear and what conditions are they associated with?

**A:** Spherocytes: hereditary spherocytosis, AIHA. Schistocytes: MAHA/TMA (TTP, HUS, DIC, HELLP). Sickle cells: HbSS, HbSC. Target cells: liver disease, thalassemia, HbSC. Dacrocytes (teardrops): myelofibrosis. Acanthocytes (spur cells): severe liver disease. Rouleaux: MM, inflammatory conditions.

Peripheral blood film examination is essential for diagnosing hematologic conditions. Schistocytes (helmet cells/fragments) indicate mechanical destruction of RBCs in the microvasculature and should prompt investigation for TMA. Auer rods in WBCs are pathognomonic for AML.

**Q:** What RBC inclusions are pathognomonic or highly associated with specific conditions?

**A:** Howell-Jolly bodies: post-splenectomy/hyposplenic states. Heinz bodies: G6PD deficiency (post-oxidant exposure). Basophilic stippling: lead poisoning, thalassemia. Sideroblasts (ring sideroblasts in BM): sideroblastic anemia, MDS.

Howell-Jolly bodies are nuclear remnants normally removed by the spleen; their presence indicates functional or anatomic asplenia. Heinz bodies are denatured hemoglobin visible with supravital staining. Basophilic stippling represents ribosome aggregation seen with heavy metal poisoning.

**Q:** What is the significance of Auer rods on blood film?

**A:** Auer rods are cytoplasmic inclusions forming long needles in myeloblasts. They are pathognomonic for acute myeloid leukemia (AML). If >20% blasts on WBC differential, this is acute leukemia and constitutes a medical emergency.

Auer rods are fused primary granules composed of crystallized myeloperoxidase. They are never seen in ALL or lymphoid malignancies. Their presence helps distinguish AML from ALL, which is critical for treatment decisions. Multiple Auer rods (faggot cells) are characteristic of APL (M3).

**Q:** What features differentiate AML from ALL?

**A:** AML: adults ('big people'), large blasts with abundant cytoplasm, 3-5 nucleoli, granules, Auer rods, myeloperoxidase/Sudan black stain positive. ALL: children ('small people'), small blasts with scant cytoplasm, 1-3 nucleoli, no granules, no Auer rods, PAS stain positive. Mnemonic: 'Big and SmALL'.

AML has a higher mortality rate in adults, while ALL is the most common childhood cancer with high cure rates (>85%). Treatment differs significantly: AML uses induction with cytarabine + daunorubicin (7+3), while ALL uses multi-agent regimens including vincristine, prednisone, and CNS prophylaxis.

**Q:** What is the Ann Arbor staging system for lymphomas?

**A:** Stage I: single lymph node region or extralymphatic site (IE). Stage II: ≥2 lymph node regions on same side of diaphragm. Stage III: lymph node regions on both sides of diaphragm. Stage IV: diffuse involvement of extralymphatic organs including bone marrow. Suffix A = no B-symptoms; B = B-symptoms present.

B-symptoms include unintentional weight loss ≥10% in 6 months, fever >38°C, and night sweats for ≥2 weeks. The staging system is used for both Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas and is essential for treatment decisions and prognosis.

**Q:** What are the classic features of Hodgkin lymphoma and what cell is diagnostic?

**A:** Reed-Sternberg cells (large binucleated cells with 'owl-eye' appearance) are diagnostic. Presentation: asymptomatic cervical/supraclavicular lymphadenopathy (60-80%), non-tender, rubbery. Bimodal age distribution: peaks at 20 and >50 years. Spreads contiguously from one nodal site to adjacent nodes.

Hodgkin lymphoma is associated with EBV in up to 50% of cases. Treatment is stage-dependent: stage I-II receives ABVD + radiation; stage III-IV receives chemotherapy (ABVD or BEACOPP). Overall prognosis is excellent with >80% cure rate. Treatment complications include secondary malignancies, cardiac disease, and infertility.

**Q:** What are the key chromosome translocations associated with specific lymphomas?

**A:** t(8;14) c-Myc: Burkitt lymphoma. t(14;18) Bcl-2: Follicular lymphoma. t(11;14) Cyclin D1: Mantle cell lymphoma. t(11;18) MALT1: MALT lymphoma. t(2;5) ALK: Anaplastic large cell lymphoma.

These translocations result in overexpression of specific oncogenes that drive lymphomagenesis. Bcl-2 inhibits apoptosis (follicular lymphoma), c-Myc drives cell proliferation (Burkitt), and Cyclin D1 promotes cell cycle progression (mantle cell). These are important for classification and targeted therapy.

**Q:** What is the characteristic finding on peripheral blood smear in CLL, and what are the flow cytometry markers?

**A:** Smear: small mature lymphocytes with smudge cells (damaged fragile lymphocytes). Flow cytometry: CD5+, CD20dim, CD23+, with light chain restriction. Diagnosis requires clonal B lymphocytes >5 × 10⁹/L.

CLL is the most common leukemia in the Western world, predominantly affecting older patients (median age 70). 25% are asymptomatic incidental findings. Richter transformation to DLBCL occurs in 5%. Treatment includes observation for early disease, with venetoclax + obinutuzumab or BTK inhibitors for progressive disease.

**Q:** What is the SLiM CRAB criteria for diagnosing multiple myeloma?

**A:** SLiM: ≥60% clonal plasma cells in BM (Sixty percent), Light chain ratio ≥100 (involved/uninvolved), MRI with >1 bone lesion ≥5mm. CRAB (end-organ damage): Calcium elevated, Renal failure, Anemia, Bone lytic lesions.

Multiple myeloma is the most common plasma cell malignancy. SPEP shows monoclonal protein spike in 80%. 15-20% produce only light chains (Bence-Jones protein in urine). Bone scans are NOT useful as they detect osteoblast activity, while myeloma causes osteolytic lesions. Spinal cord compression (10-20%) is a medical emergency.

**Q:** What defines MGUS and what is the annual risk of malignant transformation?

**A:** MGUS criteria: serum M protein <30 g/L, <10% plasma cells in BM, absence of SLiM CRAB. Annual risk of malignant transformation: 0.3-1% per year. Higher risk with: M protein ≥15 g/L, abnormal free light chain ratio, IgA or IgM type.

MGUS is found in 5% of people >70 years. It is considered pre-malignant and requires monitoring with history, physical, CBC, Cr, calcium, albumin, LDH, and SPEP every 6-12 months. Patients may transform to multiple myeloma, lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, or amyloidosis.

**Q:** What are the key features and complications of Waldenström macroglobulinemia (lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma)?

**A:** Proliferation of lymphoplasmacytoid cells producing monoclonal IgM. Triad: anemia + hyperviscosity + plasma volume expansion. Key complication: hyperviscosity syndrome (headaches, stroke, angina, retinal venous engorgement). Accounts for 85% of all hyperviscosity cases. Treatment: chemotherapy + rituximab; plasmapheresis for acute hyperviscosity.

Unlike myeloma, bone lesions and hypercalcemia are rare in LPL. IgM is large and confined mainly to the intravascular space, making hyperviscosity more common than in myeloma. Cold hemagglutinin disease (Raynaud phenomenon, hemolytic anemia) can be associated.

**Q:** What are the metabolic complications of tumour lysis syndrome?

**A:** Increased: potassium, uric acid, phosphate (released from lysed cells). Decreased: calcium (bound by excess phosphate). Complications: lethal cardiac arrhythmia (hyperkalemia), acute kidney injury (uric acid/calcium phosphate deposition).

TLS is most common in diseases with large tumour burden and high proliferative rate (high-grade lymphoma, acute leukemia). Prevention: aggressive IV hydration, allopurinol (prevents uric acid formation) or rasburicase (breaks down existing uric acid). Alkalinization is NOT recommended due to risk of calcium phosphate precipitation.

**Q:** What is the threshold for RBC transfusion and how much does 1 unit of pRBC increase hemoglobin?

**A:** Transfuse at Hb <70 g/L (may change based on patient tolerance). Maintain Hb 70-90 g/L during active bleeds. Consider higher threshold for CAD/unstable coronary syndromes. 1 unit of packed RBC increases Hb by approximately 10 g/L.

Restrictive transfusion strategy (Hb <70 g/L) is as safe or safer than liberal strategy in most patients. Each unit is infused over 2 hours (max 4 hours). O-negative blood is used for females of reproductive age in emergencies; O-positive for all others.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, and how do you manage it?

**A:** Cause: ABO incompatibility (most commonly due to incorrect patient identification) causing intravascular hemolysis via complement activation. Presentation: fever, chills, hypotension, back/flank pain, hemoglobinuria, DIC, acute renal failure. Management: STOP transfusion, notify blood bank, check for clerical error, send hemolysis labs (CBC, bilirubin, LDH, reticulocytes, DAT), maintain BP with IV fluids, maintain urine output.

Risk is <1/40,000 per unit. This is distinct from febrile nonhemolytic reactions (1/100, due to antibodies to donor WBC/platelets) and allergic reactions (1/100, IgE to donor plasma proteins). TRALI is the leading cause of transfusion-related mortality.

**Q:** What is TRALI and how does it differ from TACO?

**A:** TRALI: Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury — new-onset acute lung injury within 6h of transfusion. Hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 <300), bilateral pulmonary edema, NO left atrial hypertension. Leading cause of transfusion mortality. TACO: Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload — due to impaired cardiac function/rapid transfusion. Dyspnea, hypertension, tachycardia, elevated venous pressure. Treatment: diuretics.

TRALI typically occurs 2-4h post-transfusion and resolves in 24-72h. Treatment is supportive (oxygen). TACO presents with signs of fluid overload and responds to diuretics. TRALI shows normal/low BP while TACO shows hypertension. Both show bilateral pulmonary edema on imaging.

**Q:** What are the mechanisms and monitoring parameters for the major anticoagulant classes?

**A:** UFH: inhibits Factor Xa/IIa via antithrombin, monitor aPTT, reverse with protamine. Warfarin: vitamin K antagonist (II, VII, IX, X), monitor INR (target 2-3), reverse with vitamin K/PCC/FP. LMWH: mainly Factor Xa inhibition, anti-Xa levels (only in renal failure/extremes of weight). DOACs: direct Xa (rivaroxaban, apixaban) or thrombin (dabigatran) inhibitors, no routine monitoring. Dabigatran reversed by idarucizumab; Xa inhibitors by andexanet alfa.

Warfarin is teratogenic and cannot be used in pregnancy; heparin/LMWH is safe. DOACs are contraindicated in mechanical heart valves. LMWH can accumulate in renal failure (CrCl <30). HIT is a potentially fatal complication of heparin requiring immediate discontinuation and alternative anticoagulation.

**Q:** What management is recommended for supratherapeutic INR without bleeding?

**A:** INR above therapeutic but <4.5: lower warfarin dose or omit a dose. INR 4.5-10: omit 1-2 doses ± oral vitamin K 1-2.5 mg if high bleeding risk. INR >10: hold warfarin + oral vitamin K 2.5-5 mg. Any INR with serious/life-threatening bleeding: hold warfarin + IV vitamin K 10 mg slow infusion + four-factor PCC.

Vitamin K takes 6-24 hours to reverse warfarin, while PCC provides immediate factor replacement. FP can be used if PCC is unavailable but requires larger volumes. These guidelines are from the American College of Chest Physicians Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines.

**Q:** What is the CHOP chemotherapy regimen and when is it used?

**A:** CHOP: Cyclophosphamide, Hydroxydoxorubicin (Adriamycin), vincristine (Oncovin), Prednisone. Used as the mainstay for aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Add rituximab (anti-CD20) for B-cell lymphomas = CHOP-R (R-CHOP).

ABVD (adriamycin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) is for Hodgkin lymphoma. BEACOPP is an alternative intensive regimen for advanced Hodgkin lymphoma. For Burkitt lymphoma (highly aggressive), CODOX-M/IVAC with rituximab is used. Treatment of HL depends on stage; treatment of NHL depends on histologic subtype.

**Q:** What are the mechanisms of action of imatinib, rituximab, and venetoclax?

**A:** Imatinib (Gleevec): BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor — used in CML. Rituximab (Rituxan): anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody — used in B-cell lymphomas and CLL. Venetoclax: Bcl-2 inhibitor (promotes apoptosis) — used in CLL.

These represent the shift toward targeted therapy in hematologic malignancies. Imatinib transformed CML from a fatal disease to one with >90% 10-year survival. Rituximab added to CHOP improved outcomes in DLBCL. Venetoclax + obinutuzumab is a fixed-duration treatment for CLL.

**Q:** What are the indications for platelet transfusion based on platelet count?

**A:** <10 × 10⁹/L: non-immune thrombocytopenia (prophylactic). <20: procedures without significant blood loss. <50: procedures with blood loss or major surgery. <100: neurosurgery or head trauma. Any count: platelet dysfunction with marked bleeding. Relative contraindications: TTP, HIT, post-transfusion purpura, HELLP.

One platelet pool should increase count by ≥15 × 10⁹/L. If no increase is seen, consider autoantibodies (ITP), alloantibodies (anti-HLA), consumption (bleeding, sepsis, DIC), or hypersplenism. Platelets are stored at 20-24°C with a short shelf life, unlike RBCs stored at 4°C for up to 42 days.

**Q:** What is the Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (FLIPI)?

**A:** Risk factors: age >60, >4 nodal areas, largest node >6 cm, elevated LDH, Lugano stage III-IV, Hb <120 g/L, high β2-microglobulin, BM involvement. Based on calculated risk, 5-year survival ranges from 53-91%.

Follicular lymphoma is an indolent B-cell NHL (22-30% of NHLs) with t(14;18) Bcl-2 activation. It is rarely curable with a relapsing/remitting course. 3% per year risk of transformation to aggressive lymphoma (DLBCL). Treatment for advanced stage is symptom management with watchful waiting or bendamustine + rituximab.

**Q:** What are the viral infection risks per unit of blood transfusion in Canada?

**A:** HBV: 1/7,000,000. HCV: 1/12,000,000. HIV: 1/20,000,000. HTLV: 1/600,000,000. Other: EBV, CMV, West Nile virus.

Blood product safety has dramatically improved through donor screening (questionnaire and serologic testing) and nucleic acid testing. In Canada, all blood products are leukodepleted by filtration immediately after collection, which reduces febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reactions and CMV transmission risk.

**Q:** What distinguishes Burkitt lymphoma from other NHLs?

**A:** Highly aggressive NHL with t(8;14) c-Myc activation. 'Starry-sky' histology. Endemic form (African, EBV-associated): massive jaw lymphadenopathy. Sporadic form: abdominal/GI involvement. High risk of tumour lysis syndrome upon treatment. Treatment: intensive short-burst chemotherapy (CODOX-M/IVAC + rituximab) with CNS and TLS prophylaxis.

Burkitt lymphoma accounts for <1% of adult NHLs but 30% of childhood NHLs. It is an AIDS-defining illness in the HIV-related form. Despite being highly aggressive, it is very responsive to chemotherapy. The rapid proliferation rate necessitates aggressive TLS prophylaxis before treatment.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of anemia in North America?

**A:** Iron deficiency anemia.

Iron deficiency anemia is the most common cause of anemia in North America. It presents with microcytic hypochromic anemia, low ferritin, low serum iron, elevated TIBC, and low transferrin saturation.

**Q:** A patient with microcytic anemia has a ferritin of 12 ug/L. What is the diagnosis, and what ferritin cutoff is diagnostic?

**A:** Iron deficiency anemia. A ferritin <30 ug/L is diagnostic of iron deficiency. In the setting of inflammatory conditions or liver disease, ferritin <100 ug/L is suggestive and warrants further workup.

Serum ferritin is the most important blood test for measuring iron stores. However, ferritin is also an acute phase reactant, so it can be falsely elevated in inflammation, infection, malignancy, and liver disease.

**Q:** How should oral iron supplementation be dosed for iron deficiency anemia, and why should it NOT be given more than once daily?

**A:** Ferrous sulfate 325 mg once daily (65 mg elemental iron). Dosing more than once daily causes hepcidin upregulation, which decreases absorption and increases side effects without clinical benefit.

Hepcidin is a hormone produced by hepatocytes that regulates systemic iron levels. When iron is absorbed, hepcidin rises and inhibits further iron absorption by degrading ferroportin on enterocytes. Alternate-day dosing is equally effective and may be based on patient preference.

**Q:** How do the iron indices differ between iron deficiency anemia and anemia of chronic disease?

**A:** Iron deficiency: low ferritin, low serum iron, high TIBC, low % saturation, high RDW. Anemia of chronic disease: normal/high ferritin, low serum iron, low/normal TIBC, normal % saturation, normal RDW.

In anemia of chronic disease, hepcidin production is increased by inflammation, trapping iron in enterocytes and macrophages via ferroportin inhibition. This reduces plasma iron levels, making iron unavailable for new Hb synthesis despite adequate stores.

**Q:** What is the role of hepcidin in iron metabolism and anemia of chronic disease?

**A:** Hepcidin binds to ferroportin (iron exporter on enterocytes and reticuloendothelial cells), inducing its degradation. This traps iron intracellularly, inhibiting iron export into circulation. In inflammation, hepcidin is upregulated, mediating anemia of chronic disease through iron trapping.

Hepcidin production is increased in iron overload and inflammation (causing iron trapping) and decreased in states of increased erythropoiesis or low oxygen tension (allowing more iron absorption).

**Q:** What are the iron indices and blood film findings in sideroblastic anemia?

**A:** Normal/high ferritin, high serum iron, normal TIBC, normal/high % saturation, high RDW. Blood film shows dual population of RBCs and basophilic stippling. BM biopsy shows ring sideroblasts (iron deposits in mitochondria surrounding the nucleus).

Sideroblastic anemia results from defects in heme biosynthesis in erythroid precursors. Causes include hereditary (X-linked), MDS (refractory anemia with ringed sideroblasts), and reversible causes such as isoniazid, alcohol, lead, and copper deficiency.

**Q:** What mnemonic helps remember the causes of microcytic anemia?

**A:** TAILS: Thalassemia, Anemia of chronic disease/inflammation, Iron deficiency, Lead poisoning, Sideroblastic anemia.

Microcytic anemia is defined as MCV <80 fL. The iron indices and blood film findings differ among these causes, and ferritin is the most important initial test to differentiate them.

**Q:** What clinical features constitute Plummer-Vinson syndrome?

**A:** Dysphagia (esophageal web), glossitis, iron deficiency anemia, and stomatitis.

Plummer-Vinson syndrome is a classic triad of iron deficiency anemia, esophageal webs causing dysphagia, and glossitis. It is considered a premalignant condition with increased risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.

**Q:** How does beta-thalassemia minor differ from iron deficiency anemia on CBC and iron studies?

**A:** Beta-thalassemia minor: MCV very low (<70 fL) but anemia is mild (Hb 100-140 g/L), normal/high RBC count, normal iron studies, and elevated HbA2 (3.5-5%) on Hb electrophoresis. Iron deficiency: proportionally low Hb and MCV, low ferritin, high TIBC.

In beta-thalassemia minor, the microcytosis is disproportionately more profound relative to the mild anemia, which is the opposite pattern from iron deficiency anemia. Normal iron studies help distinguish the two.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and clinical presentation of beta-thalassemia major?

**A:** Homozygous defect in both beta-globin alleles causing absent/severely reduced beta-chain synthesis, severe anemia (Hb <60 g/L), ineffective erythropoiesis, and hemolysis. Presents at 6-12 months of age (when HbF normally switches to HbA). Features include severe anemia, jaundice, iron overload, hepatosplenomegaly (extramedullary hematopoiesis), skeletal changes (hair-on-end skull x-ray), and growth retardation.

Treatment includes lifelong regular RBC transfusions, iron chelation therapy (deferoxamine, deferasirox, deferiprone), folic acid, and potentially curative allogeneic BM transplant or gene therapy. Death results from untreated anemia, infection, or iron overload.

**Q:** In alpha-thalassemia, what are the clinical consequences of 1, 2, 3, and 4 defective alpha genes?

**A:** 1 gene (aa/a-): clinically silent, normal Hb and MCV. 2 genes (cis or trans): normal Hb, decreased MCV. 3 genes (a-/--): HbH disease (beta-4 tetramers), decreased Hb and MCV, splenomegaly. 4 genes (--/--): Hb Barts (gamma-4), hydrops fetalis, usually incompatible with life.

Cis 2-gene deletion (aa/--) is more common in Southeast Asia vs. trans (a-/a-) in Africa. This means offspring of Southeast Asian patients are at higher risk of fetal hydrops (if both parents carry cis deletions, offspring can inherit 4-gene deletion).

**Q:** What is the genetic defect in sickle cell disease, and what factors aggravate sickling?

**A:** Autosomal recessive Glu-to-Val substitution at position 6 of the beta-globin chain (chromosome 11), producing HbS. Sickling is aggravated by low pO2, acidemia, increased CO2, increased 2,3-DPG, fever, and increased osmolality.

At low pO2, deoxyHbS polymerizes forming rigid crystal-like rods that distort membranes into sickle shapes. Fragile sickle cells then cause hemolysis (nitric oxide depletion) and occlusion of small vessels (ischemia-reperfusion injury).

**Q:** What are the four types of sickle cell crises?

**A:** 1. Aplastic crisis: BM suppression (especially parvovirus B19). 2. Splenic sequestration crisis: pooling of blood in spleen with acute Hb drop and shock (mainly children). 3. Vaso-occlusive crisis: microcirculation infarction causing pain in back, chest, abdomen, extremities. 4. Acute chest syndrome: fever, respiratory symptoms, new pulmonary infiltrate on CXR.

Vaso-occlusive crises are the most common presentation. Acute chest syndrome is precipitated by pulmonary infection, fat emboli, and pulmonary infarction. It is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in SCD.

**Q:** How does hydroxyurea work in sickle cell disease, and what are its key benefits?

**A:** Hydroxyurea stops repression of the fetal Hb-gamma gene, increasing production of HbF. HbF decreases polymerization and precipitation of HbS in sickle cell RBCs. Benefits include decreased frequency of pain crises, acute chest syndrome, hospitalizations, and need for blood transfusions.

Short-term harms include dose-related leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, decreased reticulocyte count, decreased sperm production, and mucositis. Long-term harms potentially include birth defects, growth delays in children, and cancer risk.

**Q:** What organisms are sickle cell patients particularly susceptible to, and why?

**A:** Encapsulated organisms: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae type b. Also Salmonella (osteomyelitis). This is due to functional asplenia from repeated splenic infarction.

The spleen is enlarged in childhood but becomes atrophic in adulthood due to repeated infarction (autosplenectomy). Prevention includes vaccination and prophylactic penicillin from age 3 months to 5 years.

**Q:** What are the screening tests and specific tests for hemolytic anemia?

**A:** Screening: increased LDH, decreased haptoglobin, increased unconjugated bilirubin, increased urobilinogen, reticulocytosis. Intravascular-specific: schistocytes on blood film, free Hb in serum, hemoglobinuria, hemosiderinuria (most sensitive). Extravascular-specific: positive direct antiglobulin test (direct Coombs).

Haptoglobin is a circulating protein that binds free Hb for clearance by the spleen. When free Hb is abundant (intravascular hemolysis), haptoglobin is consumed and levels decrease. LDH is released from lysed RBCs.

**Q:** What is the difference between direct and indirect Coombs (antiglobulin) tests?

**A:** Direct Coombs (DAT): detects IgG or complement already bound on the surface of patient's RBCs. Add anti-IgG/anti-complement Ab to patient's RBCs; positive if agglutination. Indirect Coombs: detects free antibodies in patient's serum that can recognize RBC antigens. Mix patient's serum with donor RBCs, then add Coombs serum.

DAT is used for autoimmune hemolytic anemia, hemolytic disease of the newborn, and hemolytic transfusion reactions. Indirect Coombs is used for cross-matching, detecting atypical blood group antibodies, and prenatal screening.

**Q:** How do warm and cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) differ in antibody type, etiology, and treatment?

**A:** Warm AIHA (75-90%): IgG antibodies, agglutination at 37C, DAT positive for IgG + complement. Secondary to CLL, Hodgkin lymphoma, SLE, drugs. Treat with corticosteroids (1st-line), rituximab, splenectomy. Cold AIHA: IgM antibodies, agglutination at <37C, DAT positive for complement only. Secondary to Mycoplasma, EBV, HCV, CLL. Treat by warming patient/avoiding cold, rituximab (1st-line).

Warm AIHA shows spherocytes on blood film (IgG-coated RBCs partially phagocytosed by splenic macrophages). Cold AIHA shows agglutination on blood film. Drug-induced AIHA (e.g., penicillin, quinine, methyldopa) is a subtype of warm AIHA.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and clinical presentation of hereditary spherocytosis?

**A:** Abnormality in RBC membrane proteins (e.g., spectrin), usually autosomal dominant. Membrane loss results in spherocytic RBCs that are trapped and destroyed in the spleen. Presents with hemolytic anemia, jaundice, and splenomegaly. MCHC is elevated. Osmotic fragility is increased.

Hereditary spherocytosis is the most common type of hereditary hemolytic anemia. Treatment includes genetic counselling and splenectomy in severe cases (avoid in early childhood). Pre-splenectomy vaccinations against encapsulated organisms are mandatory.

**Q:** What triggers hemolytic episodes in G6PD deficiency, and what blood film findings are characteristic?

**A:** Triggers: oxidative stress, drugs (sulfonamides, antimalarials, nitrofurantoin), infection, and fava beans. Blood film shows Heinz bodies (denatured Hb clumps attached to RBC membrane) and bite cells (from splenic passage).

G6PD deficiency is X-linked recessive, prevalent in African, Asian, and Mediterranean populations. G6PD assay should NOT be done during acute crisis when reticulocyte count is high (reticulocytes have high G6PD levels, giving false-normal results).

**Q:** What distinguishes megaloblastic from non-megaloblastic macrocytic anemia?

**A:** Megaloblastic: large oval nucleated RBC precursors with hypersegmented neutrophils, due to failure of DNA synthesis (B12 or folate deficiency). Non-megaloblastic: large round RBCs without hypersegmented neutrophils, due to membrane abnormality (alcohol/liver disease, hypothyroidism, reticulocytosis, drugs, MDS).

The mnemonic ABCDEF covers macrocytic anemia causes: Alcoholism/liver disease, B12 deficiency, Compensatory reticulocytosis, Drugs/Dysplasia, Endocrine (hypothyroidism), Folate deficiency/Fetus (pregnancy).

**Q:** What neurological findings are specific to vitamin B12 deficiency (not folate deficiency)?

**A:** Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord: posterior columns (decreased vibration, proprioception, 2-point discrimination, paresthesia) and pyramidal tracts (spastic weakness, ataxia). Also peripheral neuropathy and cerebral effects (confusion, delirium, dementia). Spinal cord damage is irreversible.

B12 is absorbed in the terminal ileum after binding to intrinsic factor from gastric parietal cells. Total body stores last 3-4 years. Pernicious anemia (autoantibodies against IF or parietal cells) is the classic cause. Folate deficiency does NOT cause neurological manifestations.

**Q:** What is pernicious anemia and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Auto-antibodies against intrinsic factor or gastric parietal cells, leading to achlorhydria and lack of IF secretion, causing decreased ileal B12 absorption. Diagnosis: anti-parietal cell Ab (more sensitive), anti-intrinsic factor Ab (more specific), elevated gastrin level, upper endoscopy showing atrophic gastritis.

Pernicious anemia is most common in Northern European populations, usually >30 years old (median age 60). It may be associated with other autoimmune disorders (polyglandular endocrine insufficiency). Upper endoscopy also screens for gastric malignancy.

**Q:** What precaution must be taken before treating suspected folate deficiency with folic acid supplementation?

**A:** Always rule out concurrent vitamin B12 deficiency first. Supplementing folate alone in a B12-deficient patient will correct the anemia but allow neurological damage (subacute combined degeneration) to continue and potentially worsen.

Lower serum B12 leads to low RBC folate because absence of B12 results in folate polyglutamate synthesis failure. Both elevated methylmalonate and homocysteine can help confirm B12 deficiency when levels are borderline.

**Q:** What are the four stages of hemostasis?

**A:** 1. Primary hemostasis: platelet plug formation via VWF-mediated platelet adhesion, activation, and aggregation. 2. Secondary hemostasis: fibrin clot reinforces platelet plug via coagulation cascade (extrinsic and intrinsic pathways converge on common pathway). 3. Fibrin stabilization: cross-linking of fibrin clot (Factor XIII). 4. Fibrinolysis: clot dissolution mediated by plasmin.

Disorders of primary hemostasis present with immediate mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae, ecchymoses, epistaxis). Disorders of secondary hemostasis present with delayed deep tissue bleeding (hemarthroses, hematomas).

**Q:** What does PT/INR measure versus PTT, and what mnemonic helps remember?

**A:** PT/INR measures the extrinsic pathway (Factor VII) and common pathway. PTT measures the intrinsic pathway (Factors VIII, IX, XI, XII) and common pathway. Mnemonic: PT = Tennis played outside (Extrinsic); PTT = Table Tennis played inside (Intrinsic).

PT/INR is used to monitor warfarin therapy and assess hepatic function. PTT is used to monitor heparin and argatroban therapy. Both are prolonged in common pathway deficiencies (Factors II, V, X, fibrinogen), DIC, and severe liver disease.

**Q:** What are the lab value patterns for hemophilia A/B, VWD, DIC, liver failure, and ITP?

**A:** Hemophilia A/B: normal PT, prolonged PTT, normal platelets. VWD: normal PT, prolonged/normal PTT, normal/low platelets. DIC: prolonged PT, prolonged PTT, low platelets, low Hb. Liver failure: prolonged PT, normal/prolonged PTT, normal/low platelets. ITP: normal PT, normal PTT, low platelets.

These patterns are high-yield for MCCQE1. In DIC, fibrinogen is also decreased and D-dimers/FDPs are elevated. In liver disease, all factors except VIII are decreased (VIII is also produced by endothelium).

**Q:** What is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, and how does it present?

**A:** Von Willebrand disease (prevalence 1%). Presents with mucocutaneous bleeding: easy bruising, epistaxis (>10 min), heavy menstrual bleeding, post-dental extraction bleeding, peripartum bleeding, excessive postoperative bleeding, and GI bleeding.

VWF mediates platelet adhesion/aggregation and acts as a chaperone for Factor VIII. Type 1 (80% of cases) is a mild quantitative deficiency. Type 2 is a qualitative defect. Type 3 is a severe total deficiency. Usually autosomal dominant (types 2N and 3 are autosomal recessive).

**Q:** How is von Willebrand disease treated?

**A:** DDAVP (desmopressin) is effective for 85-90% of type 1 VWD and some type 2 subtypes (causes release of VWF and Factor VIII from endothelial cells). Tranexamic acid as adjunct. VWF:Factor VIII concentrate (Humate P) if DDAVP unresponsive or for severe bleeding.

DDAVP requires a documented 'DDAVP challenge' to confirm responsiveness. Tachyphylaxis occurs after 4 consecutive doses. Caution in children due to risk of hyponatremia. VWF levels are lowest in blood group O patients.

**Q:** A male patient has prolonged PTT, normal PT, normal platelets, and a history of recurrent hemarthroses. What is the most likely diagnosis and how is it confirmed?

**A:** Hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency) or Hemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency). Confirm by measuring specific factor levels. Hemophilia A: decreased Factor VIII (<40%). Hemophilia B: decreased Factor IX. Also check VWF antigen and ristocetin activity to rule out VWD.

Hemophilia A is X-linked recessive, affecting 1 in 5000 males. Hemophilia B is X-linked recessive, 1 in 30000 males. Severity: mild >5% factor level, moderate 1-5%, severe <1%. Deep bleeding (hemarthroses, hematomas) is characteristic of secondary hemostasis disorders.

**Q:** What is immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and what is the first-line treatment in adults?

**A:** ITP is an acquired immune-mediated disorder with anti-platelet antibodies causing increased platelet destruction and impaired production. Platelets <100 x 10^9/L with normal PT/PTT. First-line treatment: corticosteroids (dexamethasone 40 mg PO daily x 4 days or prednisone 0.5-2 mg/kg/day). IVIg 1 g/kg if faster rise needed.

Treatment is rarely indicated if platelets >30 x 10^9/L unless active bleeding. Second-line options include splenectomy (not within 12 months of diagnosis), TPO receptor agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag), and rituximab. Blood film shows decreased platelets with large platelets.

**Q:** What is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and what is the 4T scoring system?

**A:** HIT is an immune-mediated reaction where antibodies recognize heparin-platelet factor 4 complexes, causing platelet activation and coagulation. 4Ts: Thrombocytopenia (degree of platelet fall), Timing (5-14 days after heparin exposure), Thrombosis (new thrombosis or skin necrosis), oTher causes (alternative explanations). Score 6-8 = high probability.

HIT has 30-50% risk of thrombosis if untreated. Management: immediately discontinue all heparin (including flushes), start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, danaparoid, fondaparinux). Warfarin should not be started until platelets >150 x 10^9/L. DOACs can be started before platelet recovery.

**Q:** How does TTP differ from HUS in etiology, clinical features, and treatment?

**A:** TTP: ADAMTS13 deficiency (acquired autoantibodies or congenital), presents with thrombocytopenia, MAHA/TMA, neurological symptoms. Treatment: plasma exchange + steroids (mortality 90% if untreated). HUS: Shiga toxin E. coli O157:H7 (90%), presents with thrombocytopenia, MAHA/TMA, acute kidney injury, bloody diarrhea. Treatment: supportive (fluids, transfusion).

Both show normal PT/PTT/fibrinogen (unlike DIC), schistocytes on blood film, decreased platelets, and hemolysis markers. Platelet transfusion is avoided in TTP unless life-threatening bleeding. ADAMTS13 normally cleaves ultra-large VWF multimers; without it, uncleaved VWF promotes excessive platelet aggregation.

**Q:** What is DIC, what are its key etiologies (mnemonic), and what are the lab findings?

**A:** DIC is excessive dysregulated release of plasmin and thrombin leading to simultaneous intravascular coagulation and fibrinolysis. Mnemonic OMITS: Obstetric complications, Malignancy, Infection, Shock. Labs: prolonged PT/PTT, decreased platelets, decreased fibrinogen, increased D-dimers/FDPs, schistocytes on blood film.

DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy where patients can present with both hemorrhage AND thrombosis. Treatment focuses on the underlying cause. Bleeding phase: platelet transfusion if <50 with bleeding, FP if prolonged PT/PTT, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen <1.5 g/L.

**Q:** What are the components of Virchow's triad and the key risk factors for venous thromboembolism?

**A:** Virchow's triad: endothelial damage, venous stasis, and hypercoagulability. Key risk factors: surgery (especially orthopedic), immobilization, trauma, malignancy (pancreas, lung, stomach), pregnancy, OCP/HRT, obesity, previous VTE, age >60, heart failure, and inherited thrombophilia.

DVT most commonly originates in calf veins and propagates proximally. The most important sequelae are PE (up to 50% chance with proximal DVT) and post-thrombotic syndrome. 10-20% of idiopathic VTE cases are later found to have cancer.

**Q:** How is DVT diagnosed using the Modified Wells Score and D-dimer?

**A:** Modified Wells Score stratifies pre-test probability: >=2 points = DVT likely, <2 = DVT unlikely. D-dimer is only useful to rule out DVT if negative with low clinical suspicion (Wells <2). A positive D-dimer is non-specific. Doppler ultrasound is the most useful diagnostic test (sensitivity/specificity ~95% for proximal DVT).

Wells Score components include paralysis/casting (+1), bedridden >3 days or major surgery (+1), calf swelling >3 cm (+1), pitting edema (+1), collateral veins (+1), active cancer (+1), entire leg swelling (+1), tenderness along deep veins (+1), and alternative diagnosis more likely (-2).

**Q:** What are the initial anticoagulation options for treatment of VTE?

**A:** DOACs: apixaban or rivaroxaban alone (with loading dose); dabigatran or edoxaban require 5-10 days of parenteral anticoagulation (LMWH) first. LMWH: weight-based SC dosing. UFH: for high bleed risk or need for rapid interruption. Warfarin: must overlap with heparin for at least 48 hours with INR >2.

Duration: provoked VTE with transient risk factor = 3 months. First unprovoked VTE: at least 3 months, consider indefinite. Cancer-associated VTE: indefinite therapy while active malignancy. DOACs are contraindicated in pregnancy and with mechanical heart valves.

**Q:** Why must warfarin be overlapped with heparin when initiating treatment for VTE?

**A:** Warfarin initially creates a prothrombotic state because it inhibits natural anticoagulants protein C and S (which have shorter half-lives than procoagulant factors II, X). Without heparin overlap, this can cause warfarin-induced skin necrosis. Heparin overlap for at least 48 hours with INR >2 is required.

Warfarin decreases Factor VII levels first (shortest half-life), causing INR prolongation, but full antithrombotic effect is not achieved until Factors IX, X, and II are sufficiently reduced (after approximately 4 days). One-third of patients with warfarin-induced skin necrosis have underlying protein C deficiency.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of hereditary thrombophilia?

**A:** Factor V Leiden (activated protein C resistance). A point mutation (R506Q) in the Factor V gene results in resistance to inactivation of Factor Va by activated protein C. Present in 3-7% of European White population (heterozygotes).

Other hereditary thrombophilias include prothrombin gene mutation G20210A (1-3% European Whites), protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, and antithrombin deficiency. Testing should only be done if it will alter management decisions.

**Q:** What are the clinical and laboratory criteria for antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS)?

**A:** Clinical: arterial or venous thrombosis, or pregnancy morbidity (recurrent early losses <10 wk, late fetal loss >=10 wk, or premature birth <34 wk due to preeclampsia/placental insufficiency). Laboratory (confirmed on 2 occasions >=12 wk apart): anticardiolipin IgG/IgM, anti-beta2 glycoprotein-I Ab, or lupus anticoagulant.

Despite its name, lupus anticoagulant is actually prothrombotic in vivo (it prolongs PTT in vitro but causes thrombosis clinically). APS can cause both arterial and venous thrombosis. Management includes long-term anticoagulation.

**Q:** What vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors does warfarin affect, and what mnemonic helps remember them?

**A:** Factors II, VII, IX, X, and proteins C and S. Mnemonic: '1972' (factors X, IX, VII, II) plus proteins C and S.

Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase, preventing regeneration of active vitamin K needed for gamma-carboxylation of these factors. Factor VII has the shortest half-life, so PT/INR rises first. Full antithrombotic effect requires reduction of all affected factors.

**Q:** How do you manage a supratherapeutic INR >10 without bleeding versus any INR with serious/life-threatening bleeding?

**A:** INR >10, no bleeding: hold warfarin, give oral vitamin K 2.5-5 mg, monitor INR frequently, resume at lower dose. Any INR with serious bleeding: hold warfarin, give IV vitamin K 10 mg slow infusion, plus four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC), monitor and repeat as needed.

PCC (Octaplex, Beriplex) provides rapid reversal by supplying vitamin K-dependent factors directly. FP can be used if PCC is not available. PCCs are relatively contraindicated in liver disease or history of HIT (contains heparin).

**Q:** What are the reversal agents for each class of anticoagulant?

**A:** UFH: protamine sulfate. LMWH: partial reversal with protamine sulfate. Warfarin: vitamin K, PCC, or FP. Dabigatran: idarucizumab (Praxbind). Rivaroxaban/apixaban/edoxaban: andexanet alfa. Fondaparinux and argatroban: no specific reversal agent.

Idarucizumab completely reverses dabigatran's anticoagulant effect within minutes (RE-VERSE AD trial). Andexanet alfa is a modified Factor Xa decoy that binds direct Xa inhibitors. These reversal agents are critical for managing life-threatening bleeding.

**Q:** What differentiates AML from ALL clinically and on lab investigation?

**A:** AML: adults (median 65 yr), large blasts with lots of cytoplasm, 3-5 nucleoli, granules and Auer rods (pathognomonic), myeloperoxidase/Sudan black stain positive. ALL: children, small blasts with less cytoplasm, 1-3 nucleoli, no granules/Auer rods, PAS stain positive. Mnemonic: Big (AML) vs. SmALL.

AML accounts for 80% of acute adult leukemias. ALL is the most common childhood malignancy. Both present with manifestations of BM failure (anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia) and can cause hepatosplenomegaly.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) and why is it a medical emergency?

**A:** APL is a medical emergency because DIC is often present at diagnosis. Treatment: ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid) to induce differentiation, started ASAP if APL is suspected. ATRA + arsenic trioxide combination is superior to traditional chemotherapy. Supportive transfusions: target platelets >30 (no bleeding) or >50 (bleeding), fibrinogen >1.5.

APL has the t(15;17) translocation involving the PML-RARA gene fusion. Despite being a medical emergency at presentation, APL has one of the best prognoses among AML subtypes when properly treated with ATRA-based regimens.

**Q:** What is Hodgkin lymphoma and what are its hallmark pathological and clinical features?

**A:** Malignant proliferation of lymphoid cells with Reed-Sternberg cells (hallmark). Bimodal age distribution (20 yr and >50 yr). Associated with EBV in up to 50%. Presents with painless rubbery cervical/supraclavicular lymphadenopathy (60-80%), mediastinal mass, splenomegaly, B-symptoms, and pruritus. Spreads contiguously through lymphatic system.

Hodgkin lymphoma is differentiated from non-Hodgkin lymphoma by the presence of Reed-Sternberg cells. Diagnosis requires excisional lymph node or core biopsy. Treatment depends on stage: early stage = ABVD + radiotherapy; advanced = ABVD or BEACOPP.

**Q:** What does ABVD stand for and what are the major treatment complications of Hodgkin lymphoma therapy?

**A:** ABVD: Adriamycin, Bleomycin, Vinblastine, Dacarbazine. Complications: cardiac disease (adriamycin cardiomyopathy), pulmonary disease (bleomycin interstitial pneumonitis), infertility (<3% with ABVD), secondary malignancy (solid tumours >8 yr post-treatment, NHL), and hypothyroidism (post-radiation).

Pre-treatment cardiac assessment (MUGA scan/echocardiography) is important for patients at high risk. PFTs are done if history of lung disease. PET scan results are essential in assessing disease response.

**Q:** What are the key chromosome translocations associated with lymphomas?

**A:** t(8;14): c-Myc activation = Burkitt lymphoma. t(14;18): Bcl-2 activation = follicular lymphoma. t(11;14): cyclin D1 overexpression = mantle cell lymphoma. t(2;5): ALK mutation = anaplastic large cell lymphoma. t(11;18): MALT1 activation = MALT lymphoma.

These translocations are important for diagnosis and classification. Burkitt lymphoma has a 'starry-sky' histology pattern and is highly aggressive but very responsive to chemoradiation. Follicular lymphoma is the most common indolent NHL.

**Q:** What is the treatment difference between Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma?

**A:** Hodgkin lymphoma: treatment depends on stage (ABVD +/- radiotherapy for early; ABVD/BEACOPP for advanced). Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: treatment depends on histologic subtype. Indolent NHL: watchful waiting, rituximab-based therapy. Aggressive NHL (DLBCL): CHOP + rituximab (CHOP-R), curative intent. Highly aggressive (Burkitt): intensive chemotherapy (CODOX-M/IVAC + rituximab).

Rituximab (anti-CD20 antibody) is added to all B-cell NHL treatment regimens. The goal for aggressive NHL is cure (~40% cure rate for DLBCL), while for indolent NHL the goal is symptom management as it is rarely curable.

**Q:** What is chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and what are its characteristic lab findings?

**A:** Most common leukemia in the Western world. Indolent clonal malignancy of mature B-cells. Median age 70, M>F. CBC: clonal B lymphocytes >5 x 10^9/L. Blood film: small mature lymphocytes and smudge cells. Flow cytometry: CD5, CD20dim, CD23, light chain restriction.

25% are asymptomatic (incidental finding). Complications include autoimmune hemolytic anemia (DAT positive), ITP, hypogammaglobulinemia, and Richter's transformation (5% progress to aggressive DLBCL). Treatment is often observation if early/stable/asymptomatic.

**Q:** What are the major clinical complications of multiple myeloma?

**A:** Bone disease (lytic lesions, pathologic fractures, back pain), anemia (BM suppression), recurrent infections (S. pneumoniae, Gram-negatives due to suppressed plasma cell function), hypercalcemia, renal failure (cast nephropathy), bleeding, hyperviscosity, amyloidosis, and spinal cord compression (medical emergency).

Lytic lesions are classic (skull, spine, proximal long bones, ribs) due to osteoclast activating factors. Bone scans are NOT useful (they detect osteoblast activity, which is not increased). Rouleaux formation on peripheral blood film is characteristic.

**Q:** What is MGUS and what is the risk of malignant transformation?

**A:** Monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance: serum M protein <30 g/L, <10% plasma cells in BM, absence of SLiM CRAB. Incidence: 5% of people >70 yr. Risk of malignant transformation: 0.3-1% per year.

Higher risk of transformation with M protein >=15 g/L, abnormal free light chain ratio, or IgA/IgM type MGUS. Monitoring: history every 6-12 months, physical, CBC, Cr, calcium, albumin, LDH, and SPEP. MGUS is considered pre-malignant.

**Q:** What is the general threshold for packed RBC transfusion, and what does 1 unit raise Hb by?

**A:** Transfuse when Hb <70 g/L; maintain between 70-90 g/L during active bleeds. Consider higher threshold for CAD/unstable coronary syndromes. One unit of packed RBC increases Hb by approximately 10 g/L.

The Choosing Wisely recommendation states: do not transfuse more than the minimum number of RBC units necessary to relieve symptoms or return the patient to a safe Hb range (70-80 g/L) in stable non-cardiac patients. Both the TRICC and FOCUS trials support restrictive transfusion strategies.

**Q:** What are the platelet count thresholds for platelet transfusion?

**A:** <10 x 10^9/L: non-immune thrombocytopenia. <20: procedures without significant blood loss. <50: procedures with blood loss or major surgery. <100: pre-neurosurgery or head trauma. Any count: platelet dysfunction with marked bleeding.

Relative contraindications to platelet transfusion include TTP, HIT, post-transfusion purpura, and HELLP. One platelet pool should increase count by >=15 x 10^9/L. If no rise: consider autoantibodies (ITP), alloantibodies (anti-HLA), consumption (bleeding, sepsis, DIC), or hypersplenism.

**Q:** What is the most common and most severe acute blood transfusion reaction, and how are they managed?

**A:** Most common: febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction (1/100) - due to alloantibodies to WBC/platelets; rule out hemolytic reaction, give antipyretics. Most severe: acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (<1/40000) - ABO incompatibility, most commonly due to incorrect patient identification. Management: STOP transfusion, notify blood bank, check for clerical error, maintain BP with IV fluids, maintain urine output.

TRALI (transfusion-related acute lung injury) is currently the leading cause of transfusion-related morbidity and mortality. It presents with hypoxemia and bilateral pulmonary edema within 6 hours, typically resolving in 24-72 hours with supportive care.

**Q:** What are the indications for irradiated blood products?

**A:** Irradiated blood products prevent proliferation of donor T-cells in recipients at risk of transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GVHD). Indications: severe T-cell immunodeficiency, purine analogue chemotherapy, Hodgkin lymphoma, BM transplant candidates, directed donations from first-degree relatives, HLA-matched products, and intrauterine transfusions.

TA-GVHD occurs 4-30 days post-transfusion when donor T-lymphocytes recognize and attack the immunocompromised host. It presents with fever, diarrhea, liver dysfunction, and pancytopenia. It is highly fatal and can be prevented with irradiation.

**Q:** What is tumour lysis syndrome (TLS), what metabolic abnormalities occur, and how is it prevented?

**A:** TLS results from breakdown of cancer cells (spontaneous or treatment-related), most common in high tumour burden with high proliferative rate (high-grade lymphoma, acute leukemia). Metabolic abnormalities: increased K+, uric acid, PO4; decreased Ca2+ (PO4 binds Ca). Prevention: aggressive IV hydration, allopurinol (prevents uric acid accumulation) or rasburicase (lowers existing uric acid).

Complications include lethal cardiac arrhythmia (hyperkalemia) and acute kidney injury (uric acid and calcium phosphate crystal deposition). Alkalinization is NOT recommended due to risk of calcium phosphate or xanthine precipitation. Dialysis may be needed.

**Q:** How does aspirin differ from clopidogrel in mechanism of antiplatelet action?

**A:** Aspirin: irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase (COX), inhibiting thromboxane A2 (TXA2) synthesis, thus inhibiting platelet aggregation. Effect lasts 7-10 days. Clopidogrel: irreversibly inhibits ADP binding to platelets (P2Y12 receptor), decreasing platelet aggregation. It is a prodrug requiring two-step activation; CYP2C19 poor metabolizers have diminished response.

Prasugrel and ticagrelor are alternatives to clopidogrel with higher potency. Ticagrelor reversibly inhibits ADP binding and does not need metabolic activation. GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors (abciximab, eptifibatide) block the final common pathway of platelet aggregation.

**Q:** How do unfractionated heparin (UFH) and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) differ in mechanism, monitoring, and reversal?

**A:** UFH: inhibits Factor Xa and IIa via antithrombin; IV/SC; monitor with aPTT; fully reversed by protamine sulfate; higher risk of HIT. LMWH: mainly Factor Xa inhibition via antithrombin; SC; no routine monitoring needed (anti-Xa levels in special populations); only partially reversed by protamine; lower HIT risk; accumulates in renal failure (CrCl <30).

LMWH has higher bioavailability, predictable dose response, fixed dosing, and is standard treatment for VTE in pregnancy and cancer. UFH is preferred when rapid interruption is needed (high bleed risk, surgical procedures) because it is rapidly and fully reversible.

**Q:** What are the key differences among DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban)?

**A:** Rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban: direct Factor Xa inhibitors; reversed by andexanet alfa. Dabigatran: direct thrombin (IIa) inhibitor; reversed by idarucizumab. All are oral, contraindicated in mechanical heart valves. Dabigatran/edoxaban require initial parenteral anticoagulation for VTE. Rivaroxaban must be taken with food. All require adequate renal function (varying CrCl cutoffs).

DOACs do not require routine monitoring (unlike warfarin). Anti-Xa levels can detect presence of Xa inhibitors; dilute thrombin time can detect dabigatran. DOACs are now first-line for VTE and stroke prevention in non-valvular atrial fibrillation.

**Q:** What is the CRASH-2 trial's key finding about tranexamic acid in trauma?

**A:** Early administration of tranexamic acid (TXA) significantly reduced all-cause mortality (14.5% vs 16.0%, p=0.0035) and death from bleeding (4.9% vs 5.7%) in bleeding trauma patients. Benefit was greatest with immediate treatment (70% improvement). Beyond 3 hours of injury, TXA was likely ineffective and may increase mortality from bleeding.

TXA is an antifibrinolytic agent that stabilizes clot formation. The key clinical message is that TXA must be given early (within 3 hours of injury) to be effective. Survival decreased by 10% for every 15-minute delay in treatment.

**Q:** What RBC morphologies on peripheral blood smear are associated with specific clinical conditions?

**A:** Spherocytes: hereditary spherocytosis, immune hemolytic anemia. Schistocytes (helmet cells): MAHA/TMA (TTP, HUS, DIC, HELLP). Sickle cells: SCD. Target cells: liver disease, thalassemia, HbSC, iron deficiency. Teardrop cells (dacrocytes): myelofibrosis, BM infiltration. Rouleaux: multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom's. Howell-Jolly bodies: post-splenectomy, hyposplenia.

Also important: basophilic stippling (thalassemia, lead poisoning, MDS), Heinz bodies (G6PD deficiency), bite cells (oxidative hemolysis in G6PD), pencil cells (iron deficiency), and smudge cells (CLL).

**Q:** A hospitalized patient on heparin develops a platelet drop from 200 to 80 x 10^9/L on day 7, with a new DVT. What is the diagnosis and immediate management?

**A:** Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). 4Ts score: thrombocytopenia >50% fall (2 points), timing day 5-10 (2 points), new thrombosis (2 points) = high probability (6 points). Immediate management: STOP all heparin (including flushes), start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, fondaparinux, or danaparoid), send HIT ELISA and serotonin release assay.

Do NOT give warfarin until platelets >150 x 10^9/L (risk of warfarin-induced venous limb gangrene). Do NOT give platelet transfusions (may worsen thrombosis). HIT has 30-50% risk of thrombosis if untreated. DOACs can be started before platelet recovery.

**Q:** What is Waldenstrom's macroglobulinemia (lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma) and its key complication?

**A:** Proliferation of lymphoplasmacytoid cells producing monoclonal IgM paraprotein. Chronic disorder of elderly patients (median age 64). Key complication: hyperviscosity syndrome (because IgM is large and confined mainly to intravascular space). Symptoms: weakness, fatigue, oronasal bleeding, neurological symptoms, CHF (triad of anemia, hyperviscosity, plasma volume expansion).

Waldenstrom's accounts for 85% of all cases of hyperviscosity syndrome. Treatment: chemotherapy + rituximab (most commonly bendamustine + rituximab), plasmapheresis for acute hyperviscosity. Unlike myeloma, bone lesions are usually NOT present.

**Q:** What is aplastic anemia and what are its key investigations and treatment?

**A:** Destruction of hematopoietic BM cells causing pancytopenia and hypocellular BM. Causes: idiopathic (often T-cell mediated), drugs (chloramphenicol), toxins (benzene), radiation, viral (parvovirus B19, hepatitis), PNH. Investigations: CBC (pancytopenia), low reticulocytes (<1%), BM biopsy showing hypocellular marrow (<25%) with adipose replacement. Treatment: immunosuppressive therapy (anti-thymocyte globulin + cyclosporine), allogeneic BM transplant.

50% of aplastic anemia patients have PNH (paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria). Supportive care includes judicious blood product use to minimize immune sensitization and iron chelation for iron overload from repeated transfusions.

**Q:** What are the hemoglobin and hematocrit thresholds that define erythrocytosis in males and females?

**A:** Males: Hb >165 g/L or Hct >49%. Females: Hb >160 g/L or Hct >48%.

Erythrocytosis is defined as an absolute increase in red cell mass. The sex-specific thresholds reflect physiologic differences in baseline Hb/Hct values. Accurate thresholds matter for guiding workup (serum EPO, JAK2 mutation).

**Q:** What distinguishes relative (spurious) erythrocytosis from absolute erythrocytosis?

**A:** Relative erythrocytosis is due to decreased plasma volume (e.g., diuretics, severe dehydration, burns, 'stress'/Gaisböck's syndrome) — the red cell mass is normal. Absolute erythrocytosis reflects a true increase in red cell mass.

Differentiating these is critical because relative erythrocytosis does not carry the same thrombotic risk and does not require specific hematologic therapy — treating the volume depletion resolves the finding.

**Q:** What tumours are associated with inappropriate (ectopic) erythropoietin production causing secondary erythrocytosis?

**A:** Hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, cerebellar hemangioblastoma, pheochromocytoma, uterine leiomyoma, and ovarian tumour.

These tumours secrete EPO autonomously, causing erythrocytosis independent of hypoxia. Identifying an EPO-secreting tumour is important because resection can cure the erythrocytosis and the malignancy simultaneously.

**Q:** Which investigation differentiates primary erythrocytosis (polycythemia vera) from secondary causes?

**A:** Serum EPO level: low/normal in primary erythrocytosis (PV); elevated in secondary causes. JAK2 mutation analysis (positive in >96% of PV) should be sent when EPO is low/normal.

In PV, the malignant clone proliferates autonomously, suppressing normal EPO production. Sending JAK2 only when EPO is low/normal avoids unnecessary testing in patients with secondary erythrocytosis where JAK2 is unlikely to be positive.

**Q:** What clinical symptom of erythrocytosis is found ONLY in myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs)?

**A:** Aquagenic pruritus (itching triggered by contact with water) is seen specifically in MPNs, not in secondary erythrocytosis.

Aquagenic pruritus results from mast cell degranulation and histamine release linked to the MPN clone. Its presence in a patient with erythrocytosis strongly suggests a primary myeloproliferative disorder (e.g., PV).

**Q:** What is the definition of thrombocytopenia, and what is pseudothrombocytopenia?

**A:** Thrombocytopenia: platelet count <150 × 10⁹/L. Pseudothrombocytopenia: falsely low platelet count due to EDTA-induced platelet clumping (platelet dumping secondary to EDTA antibodies). Confirm by repeating CBC in a citrated sample.

Always rule out pseudothrombocytopenia before pursuing an extensive workup. Examining the blood film for platelet clumps or satellitism is the first step; confirming with a citrated (sodium citrate) tube resolves the issue.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of thrombocytopenia in hospitalized patients?

**A:** Drugs and infection are the most common causes of thrombocytopenia in hospitalized patients.

This clinical pearl guides the initial workup: a careful medication reconciliation and infectious screen (cultures, serology) should precede more invasive investigations in an inpatient with new-onset thrombocytopenia.

**Q:** On peripheral blood film, what finding suggests immune-mediated thrombocytopenia vs. microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA/TMA)?

**A:** Large platelets are often seen in ITP (immune-mediated). Schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) are seen in MAHA/TMA (e.g., TTP, HUS, DIC).

In ITP, the spleen destroys antibody-coated platelets and releases large, young platelets. In MAHA/TMA, mechanical RBC destruction produces schistocytes — this distinction is critical because TTP requires urgent plasma exchange, not platelet transfusion.

**Q:** What is the definition of thrombocytosis, and what are its two broad categories?

**A:** Platelet count >450 × 10⁹/L. Categories: (1) Primary thrombocytosis — due to MPNs (CML, PV, MF, ET); (2) Reactive/secondary thrombocytosis — acute phase response to surgery, inflammation, infection, bleeding, iron deficiency, neoplasm, asplenia.

Reactive thrombocytosis is far more common. Key distinguishing features: reactive causes are usually self-limited, don't require cytoreduction, and the platelet count rarely exceeds 1000 × 10⁹/L in most reactive states, though overlap exists.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for primary (MPN-associated) thrombocytosis?

**A:** Aspirin plus cytoreductive agents (e.g., hydroxyurea, anagrelide, or interferon-alpha).

Aspirin reduces thrombotic and vasomotor symptoms. Cytoreductive agents lower the platelet count in high-risk patients (age >60, prior thrombosis, platelet count >1500 × 10⁹/L). Hydroxyurea is first-line cytoreduction in Canada; anagrelide or IFN-α are alternatives.

**Q:** What is the definition of pancytopenia, and what does a deficit in all cell lines suggest?

**A:** Pancytopenia is a decrease in all hematopoietic cell lines (RBCs, WBCs, platelets) below normal ranges. It suggests decreased production, sequestration (e.g., hypersplenism), or hemodilution.

A deficit in all three cell lines points to a global BM problem or massive splenomegaly. If only platelets and RBCs are low (without leukopenia), consider non-immune destruction (MAHA) or Evans' syndrome (autoimmune hemolysis + ITP).

**Q:** What is the WHO definition of neutrophilia, and what are the major categories of cause?

**A:** ANC >7.7 × 10⁹/L. Causes: Primary (CML, chronic neutrophilic leukemia, other MPNs, hereditary neutrophilia, leukocyte adhesion deficiency); Secondary (stress/epinephrine, obesity, infection, inflammation [RA, IBD, MI, PE, burns], malignancy, medications [glucocorticoids, β-agonists, lithium, G-CSF]).

Secondary neutrophilia is far more common. Glucocorticoids cause neutrophilia by demargination (shifting neutrophils from the marginated pool into circulation) and delayed apoptosis. Persistent or extreme neutrophilia warrants BM biopsy to exclude CML.

**Q:** What blood film findings suggest infection in a patient with neutrophilia?

**A:** Left shift (bands >20% of WBC), Döhle bodies (intracytoplasmic structures of agglutinated ribosomes), toxic granulation, and cytoplasmic vacuoles.

These morphologic changes reflect accelerated granulopoiesis and neutrophil activation in response to severe infection. Recognizing these on the blood film guides appropriate antibiotic therapy before culture results return.

**Q:** Define the severity grades of neutropenia and state the threshold for febrile neutropenia concern.

**A:** Mild: ANC 1.0–1.5 × 10⁹/L. Moderate: ANC 0.5–1.0 × 10⁹/L (infection risk starts increasing). Severe: ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L. Profound: ANC <0.1 × 10⁹/L for >7 days. FEBRILE NEUTROPENIA: fever + ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L.

ANC = WBC count × (%PMNs + %bands). The threshold of ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L with fever defines febrile neutropenia — a medical emergency requiring prompt broad-spectrum IV antibiotics.

**Q:** What is Felty syndrome and in which condition does it occur?

**A:** Felty syndrome is the triad of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), splenomegaly, and neutropenia. It is an autoimmune cause of neutropenia due to anti-neutrophil antibodies and splenic sequestration.

Felty syndrome occurs in severe, seropositive RA. Neutropenia predisposes to recurrent bacterial infections. Recognition is important because standard RA treatment (DMARDs) may also help the neutropenia; splenectomy is reserved for refractory cases.

**Q:** Which medications are classic drug causes of neutropenia?

**A:** Alkylating agents, antimetabolites, anticonvulsants, antipsychotics, anti-inflammatory agents, antithyroid drugs (thionamides), a-methyldopa (hapten-mediated). Also: clozapine, sulfasalazine, ticlopidine.

Drug-induced neutropenia can be immune-mediated (hapten mechanism — anti-neutrophil antibodies) or directly myelosuppressive (alkylating agents). Regular CBC monitoring is required when initiating clozapine or thionamides.

**Q:** When is G-CSF used in neutropenia management, and what is the evidence for prophylactic G-CSF in chemotherapy?

**A:** G-CSF is used in severe immune-mediated neutropenia to boost counts. Prophylactic G-CSF in chemotherapy reduces infection rate and febrile neutropenia rates but does NOT reduce all-cause mortality or infection-related death.

A meta-analysis of 18 RCTs showed prophylactic CSFs reduced infection rate (38.9% vs. 43.1%), microbiologically documented infections, and febrile neutropenia — but no mortality benefit. This informs risk-stratified use in chemotherapy protocols.

**Q:** What is the definition of lymphocytosis, and what are the most common causes?

**A:** Absolute lymphocyte count >4.0 × 10⁹/L. Most common causes: viral infections (especially EBV/mononucleosis), TB, pertussis, brucellosis, toxoplasmosis, smoking, physiologic stress, hypersensitivity reactions, autoimmune disease (RA), neoplasm (CLL).

Atypical lymphocytes on blood film suggest viral infection (especially EBV). If lymphocyte count persists above 5.0 × 10⁹/L for >3 months, flow cytometry of peripheral blood is indicated to rule out a lymphoproliferative disorder.

**Q:** When should flow cytometry be ordered for lymphocytosis?

**A:** When lymphocyte count is persistently >5.0 × 10⁹/L for >3 months, flow cytometry of peripheral blood should be performed to assess lineage and clonality (to rule out CLL or other lymphoproliferative disorder).

Monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis (MBL) — a precursor to CLL — may be detected this way. Clonality of B-cells is confirmed by kappa/lambda light chain restriction on flow cytometry.

**Q:** What is the definition of lymphopenia, and what are its major causes?

**A:** Absolute lymphocyte count <1.0 × 10⁹/L. Causes: older age, idiopathic CD4+ lymphopenia, iatrogenic (radiation, chemotherapy, immunosuppressives), HIV/AIDS, HBV, HCV, malignancy, malnutrition, alcoholism, autoimmune disease (SLE).

Lymphopenia predisposes to opportunistic infections. In all patients with unexplained lymphopenia, HIV testing is mandatory. CD4+ subset analysis guides antimicrobial prophylaxis thresholds (e.g., PCP prophylaxis when CD4 <200 cells/μL).

**Q:** What is the definition of eosinophilia, and what is hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES)?

**A:** Eosinophilia: absolute eosinophil count >0.5 × 10⁹/L. HES: absolute eosinophil count >1.5 × 10⁹/L on 2 exams ≥1 month apart, with end-organ damage (heart, BM, CNS) and no other identifiable cause.

HES can cause endomyocardial fibrosis, Löffler endocarditis, and neurologic damage. It is classified as primary (clonal BM disorder) or idiopathic. Treatment is corticosteroids; rule out Strongyloides before initiating steroids in endemic-exposure patients.

**Q:** What are the most common secondary causes of eosinophilia?

**A:** Parasitic infections (especially helminth infections) and allergic reactions are the most common. Less common: collagen vascular diseases (RA, polyarteritis nodosa), asthma, eosinophilic pneumonia, EGPA, cholesterol emboli, hematologic malignancy, adrenocortical insufficiency, penicillins, atopic dermatitis.

Helminth infections (e.g., Ascaris, Toxocara, Strongyloides) are the classic cause worldwide. In the Canadian context, allergic diseases (asthma, atopy) are more prevalent. Always consider Addison's disease as a reversible cause.

**Q:** An important precaution before treating eosinophilia with steroids — what must be checked first?

**A:** Strongyloides serology must be collected before initiating steroids in patients at risk of Strongyloides infection. Steroids can cause hyperinfection syndrome in Strongyloides carriers, which is potentially fatal.

Strongyloides stercoralis can persist in the host for decades. Corticosteroid-induced immunosuppression removes the eosinophil-mediated control of the parasite, leading to disseminated hyperinfection with sepsis and meningitis.

**Q:** Define agranulocytosis and list the most common associated medications.

**A:** Agranulocytosis: ANC <100/μL. Associated medications (70% of cases): chemotherapy, clozapine, thionamides (antithyroid drugs), sulfasalazine, ticlopidine.

Mechanism is either immune-mediated (drug-induced antibodies destroy circulating granulocytes) or direct toxic effects on BM precursors. Clozapine requires mandatory CBC monitoring (weekly for first 6 months in Canada). Abrupt fever with oropharyngeal ulcers is the classic presentation.

**Q:** Define leukemoid reaction and how is it distinguished from CML?

**A:** Leukemoid reaction: leukocytosis >50 × 10⁹/L with a marked left shift (myelocytes, metamyelocytes, bands on smear). Unlike CML, it is reactive (causes: infection, drugs, asplenia, malignancy, hemorrhage, acute hemolysis). Must rule out CML and chronic neutrophilic leukemia.

CML is distinguished by the Philadelphia chromosome (BCR-ABL1), basophilia, low leukocyte alkaline phosphatase (LAP) score, and splenomegaly. In leukemoid reaction, LAP is typically elevated and BCR-ABL1 is absent.

**Q:** Which lymph node location carries the HIGHEST risk of malignancy, and what cancers are associated with left vs. right supraclavicular nodes?

**A:** Supraclavicular nodes carry the highest malignancy risk. Right supraclavicular: mediastinal, bronchogenic, esophageal cancers. Left supraclavicular (Virchow's node): gastric, gallbladder, pancreatic, renal, testicular/ovarian cancers.

The left supraclavicular node (Virchow's) drains the thoracic duct, which receives lymph from the abdomen. Its enlargement (Troisier's sign) is a classic finding in advanced abdominal malignancy, especially gastric cancer.

**Q:** What is the gold standard investigation for lymphadenopathy in suspected lymphoproliferative disease, and why should FNA be avoided?

**A:** Excisional biopsy is the gold standard — it preserves lymph node architecture essential for classifying lymphomas. FNA should NOT be used for lymphoproliferative disease because it disrupts architecture needed for histologic subtyping. FNA is acceptable for suspected solid tumour recurrence.

Lymphoma subtyping (e.g., follicular vs. diffuse large B-cell) requires intact nodal architecture. Core needle biopsies may be used when excisional biopsy is not feasible (retroperitoneal, mediastinal nodes), but are second-best.

**Q:** What are the B-symptoms (constitutional symptoms) seen in lymphoma and TB?

**A:** Unexplained fever >38°C, drenching night sweats, and unintentional weight loss >10% body weight over 6 months.

B-symptoms in lymphoma (Hodgkin and NHL) are an adverse prognostic indicator and influence staging. Their presence prompts a thorough workup for lymphoproliferative disease. Pruritus is also associated with Hodgkin lymphoma specifically.

**Q:** What are the features distinguishing inflammatory vs. neoplastic lymph nodes on physical examination?

**A:** Inflammatory: fluctuant/soft, mobile, tender, <1 cm². Neoplastic: firm/hard, matted/immobile, non-tender, >1 cm². Note: lymphoma and CLL nodes can feel rubbery and mobile — these classifications are not absolute.

While these clinical features guide initial assessment, they lack sufficient sensitivity/specificity to replace biopsy. The key caveat: inguinal nodes up to 2 cm can be non-pathologic. Supraclavicular, infraclavicular, and epitrochlear adenopathy is always considered pathologic.

**Q:** What conditions cause MASSIVE splenomegaly (spleen crosses midline or reaches pelvis)?

**A:** CML, CLL, lymphoproliferative disease (NHL/HL), myeloproliferative neoplasms (CML, myelofibrosis), and metastatic tumour.

Massive splenomegaly (starred conditions in the Toronto Notes table) is a clinical clue pointing toward a myeloid or lymphoid malignancy. Myelofibrosis causes massive splenomegaly due to extramedullary hematopoiesis. Other causes of splenomegaly (infection, portal HTN) rarely produce truly massive spleens.

**Q:** What is the CHINA mnemonic for causes of splenomegaly?

**A:** C — Cirrhosis/Congestion (portal HTN); H — Hematological (hemolytic anemias, MPNs, leukemias); I — Infectious (EBV, malaria, HIV, TB); N — Neoplasm (lymphoma, leukemia, metastatic); A — Autoimmune (SLE, Felty syndrome, sarcoidosis).

This mnemonic efficiently organizes the differential diagnosis of splenomegaly for clinical exams. The most common causes in Canada are portal hypertension (cirrhosis) and hematologic malignancies.

**Q:** What are the MCV and key iron indices in iron deficiency anemia vs. anemia of chronic disease vs. thalassemia vs. sideroblastic anemia?

**A:** Iron deficiency anemia: ↓ferritin, ↓serum iron, ↑TIBC, ↓saturation, ↑RDW — hypochromic microcytic film. Anemia of chronic disease: N/↑ferritin, ↓serum iron, ↓TIBC, N saturation, N RDW — normocytic/microcytic. Thalassemia: N/↑ferritin, N/↑serum iron, N TIBC, N/↑saturation, N/↑RDW — hypochromic microcytic with basophilic stippling. Sideroblastic anemia: N/↑ferritin, ↑serum iron, N TIBC, N/↑saturation, ↑RDW — dual population, basophilic stippling.

Ferritin is the single most important test for distinguishing iron deficiency from other causes of microcytic anemia. TIBC is elevated in IDA (body compensates by making more transferrin to capture scarce iron) and decreased in ACD (inflammatory downregulation of transferrin).

**Q:** Describe hepcidin's role in iron homeostasis and how it mediates anemia of chronic disease.

**A:** Hepcidin is a hepatocyte-produced hormone that binds ferroportin (iron exporter on duodenal enterocytes and reticuloendothelial cells) and induces its degradation, thereby trapping iron intracellularly and reducing GI absorption. In inflammation, hepcidin is upregulated → iron trapped in macrophages → unavailable for erythropoiesis → anemia of chronic disease.

Understanding hepcidin explains why serum iron is low in ACD despite adequate iron stores (the iron is trapped). Hepcidin is the key regulatory peptide linking infection/inflammation to the iron-restricted anemia seen in chronic disease.

**Q:** Where is dietary iron absorbed, and what enhances or inhibits its absorption?

**A:** Absorbed in the duodenum. Enhancers: citric acid, ascorbic acid (vitamin C), heme iron. Inhibitors: polyphenols (tea), phytate (bran), dietary calcium, soy protein. Absorption impaired in IBD (duodenal) and celiac disease.

This explains practical dietary counselling: take iron supplements with vitamin C and avoid tea/coffee around the time of supplementation. Post-gastrectomy patients lose the acidic duodenal environment needed for Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ reduction, impairing absorption.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic features of iron deficiency anemia on peripheral blood film?

**A:** Hypochromic microcytosis, pencil forms (oval-shaped RBCs), anisocytosis, target cells, and increased RDW.

Pencil forms (elliptocytes/oval forms) are particularly characteristic of IDA. Target cells reflect a relative excess of RBC membrane. Increased RDW reflects the heterogeneity of RBC size — in contrast, thalassemia typically has uniform microcytosis with normal/mildly elevated RDW.

**Q:** What is the gold standard test for iron stores, and what is the most clinically practical blood test?

**A:** Gold standard: bone marrow aspirate with iron stain (Prussian blue). Most practical blood test: serum ferritin (low ferritin <30 μg/L is diagnostic of iron deficiency).

BM aspirate is rarely performed solely for iron assessment given its invasiveness. Ferritin <30 μg/L has high specificity for IDA. In inflammatory states or liver disease, ferritin may be falsely elevated; <100 μg/L in these settings is suggestive of iron deficiency and warrants further workup (complete iron indices, sTfR).

**Q:** What is Plummer-Vinson syndrome and which underlying condition causes it?

**A:** Plummer-Vinson syndrome: the triad of dysphagia (due to esophageal webs), glossitis, and iron deficiency anemia. It is caused by severe, chronic iron deficiency anemia.

Plummer-Vinson syndrome is a premalignant condition — esophageal webs increase the risk of squamous cell carcinoma of the esophagus. Treatment is iron supplementation; the dysphagia often resolves with iron repletion alone, though webs may require dilation.

**Q:** What is the recommended oral iron supplementation regimen, and what monitoring parameter indicates adequate response?

**A:** Ferrous sulphate 325 mg once daily (65 mg elemental iron), OR ferrous gluconate 300 mg once daily (35 mg elemental), OR ferrous fumarate 300 mg once daily (100 mg elemental). Continue until anemia corrects, then for ≥3 more months until ferritin normalizes. Response: reticulocyte count rises after 1 week; Hb increases by 10 g/L per week (if no ongoing blood loss).

Once-daily dosing is preferred — multiple daily doses up-regulate hepcidin, reduce absorption, and increase GI side effects without clinical benefit. Alternate-day dosing shows insignificant differences vs. daily in recent studies. IV iron is used when oral is not tolerated/absorbed or rapid response is needed.

**Q:** What are the key pathophysiologic mechanisms of anemia of chronic disease (anemia of inflammation)?

**A:** 1) Increased hepcidin (inflammation-driven) → ferroportin degradation → iron trapped in macrophages and enterocytes → reduced plasma iron for erythropoiesis. 2) Marrow unresponsive to normal/slightly elevated EPO. 3) Mild hemolytic component (modestly decreased RBC survival).

ACD is an anemia of underproduction with functional iron deficiency — stores are adequate but iron is inaccessible. This distinguishes it from true IDA where stores are depleted. BM shows normal/increased iron stores with decreased staining in erythroid precursors.

**Q:** How does the peripheral blood film and serum ferritin differ in ACD compared to iron deficiency anemia?

**A:** ACD: ferritin is normal or elevated (acute phase reactant), serum iron ↓, TIBC ↓, normocytic/normochromic (mild) to microcytic/normochromic (moderate) to microcytic/hypochromic (severe), reticulocyte count low. IDA: ferritin ↓, serum iron ↓, TIBC ↑, microcytic/hypochromic, reticulocyte count low.

The critical differentiator is ferritin and TIBC: elevated ferritin and low TIBC point to ACD, while low ferritin and high TIBC point to IDA. When both coexist, sTfR/ferritin index or BM biopsy may be needed.

**Q:** What is the role of soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) in diagnosing iron deficiency, especially in the setting of inflammation?

**A:** sTfR reflects functional iron availability at the tissue level. In iron deficiency, more transferrin receptors are expressed on erythroblasts → sTfR increases. sTfR is also elevated in thalassemia (extramedullary hematopoiesis) and decreased in reduced erythropoiesis and iron overload. It is particularly useful to identify iron deficiency when ferritin is elevated due to inflammation.

Ferritin can be falsely elevated in chronic disease despite true iron deficiency. sTfR is not an acute phase reactant, so it remains elevated in IDA even when ferritin appears normal. The sTfR/log ferritin index further improves discrimination between IDA and ACD.

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis has Hb 98 g/L, MCV 78 fL, ferritin 85 μg/L, serum iron low, TIBC low. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Anemia of chronic disease (anemia of inflammation) secondary to rheumatoid arthritis. The low serum iron with low TIBC and normal/elevated ferritin (>45 μg/L) is characteristic of ACD, distinguishing it from IDA (which has high TIBC and low ferritin).

In RA, inflammatory cytokines (IL-6) stimulate hepatic hepcidin production, trapping iron in macrophages. TIBC is low because transferrin production is suppressed in inflammatory states. Ferritin 85 μg/L — while below 100 μg/L and suggestive of possible coexisting IDA in this inflammatory context — with low TIBC favors ACD. sTfR can help distinguish.

**Q:** In a post-menopausal woman with iron deficiency anemia, what investigation is always warranted regardless of symptoms?

**A:** GI workup (gastroscopy and colonoscopy) is always warranted in post-menopausal women (and all men) with iron deficiency anemia to rule out occult GI blood loss (e.g., right-sided colorectal cancer, angiodysplasia).

Iron deficiency anemia is a common presentation of chronic lower GI bleeding. Right-sided colonic cancers are 'silent' — they bleed slowly without change in bowel habits. Missing this diagnosis is a critical clinical error.

**Q:** What clinical features of pica and restless leg syndrome are associated with iron deficiency?

**A:** Pica: appetite for non-food substances (e.g., ice [pagophagia], paint, dirt). Restless leg syndrome: uncomfortable leg sensations compelling movement, especially at night. Both are non-anemic manifestations of iron deficiency that may precede anemia.

Iron deficiency may cause fatigue and these symptoms before Hb falls. Treating the iron deficiency often resolves restless leg syndrome — making iron deficiency an important treatable cause in the differential of RLS.

**Q:** What is basophilia/eosinophilia associated with in the context of MPNs?

**A:** Basophilia and/or eosinophilia can be an indicator of CML or other MPNs. They are associated with pruritus due to excessive histamine production from basophil/mast cell degranulation.

In CML, basophilia is a hallmark feature — basophils release histamine causing pruritus. Increasing basophilia (>20%) is a sign of blast phase/accelerated disease. Aquagenic pruritus in PV shares a similar histamine-mediated mechanism.

**Q:** What is Gaisböck's syndrome?

**A:** Gaisböck's syndrome (also called stress polycythemia or spurious/relative erythrocytosis) is a condition of elevated Hb/Hct due to decreased plasma volume rather than a true increase in red cell mass. It is associated with stress, obesity, hypertension, and diuretic use.

Unlike true polycythemia vera, Gaisböck's syndrome does not require cytoreduction or phlebotomy. Management focuses on treating the underlying causes (weight loss, hypertension control, hydration). EPO levels are normal and JAK2 is negative.

**Q:** What does PTT measure and what mnemonic helps remember which pathway it tests?

**A:** PTT measures the intrinsic pathway (factors VIII, IX, XI, XII) and common pathway. Mnemonic: 'Table Tennis is played Inside' (Intrinsic pathway).

PTT is prolonged in hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) and hemophilia B (factor IX deficiency) when factor deficiency is below the reagent threshold of detection. It is also used to monitor heparin and argatroban therapy, and is prolonged (paradoxically) by lupus anticoagulant in vitro.

**Q:** What does PT/INR measure and what mnemonic helps remember which pathway it tests?

**A:** PT/INR measures the extrinsic pathway (factor VII) and common pathway. Mnemonic: 'Tennis is played Outside' (Extrinsic pathway). INR is used to monitor warfarin therapy.

PT is prolonged in vitamin K deficiency, warfarin therapy, factor VII deficiency, and liver disease. INR is mathematically derived from PT. The INR normalizes PT measurements across different laboratory reagents, making it the standard for monitoring anticoagulation with warfarin.

**Q:** What are the four main causes of a prolonged PTT with a normal PT in a patient NOT on heparin?

**A:** 1. Early contact factor deficiency (Factor XII, HMWK, PK) — no bleeding risk; 2. Lupus anticoagulant; 3. Inappropriate blood draw (e.g., heparin line contamination); 4. Laboratory error.

Isolated prolonged PTT with normal PT suggests intrinsic pathway involvement. Factor XII, HMWK, and PK deficiencies prolong PTT but do NOT cause clinical bleeding. Lupus anticoagulant inhibits the pathway in vitro (paradoxically associated with thrombosis in vivo). Always consider pre-analytical errors before pursuing extensive workup.

**Q:** How do mixing studies differentiate a factor deficiency from a coagulation inhibitor?

**A:** Mix patient plasma 1:1 with normal plasma and repeat the abnormal test. Normalization indicates a factor deficiency. Lack of normalization indicates an inhibitor (e.g., lupus anticoagulant, factor VIII inhibitor).

Normal plasma contains all coagulation factors; diluting with it will correct the prolonged PT/PTT if a single factor is deficient. If an inhibitor is present, it will inhibit the coagulation factors in the normal plasma too, and the test remains prolonged. Normalization may not occur if multiple coagulation factors are deficient.

**Q:** What are the clinical hallmarks distinguishing primary hemostasis disorders from secondary hemostasis disorders?

**A:** Primary (platelet/vessel): immediate bleeding after injury, superficial mucocutaneous sites (epistaxis, gingival, GI, menorrhagia), petechiae and ecchymoses. Secondary (coagulation): delayed bleeding, deep bleeding (joints, muscles), hemarthroses and hematomas.

This distinction is key on the MCCQE1. Primary hemostasis disorders result from platelet plug failure; bleeding is immediate and superficial. Secondary hemostasis disorders result from failure to form a stable fibrin clot; bleeding is delayed (the platelet plug forms but dissolves) and occurs at deep sites. Recognizing this pattern guides appropriate laboratory workup.

**Q:** What are the lab findings in Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)?

**A:** CBC: isolated thrombocytopenia (platelets <100×10⁹/L). PT and PTT: normal. Peripheral blood film: decreased platelets with large platelets (rule out platelet clumping). Bone marrow: increased megakaryocytes.

ITP is an isolated platelet disorder; coagulation cascade is intact so PT and PTT are normal. Large platelets reflect increased platelet turnover (young platelets are larger). Bone marrow biopsy showing increased megakaryocytes confirms peripheral destruction rather than underproduction. HIV and HCV should be tested to rule out secondary ITP.

**Q:** Compare childhood ITP vs. adult ITP in terms of key distinguishing features.

**A:** Childhood ITP: peak age 2–6 yr, equal M:F, common preceding infection, usually weeks duration, >80% spontaneous remission. Adult ITP: peak age 20–40 yr, F>M, infection rare, months to years duration, spontaneous remission uncommon.

In children, ITP is typically a self-limited post-infectious condition (often viral), while adult ITP tends to be chronic and requires more aggressive management. Adult ITP is associated with autoimmune conditions (SLE, HIV, HCV, CLL). The sex difference in adults reflects autoimmune disease predilection in women.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of ITP?

**A:** Anti-platelet antibodies bind to platelet surface antigens → increased splenic clearance of platelets. Additionally, helper T-cell and cytotoxic T-cell activation, and impaired platelet production (megakaryocyte suppression).

ITP is an acquired immune-mediated disorder involving both humoral (anti-platelet antibodies) and cellular (T-cell) immunity. The antibodies are typically directed against GPIIb/IIIa or GPIb. Platelet destruction occurs primarily in the spleen, which is why splenectomy can be effective as second-line therapy.

**Q:** Outline the ITP treatment algorithm: when to treat, emergency treatment, and line-by-line therapy.

**A:** Treatment rarely indicated if platelets >30×10⁹/L unless active bleeding/surgery. Emergency: corticosteroids ± IVIg, platelet transfusion for major bleed. 1st-line: corticosteroids or IVIg. 2nd-line: splenectomy, TPO agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag), rituximab. 3rd-line: immunomodulators (mycophenolate, azathioprine), Syk inhibitors (fostamatinib).

IVIg raises platelet count faster than corticosteroids and can be combined with steroids for urgency. Splenectomy is not preferred within 12 months of diagnosis. Vaccinations (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae type b) are required before splenectomy. Response definitions: complete (>100×10⁹/L), partial (30–100×10⁹/L), no response (<30×10⁹/L).

**Q:** A patient on heparin develops thrombocytopenia on day 7. What is the diagnosis, pathophysiology, and initial management?

**A:** Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT). Pathophysiology: antibodies against heparin–platelet factor 4 complex activate platelets via Fc receptor → platelet consumption and coagulation activation → paradoxical thrombosis. Management: immediately stop ALL heparin (including flushes), initiate non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, danaparoid, fondaparinux, or bivalirudin).

HIT is an immune-mediated prothrombotic disorder with 30–50% risk of thrombosis (25% arterial) if untreated. Diagnosis is supported by the 4T score. Warfarin is started only after platelet count recovers >150×10⁹/L (warfarin can initially worsen thrombosis by depleting protein C). DOACs can be started before platelet recovery. LMWH is also contraindicated (also causes HIT, though less commonly).

**Q:** What is the 4T score for HIT and how is it interpreted?

**A:** 4Ts score assesses: Thrombocytopenia severity, Timing of platelet fall, Thrombosis/sequelae, and other causes (oTher). Score 6–8 = high probability; 4–5 = intermediate probability; 0–3 = low probability (HIT can be excluded).

HIT onset is typically 5–14 days after heparin initiation. If previously exposed to heparin within 100 days, HIT can develop within hours (anamnestic response). Per American Society of Hematology Choosing Wisely: do not test or treat for HIT in low pre-test probability patients (4T score 0–3); stopping heparin unnecessarily increases bleeding risk and cost.

**Q:** What are the two confirmatory tests for HIT and how do they differ?

**A:** 1. 14C Serotonin Release Assay (SRA): functional assay testing if patient plasma can activate platelets; gold standard. 2. ELISA for HIT-IgG: more sensitive but less specific than SRA; faster turnaround; high negative predictive value.

Screening begins with ELISA (high sensitivity means a negative result effectively rules out HIT). Positive ELISA results should be confirmed with functional testing (SRA) due to lower specificity. Clinical pre-test probability (4T score) should always guide testing decisions — low probability should prompt neither testing nor treatment.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)?

**A:** Deficiency or inhibition of ADAMTS13 (metalloproteinase that cleaves ultra-large VWF multimers) → accumulation of uncleaved large VWF multimers → excessive platelet adhesion and aggregation in small blood vessels → microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHATMA) and thrombocytopenia.

Acquired TTP (most common) is due to autoantibodies against ADAMTS13. Congenital TTP (Upshaw-Schulman syndrome) is due to genetic ADAMTS13 deficiency. Ultra-large VWF multimers are the most active in platelet adhesion, so their accumulation drives pathological microvascular thrombosis. Plasma exchange replaces ADAMTS13 and removes antibodies.

**Q:** Compare TTP vs. HUS: clinical features, etiology, and management.

**A:** TTP: MAHATMA + neurological symptoms (headache, confusion, seizures); ADAMTS13 deficiency; treat with plasma exchange + steroids ± caplacizumab. HUS: MAHATMA + severe AKI + bloody diarrhea (GI prodrome); Shiga toxin E. coli O157:H7 in 90%; treat with supportive therapy (fluids, RBC transfusion).

Both TTP and HUS are thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) presenting with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHATMA = schistocytes on smear, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, elevated unconjugated bilirubin, negative Coombs test). TTP predominantly affects adults; HUS predominantly affects children and elderly. Platelet transfusion is avoided in TTP (associated with microvascular thrombosis). TTP mortality ~90% if untreated.

**Q:** What are the laboratory findings common to all thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs)?

**A:** CBC/blood film: thrombocytopenia + schistocytes. Hemolysis markers: elevated unconjugated bilirubin, elevated LDH, decreased haptoglobin. Negative Coombs test (DAT). Normal PT, PTT, and fibrinogen (distinguishes from DIC).

The microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHATMA) pattern is key: mechanical destruction of RBCs by fibrin strands creates schistocytes. The negative Coombs test distinguishes TMA from autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Normal coagulation studies distinguish TMA from DIC (both may have thrombocytopenia and hemolysis). ADAMTS13 testing differentiates TTP from HUS.

**Q:** What is von Willebrand Disease (VWD), what is its inheritance pattern, and what is its prevalence?

**A:** VWD is the most common inherited bleeding disorder (prevalence ~1%). Most types are autosomal dominant (types 2N and 3 are autosomal recessive). Affects males and females equally, but females are more commonly diagnosed due to heavy menstrual and peripartum bleeding.

VWF mediates platelet adhesion/aggregation (primary hemostasis) and acts as a chaperone for Factor VIII, extending its half-life (secondary hemostasis). Therefore, severe VWD (type 3) can affect both primary and secondary hemostasis. VWF levels are influenced by blood type (lowest in group O), pregnancy, hormonal medications, and acute inflammation.

**Q:** Classify the types of VWD and their mechanisms.

**A:** Type 1 (80%): mild quantitative deficiency — decreased VWF amount. Type 2 (20%): qualitative defects — 2A (loss of high/intermediate multimers, reduced platelet adhesion), 2B (increased GPIb affinity, loss of large multimers, mild thrombocytopenia), 2M (reduced platelet adhesion with normal multimers), 2N (decreased Factor VIII binding). Type 3 (1/million): severe total absence of VWF.

Type 2B is unique because the increased affinity for platelet GPIb causes clearance of the large multimers (which are most active) AND can cause mild thrombocytopenia (DDAVP is contraindicated in type 2B as it releases VWF and can worsen thrombocytopenia). Type 3 patients can have hemarthroses due to severe factor VIII deficiency from loss of VWF chaperoning. Type 2N mimics hemophilia A (decreased factor VIII) but is autosomal recessive.

**Q:** What investigations are required to diagnose and classify VWD?

**A:** CBC (platelets — may be low in type 2B), VWF antigen (quantity), VWF ristocetin cofactor activity (VWF function/binding to platelet GPIb), Factor VIII level (VWF chaperoning), PTT (may be prolonged). Further: multimer analysis, ristocetin-induced platelet agglutination (RIPA), genetic studies. Blood group affects VWF antigen levels (lower in group O).

The ristocetin cofactor activity reflects how well VWF binds to platelets — it is disproportionately lower than antigen in type 2 VWD. The ratio of activity to antigen helps classify the subtype. Factor VIII may be decreased in type 2N and type 3 due to loss of VWF chaperoning. Consider VWD in all women with heavy menstrual bleeding.

**Q:** What is the treatment for VWD?

**A:** DDAVP (desmopressin): effective for 85–90% of type 1 VWD and some type 2; releases VWF and Factor VIII from endothelial cells; tachyphylaxis after 4 consecutive doses; caution in children (hyponatremia). VWF:Factor VIII concentrate (Humate-P, Wilate): if DDAVP unresponsive or severe bleeding. Tranexamic acid (antifibrinolytic): especially for mucosal bleeding. Estrogens: increase VWF levels (for heavy menstrual bleeding).

A DDAVP challenge should document responsiveness before relying on it for treatment. Very high Factor VIII levels from VWF concentrates can be prothrombotic, so monitoring is required. Type 1 VWD often auto-corrects in pregnancy (VWF increases physiologically). DDAVP is contraindicated in type 2B (releases abnormal VWF that over-binds platelets). Only ~10% of VWD patients require long-term prophylactic therapy.

**Q:** What is Hemophilia A? Describe its genetics, severity classification, and lab findings.

**A:** Hemophilia A: X-linked recessive factor VIII deficiency; 1/5000 males. Severity: mild (>5% factor VIII), moderate (1–5%), severe (<1%). Lab: prolonged PTT, normal PT/INR, decreased factor VIII (<40% of normal). Female carriers have variable factor VIII levels due to skewed X-inactivation.

Hemophilia A presents with delayed deep bleeding (hemarthroses, hematomas, muscle bleeds) — features of secondary hemostasis disorder. The PTT is prolonged because factor VIII is part of the intrinsic pathway. PT is normal because factor VIII is not in the extrinsic pathway. In Canada, patients may have acquired HIV or HCV from contaminated blood products — no cases since 1985.

**Q:** What is the treatment for Hemophilia A?

**A:** Mild: DDAVP (releases endothelial factor VIII). Moderate/severe: factor VIII concentrate (prophylaxis recommended for severe; on-demand for bleeds). Antifibrinolytic agents (tranexamic acid) for mucosal bleeds. Gene therapy under development.

Prophylactic factor VIII is recommended for severe hemophilia A to prevent recurrent hemarthroses that lead to joint destruction (hemophilic arthropathy). DDAVP is only effective in mild hemophilia A (those who have some residual endothelial VWF/factor VIII stores). VWF antigen and ristocetin activity should be tested to rule out co-existing VWD.

**Q:** Compare Hemophilia A vs. Hemophilia B.

**A:** Hemophilia A: factor VIII deficiency, 1/5000 males. Hemophilia B (Christmas disease): factor IX deficiency, 1/30,000 males; ~50% have severe disease. Clinical and laboratory features are identical. Treatment: Hemophilia A — factor VIII concentrate; Hemophilia B — factor IX concentrate. Both: antifibrinolytics, gene therapy.

Both are X-linked recessive disorders affecting males, with identical clinical presentations (deep bleeding, hemarthroses) and laboratory findings (prolonged PTT, normal PT). They can only be distinguished by specific factor assays. Factor IX (Christmas factor) deficiency was named after Stephen Christmas, the first patient described. Hemophilia B is less common than A.

**Q:** What is Factor XI deficiency (Hemophilia C) and what population is it most common in?

**A:** Factor XI deficiency is autosomal recessive, most common in Ashkenazi Jewish population. Usually mild; severe spontaneous bleeding is rare. Factor XI level does NOT correlate with bleeding risk — bleeding risk correlates with previous/family history of bleeding. Treatment: antifibrinolytics, fresh frozen plasma, factor XI concentrate, DDAVP.

Hemophilia C is unique because factor XI level does not predict bleeding phenotype — some patients with very low factor XI levels bleed minimally, while others bleed significantly. This is because factor XI amplifies, rather than initiates, thrombin generation. It is often diagnosed incidentally on pre-operative testing or following a surgical bleeding challenge in adulthood.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of coagulopathy in liver disease and what lab findings are expected?

**A:** Thrombocytopenia (hypersplenism, decreased TPO, alcohol toxicity, HCV). Decreased synthesis of all coagulation factors EXCEPT factor VIII (made in endothelium). Decreased protein C and S (anticoagulants). Decreased fibrinogen in end-stage disease. Labs: elevated INR (PT), elevated PTT, low fibrinogen. Factor VIII normal or increased.

Liver disease creates a 'rebalanced hemostasis' — both procoagulant and anticoagulant factors are reduced. This means INR does not accurately reflect bleeding risk. Factor V and VII have the shortest half-lives and decrease earliest. Factor VIII is made in sinusoidal endothelial cells (not hepatocytes) and is therefore normal or elevated in liver disease (also an acute phase reactant).

**Q:** What are the vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors and what conditions cause vitamin K deficiency?

**A:** Vitamin K-dependent factors: X, IX, VII, II (prothrombin), Protein C and S. Mnemonic: '1972' (factors II, VII, IX, X). Causes of deficiency: warfarin, antibiotics (eradicate gut flora), poor diet/prolonged fasting, biliary obstruction, fat malabsorption (celiac, pancreatic insufficiency, cystic fibrosis), chronic liver disease.

Vitamin K deficiency affects the extrinsic pathway earliest (factor VII has the shortest half-life) — INR (PT) is elevated out of proportion to the PTT. PT should improve within 24 hours of vitamin K repletion (onset 6–12 hours). If PT does not improve, search for other causes (liver disease, DIC, inhibitors). Proteins C and S are also vitamin K-dependent anticoagulants.

**Q:** How do you manage a patient on warfarin with life-threatening bleeding?

**A:** Give vitamin K 10 mg IV (reversal takes up to 12 hours) PLUS prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). If PCC is contraindicated, give fresh frozen plasma (FFP). PCC is relatively contraindicated in liver disease or previous HIT (contains heparin). Do not use PCC/FFP for non-emergent reversal (per ASH Choosing Wisely).

PCC contains factors II, VII, IX, X (vitamin K-dependent factors) and provides rapid reversal of warfarin. It is preferred over FFP because it requires smaller volumes (avoids fluid overload) and provides faster, more complete reversal. Vitamin K alone takes too long for life-threatening hemorrhage. Indications for urgent reversal: major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, or emergent surgery.

**Q:** What is DIC? Describe its pathophysiology, clinical features, and key lab findings.

**A:** DIC: excessive, dysregulated thrombin and plasmin release → intravascular coagulation and fibrinolysis → depletion of platelets, coagulation factors, and fibrinogen → simultaneous hemorrhage AND thrombosis. Labs: thrombocytopenia, elevated PT/PTT/TT, decreased fibrinogen, elevated D-dimers/FDPs, schistocytes on smear.

DIC is always secondary to an underlying condition (OMITS: Obstetric complications, Malignancy, Infection, Trauma/surgery, Shock). Unlike TMA (TTP/HUS), DIC has abnormal coagulation studies (elevated PT/PTT, low fibrinogen). Unlike liver disease, fibrinogen is consumed in DIC. The ISTH scoring system uses platelet count, D-dimer/FDPs, PT prolongation, and fibrinogen to diagnose DIC (≥5 points = overt DIC).

**Q:** What is the mnemonic for common etiologies of DIC?

**A:** OMITS: Obstetric complications (placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, HELLP), Malignancy (especially promyelocytic leukemia), Infection (sepsis, gram-negative bacteria), Trauma/surgery (burns, crush injury), Shock.

DIC occurs as a complication of conditions causing widespread endothelial damage and inflammatory cytokine release. In obstetric DIC, placental abruption and amniotic fluid embolism are classic triggers. Sepsis is the most common cause of DIC in ICU patients. Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL/AML-M3) releases tissue factor-like procoagulants from granules, causing severe DIC.

**Q:** How is DIC managed in the bleeding phase vs. thrombotic phase?

**A:** Bleeding phase: treat underlying cause; platelets if <50×10⁹/L with active bleeding (or <20×10⁹/L at high risk); FFP if prolonged PT/PTT >1.5× normal with active bleeding; cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate if fibrinogen <1.5 g/L; PCC if FFP not possible. Thrombotic phase: LMWH preferred over UFH in critically ill non-bleeding patients.

The key principle is treating the underlying cause while providing supportive transfusion therapy. Replacement blood products are only used for active bleeding or planned procedures, NOT prophylactically (giving products without bleeding may 'fuel the fire'). Cryoprecipitate is the concentrated source of fibrinogen, VWF, and factor VIII. RBC transfusion is used for severe anemia from hemolysis/bleeding.

**Q:** What pattern of coagulation test abnormality suggests isolated warfarin use or vitamin K deficiency (early)?

**A:** Isolated elevation of PT/INR with normal PTT. This reflects selective factor VII deficiency (shortest half-life, extrinsic pathway only). As deficiency progresses, PTT also becomes elevated (factors II, IX, X are shared in common and intrinsic pathways).

Factor VII has the shortest half-life (~6 hours), so its levels fall first in warfarin use or vitamin K deficiency. Early vitamin K deficiency or warfarin effect is therefore detected by elevated PT/INR before the PTT becomes abnormal. The differential for isolated elevated PT includes: warfarin, vitamin K deficiency, factor VII deficiency, and liver disease.

**Q:** What are the criteria for diagnosing Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APS)?

**A:** Revised Sapporo Criteria: ≥1 clinical criterion AND ≥1 laboratory criterion. Clinical: arterial/venous thrombosis, ≥3 consecutive early fetal losses (<10 wk), 1 late fetal loss (≥10 wk) of morphologically normal fetus, premature birth <34 wk due to (pre)eclampsia or placental insufficiency. Laboratory (confirmed on 2 occasions ≥12 wk apart): anticardiolipin IgG/IgM, anti-β2 glycoprotein-I Ab, or lupus anticoagulant.

APS is an acquired thrombophilia associated with both arterial and venous thrombosis, as well as pregnancy morbidity. The lupus anticoagulant paradoxically prolongs PTT in vitro but causes thrombosis in vivo. Laboratory criteria must be confirmed on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart to exclude transient antibody positivity (e.g., post-infectious). APS is associated with SLE in about 30–40% of cases.

**Q:** What is Factor V Leiden mutation and why does it cause hypercoagulability?

**A:** Factor V Leiden: most common hereditary thrombophilia (3–7% of European White population are heterozygotes). Point mutation (R506Q) in the Factor V gene → Factor Va is resistant to inactivation by activated Protein C (APC resistance) → sustained thrombin generation → hypercoagulability.

Normally, activated Protein C inactivates Factor Va (and Factor VIIIa) to limit coagulation. The R506Q mutation changes the cleavage site, making Factor Va resistant to APC inactivation. Factor V Leiden is primarily associated with venous thrombosis, not arterial. Homozygotes have higher thrombotic risk than heterozygotes. It does NOT cause arterial thrombosis typically — arterial events suggest APS, HIT, or malignancy.

**Q:** What is the Prothrombin Gene Mutation G20210A and what thrombophilias are associated with European White populations?

**A:** Prothrombin G20210A: G→A transposition at nucleotide 20210 in the prothrombin gene promoter → increased prothrombin levels → increased thrombin generation → venous thrombosis. Affects 1–3% of European White population (heterozygotes). Also associated with hereditary thrombophilia: Factor V Leiden (3–7%) is most common.

Prothrombin G20210A is the second most common hereditary thrombophilia after Factor V Leiden. The mutation increases prothrombin mRNA stability, leading to higher prothrombin production and a prothrombotic state. Both Factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A are more common in people of European ancestry. Together they account for the majority of identified hereditary thrombophilias.

**Q:** What are Protein C and Protein S deficiencies and what conditions cause acquired deficiency?

**A:** Protein C inactivates Factors Va and VIIIa; Protein S is its cofactor. Deficiency leads to hypercoagulability. Acquired causes: liver disease, sepsis, DIC, warfarin, certain chemotherapy. Homozygous Protein C deficiency causes neonatal purpura fulminans. 1/3 of warfarin necrosis cases have underlying Protein C deficiency.

Warfarin initially depletes Protein C and S faster than vitamin K-dependent procoagulant factors (shorter half-life of Protein C), transiently increasing thrombosis risk — this is why warfarin necrosis occurs in Protein C deficient patients. Protein C and S levels are affected by acute thrombosis and anticoagulation therapy, so testing should occur at least 6 weeks after acute thrombosis and off anticoagulation. Both are autosomal dominant (type I: decreased levels; type II: decreased function).

**Q:** What is Antithrombin (AT) deficiency and how does it affect heparin therapy?

**A:** Antithrombin slowly inactivates thrombin; heparin accelerates this process. AT deficiency causes: autosomal dominant inheritance, urinary losses (nephrotic syndrome), or reduced synthesis (liver disease). Deficiency can cause heparin resistance (suspect if >35,000 IU UFH required in 24 hours). LMWH with anti-Xa monitoring may be considered.

Because heparin exerts its anticoagulant effect by accelerating antithrombin activity, AT deficiency makes heparin therapy less effective. Testing for AT deficiency must be done outside the window of acute thrombosis and anticoagulation (both heparin and acute thrombosis consume antithrombin, leading to false low levels). AT deficiency is the most thrombogenic of the hereditary thrombophilias.

**Q:** When should hypercoagulability workup be performed and what are the key indications?

**A:** Workup only if it will alter treatment decisions. Consider in: multiple recurrent thromboses, warfarin-induced skin necrosis or neonatal purpura fulminans (Protein C/S deficiency), thrombosis at unusual sites (cerebral, jugular, splanchnic, portal, upper extremity), abnormal constitutional findings suggesting cancer. Protein C, S, ATIII tested >6 weeks post-thrombosis and off anticoagulation.

Per ASH Choosing Wisely: do NOT test for thrombophilia in adults with a first VTE occurring in setting of a major transient risk factor (e.g., major surgery, hospitalization, prolonged immobility) — results rarely change management and testing often leads to unnecessary anxiety and anticoagulation. Arterial thrombotic events are typically associated with APS, HIT, JAK2/MPNs, malignancy, and PNH — NOT hereditary thrombophilias.

**Q:** What conditions are associated with BOTH venous and arterial thrombosis (as opposed to venous only)?

**A:** Conditions causing both venous AND arterial thrombosis: APS (antiphospholipid syndrome), HIT, JAK2 mutations/MPNs, malignancy, PNH. Distal venous clot with patent foramen ovale (paradoxical embolism) can cause arterial events.

Most hereditary thrombophilias (Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, Protein C/S deficiency, AT deficiency) cause ONLY venous thrombosis. Arterial thrombosis with thrombophilia should prompt evaluation for APS, HIT, or MPN. This distinction is clinically important as the workup and management differ significantly between arterial and venous causes.

**Q:** What is Virchow's Triad and give examples of each component predisposing to DVT?

**A:** Virchow's Triad: 1) Endothelial damage (surgery, trauma, IV catheters, inflammation); 2) Venous stasis (immobilization, CHF, stroke, post-op, prolonged travel); 3) Hypercoagulability (inherited thrombophilias, malignancy, OCP, pregnancy, APS, HIT, MPNs).

These three components define the conditions predisposing to thrombus formation in any vessel. DVT risk is highest with orthopedic, thoracic, GI, and GU surgery. Neoplasms most associated with VTE include pancreas, stomach, lung, lymphoma, bladder, testicular, colorectal, and gynecologic cancers (Khorana score). Idiopathic DVT — 10–20% are later found to have occult cancer.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of DVT and which physical finding is unreliable?

**A:** DVT: unilateral leg edema, erythema, warmth, tenderness; purple-blue colour may indicate limb-threatening thrombus; palpable cord (thrombosed vein); phlegmasia alba dolens (white) or cerulea dolens (blue/purple, acute pain + edema) with massive thrombosis. Homan's sign (pain with foot dorsiflexion) is UNRELIABLE.

Absence of physical findings does NOT rule out DVT — up to 50% of DVTs are asymptomatic. Homan's sign has poor sensitivity and specificity. Phlegmasia cerulea dolens indicates massive DVT with venous outflow obstruction, requiring urgent intervention. The most important sequelae of DVT are pulmonary embolism (~50% with proximal DVT) and post-thrombotic syndrome (chronic venous insufficiency).

**Q:** In the lab findings table for disorders of hemostasis, what pattern is seen in DIC vs. TTP vs. liver disease?

**A:** DIC: thrombocytopenia ↓, PT ↑, PTT ↑, Hb ↓. TTP: thrombocytopenia ↓↓, PT/PTT normal, Hb ↓ (MAHATMA). Liver disease: thrombocytopenia ↓, PT ↑, PTT normal/↑, Hb normal/↓. ITP: thrombocytopenia ↓, PT/PTT normal, Hb normal. VWD: PT normal, PTT normal/↑, platelets normal/↓ (type 2B), Hb normal.

The coagulation profile (PT, PTT) combined with platelet count and Hb is essential for narrowing the differential of bleeding disorders. DIC uniquely affects all components (platelets, PT, PTT, fibrinogen). TTP/HUS have normal coagulation studies (PT/PTT) — key distinguishing feature from DIC. ITP is an isolated platelet disorder with normal coagulation. VWD type 1 can have normal PT/PTT, making VWF-specific tests essential.

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis for isolated prolonged INR (PT) only?

**A:** Warfarin use, vitamin K deficiency, factor VII deficiency, liver disease (early or mild — factor VII has shortest half-life), factor VII inhibitor, severe vitamin K deficiency.

An isolated elevated PT/INR (normal PTT) localizes the defect to the extrinsic pathway (factor VII) or early common pathway. Factor VII has the shortest half-life (~6 hours) and is exclusively in the extrinsic pathway. Therefore, it falls first in vitamin K deficiency, warfarin therapy, or liver disease. PT should normalize within 24 hours of vitamin K repletion if vitamin K deficiency is the cause.

**Q:** What is the significance of lupus anticoagulant in the context of PTT and clinical thrombosis?

**A:** Lupus anticoagulant paradoxically PROLONGS the PTT in vitro (inhibits the pathway in vitro), but CAUSES THROMBOSIS in vivo (associated with APS). It will NOT correct on mixing studies (it is an inhibitor). It is a misnomer — it does not indicate lupus and is not an anticoagulant clinically.

The lupus anticoagulant binds to phospholipid-protein complexes used in coagulation testing, thereby prolonging PTT in the lab. In vivo, the same antibodies promote thrombosis by interacting with platelet and endothelial phospholipids. Testing for lupus anticoagulant should be done before starting anticoagulation, as anticoagulation can affect the result. It is part of the APS laboratory criteria.

**Q:** What vaccines are required before splenectomy in a patient with ITP?

**A:** Pre-splenectomy vaccinations required: Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumovax), Neisseria meningitidis (including quadrivalent and serotype b), Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib). These are encapsulated organisms that the spleen is responsible for clearing.

Post-splenectomy patients are at lifelong risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI), particularly from encapsulated bacteria (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae type b) that require opsonization for clearance — a function of the spleen. Vaccines should be given at least 2 weeks before splenectomy if possible. Lifelong penicillin prophylaxis is also recommended in some guidelines.

**Q:** What is the difference between heparin-associated thrombocytopenia (HAT) and HIT (previously HIT type II)?

**A:** HAT (previously HIT type I): direct heparin-mediated platelet aggregation (non-immune), onset 24–72 hours, mild thrombocytopenia (>100×10⁹/L), self-limited, NO thrombotic risk, can continue heparin. HIT (previously HIT type II): immune-mediated (anti-PF4/heparin antibodies), onset 5–14 days, significant thrombocytopenia, 30–50% thrombosis risk, MUST stop heparin.

This distinction is clinically critical. HAT is a benign, transient phenomenon that does not require stopping heparin. HIT is a serious prothrombotic disorder requiring immediate discontinuation of all heparin products (including LMWH and heparin flushes) and substitution with a non-heparin anticoagulant. LMWH is also associated with HIT but at a lower rate than UFH.

**Q:** What are the main causes of venous vs. arterial thrombosis to consider in a hypercoagulability workup?

**A:** Venous thrombosis causes: hereditary thrombophilias (Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, Protein C/S/AT deficiency), APS, malignancy, surgery/trauma, immobility, OCP/pregnancy, MPN, PNH. Arterial thrombosis causes: APS, HIT, JAK2 mutations/MPNs, malignancy, PNH, and cardiovascular risk factors (atherosclerosis, AFib).

This distinction guides the workup. Hereditary thrombophilias are primarily associated with venous thromboembolism. Arterial thrombosis in a young patient without conventional cardiovascular risk factors should prompt investigation for APS, HIT, MPNs (JAK2 mutation), and PNH. Hematology consultation should be sought for complex cases, as most thrombophilia tests do not change acute management.

**Q:** What is caplacizumab and in what setting is it used for TTP?

**A:** Caplacizumab is an anti-VWF nanobody that blocks the interaction between ultra-large VWF multimers and platelet GPIb, preventing pathological platelet aggregation. It is used in certain cases of ACQUIRED TTP (immune-mediated ADAMTS13 deficiency) in addition to plasma exchange and steroids.

Caplacizumab does not address the underlying cause of TTP (ADAMTS13 deficiency due to autoantibodies) but rapidly blocks the downstream consequences (platelet aggregation). It reduces time to platelet count normalization and TTP recurrence. It is not indicated for congenital TTP or HUS. Clinical evidence supports its use but availability and cost may limit access in Canada.

**Q:** Why should platelet transfusion be AVOIDED in TTP?

**A:** Platelet transfusion in TTP is associated with microvascular thrombosis (worsening the disease) and should only be used for life-threatening bleeding. The pathology of TTP is excessive platelet aggregation in small vessels; adding more platelets provides more substrate for thrombosis.

This is a classic MCCQE1 'do not do' scenario. Unlike ITP (where platelet transfusion may be used for severe bleeding), TTP is characterized by pathological platelet consumption in microvascular thrombi. Transfusing platelets in TTP has been described as 'adding fuel to the fire.' Plasma exchange is the cornerstone of TTP management, providing ADAMTS13 and removing anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies.

**Q:** What is the ISTH DIC scoring system and how is a score calculated?

**A:** ISTH DIC Score requires an underlying predisposing condition. Points scored for: Platelet count (>100: 0, 50–100: 1, <50: 2), Fibrin markers/D-dimer (no increase: 0, moderate: 2, strong increase: 3), Prolonged PT (<3 sec: 0, 3–6 sec: 1, >6 sec: 2), Fibrinogen (>1 g/L: 0, <1 g/L: 1). Score ≥5 = overt DIC.

The ISTH scoring system provides a standardized approach to DIC diagnosis. A score of ≥5 points in a patient with a predisposing condition indicates overt DIC. Scores of 4 or below suggest non-overt DIC (early or compensated). Serial scoring every 1–2 days is recommended in patients with predisposing conditions to monitor disease progression. D-dimer and FDPs reflect accelerated fibrinolysis.

**Q:** What is the most useful diagnostic test for DVT, and what are its sensitivity and specificity for proximal DVT?

**A:** Doppler ultrasound is the most useful diagnostic test for DVT. Sensitivity and specificity for proximal DVT are approximately 95% each. Sensitivity for calf DVT is approximately 70%.

Doppler ultrasound is non-invasive and widely available. Venography is the gold standard but is invasive, expensive, and higher risk. For calf DVT, sensitivity is lower (~70%), meaning repeat imaging may be needed if initial study is negative but clinical suspicion persists.

**Q:** When is a D-dimer test useful in the evaluation of DVT?

**A:** D-dimer is only useful to RULE OUT DVT if the result is NEGATIVE in a patient with LOW clinical suspicion (Modified Wells' Pre-test Probability <1) and no other acute medical issues. A positive D-dimer is non-specific and does not confirm DVT.

D-dimer has high sensitivity but low specificity for DVT. It is elevated in many conditions (infection, trauma, malignancy, pregnancy). Its negative predictive value is high in low-probability patients, making it useful to rule out disease and reduce unnecessary ultrasounds.

**Q:** List the components of the Wells Score for predicting DVT.

**A:** 1. Paralysis, paresis, or recent orthopedic casting of lower extremity (+1)
2. Recently bedridden ≥3 days or major surgery within 12 weeks (+1)
3. Local tenderness along deep venous system (+1)
4. Swelling of entire leg (+1)
5. Calf swelling >3 cm compared to the other leg (+1)
6. Pitting edema greater in symptomatic leg (+1)
7. Collateral non-varicose superficial veins (+1)
8. Active cancer or treated within 6 months (+1)
9. Alternative diagnosis more likely than DVT (e.g., Baker's cyst, cellulitis) (-2)
Total: ≥3 High probability; 1-2 Moderate probability; ≤0 Low probability

The Wells Score stratifies pre-test probability of DVT to guide imaging and D-dimer use. A Modified Wells Score adds 1 additional point for a history of DVT or major surgery. Patients with 'DVT unlikely' score (≤1 on Modified Wells) can have D-dimer ordered to fully rule out DVT, reducing unnecessary ultrasounds.

**Q:** What is Post-Thrombotic Syndrome and how is it managed?

**A:** Post-Thrombotic Syndrome (PTS) is the development of chronic venous stasis signs and symptoms secondary to a DVT. Symptoms include pain, venous dilatation, edema, skin pigmentation/changes, and venous ulcers. Clinical severity is assessed with the Villalta score. Management includes extremity elevation, exercise, compression stockings, and skin/ulcer care.

PTS has a large impact on quality of life following DVT. Chronic venous insufficiency results from valvular damage caused by the thrombus. Compression therapy is the cornerstone of treatment to reduce edema and prevent ulceration.

**Q:** What are the first-line DOAC options for initial treatment of VTE, and which require prior parenteral anticoagulation?

**A:** For initial VTE treatment:
- Apixaban or rivaroxaban: can be used ALONE with a loading dose (no parenteral anticoagulation needed)
- Dabigatran or edoxaban: require 5-10 days of parenteral anticoagulation (usually LMWH) PRIOR to initiation
Contraindications for DOACs: during conception/pregnancy and in breastfeeding women.

Apixaban and rivaroxaban have loading doses incorporated into initial dosing regimens, making them monotherapy options for VTE. Dabigatran and edoxaban lack this property and require a bridging period with parenteral therapy to achieve immediate anticoagulation.

**Q:** What are the advantages and disadvantages of LMWH compared to UFH for VTE treatment?

**A:** LMWH advantages: predictable dose response, fixed dosing schedule, no lab monitoring required, <1% risk of HIT, safe outpatient use (including pregnant and cancer patients).
LMWH disadvantages: only partially reversible by protamine, long-term use associated with osteoporosis, subcutaneous injection, renally cleared (may require dose adjustment in renal dysfunction).
UFH advantages: rapidly reversible by protamine.
UFH disadvantages: must monitor aPTT (~2x normal), higher risk of HIT.

LMWH's predictable pharmacokinetics allow fixed weight-based dosing without routine lab monitoring, enabling outpatient therapy. UFH is preferred when rapid reversibility is needed (high bleeding risk, perioperative) and in severe renal failure where LMWH accumulates.

**Q:** What are the recommended durations of anticoagulation therapy for VTE?

**A:** - Provoked VTE with transient risk factor: 3 months
- Provoked VTE with ongoing risk factor: consider indefinite therapy with annual reassessment
- First unprovoked proximal DVT or PE: ≥3 months, consider indefinite therapy based on bleeding vs. thrombotic risk (use HERDOO2 or DASH scores)
- Second unprovoked VTE: consider indefinite therapy if not already on it
- Cancer-associated DVT: consider indefinite therapy for as long as active malignancy (extend 3-6 months post last treatment if in remission)

Duration depends on the balance between risk of recurrence (higher with unprovoked or ongoing risk factors) and risk of bleeding from anticoagulation. Annual reassessment is recommended for patients on indefinite therapy.

**Q:** Why must warfarin be overlapped with heparin for at least 48 hours when initiating anticoagulation, and what INR target is used?

**A:** Warfarin must overlap with heparin for at least 48 hours with INR >2 because warfarin initially causes a PROTHROMBOTIC state by inhibiting natural anticoagulants protein C and S (which have shorter half-lives than procoagulant factors). The INR target is 2-3. Heparin is discontinued after INR >2.0 for 2 consecutive days.

Warfarin inhibits vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X, protein C, S). Factor VII has the shortest half-life, causing early INR prolongation, but full antithrombotic effect requires reduction of factors II, IX, and X (~4 days). Initial reduction of protein C/S causes a transient hypercoagulable state, risking warfarin-induced skin necrosis.

**Q:** What are the indications and important considerations for IVC filters in VTE management?

**A:** Temporary IVC filter is indicated ONLY for: acute VTE (<4 weeks) with significant contraindications to anticoagulant therapy (i.e., active bleeding) OR when anticoagulation must be interrupted (e.g., urgent surgery). The filter MUST be retrieved once it is safe to do so, to prevent long-term complications including migration, thrombosis, IVC penetration, and filter fracture.

IVC filters reduce the risk of PE but do not treat the underlying thrombosis. They are associated with significant long-term complications if left in place. Once anticoagulation can be safely resumed, retrieval is essential.

**Q:** How should anticoagulation be managed during pregnancy for VTE?

**A:** - Use LMWH during pregnancy, then LMWH or warfarin for 6 weeks post-partum (minimum total anticoagulation of 3-6 months, must include 6 weeks post-partum)
- Avoid warfarin in pregnancy due to teratogenicity (may be used by some experts in 2nd/3rd trimesters for mechanical heart valves)
- Avoid DOACs in conception, pregnancy (lack of data), and while breastfeeding post-partum

The post-partum period (especially 6 weeks) is a high-risk period for thromboembolism, mandating continued anticoagulation. LMWH does not cross the placenta, making it the preferred agent. Warfarin crosses the placenta and causes fetal coagulopathy and embryopathy.

**Q:** What perioperative anticoagulation management is recommended for patients on warfarin or DOACs?

**A:** - Warfarin or DOACs: discontinue at least 2-5 days preoperatively (based on drug clearance)
- IV heparin may be used up to 4-6 hours preoperatively
- For high thromboembolism risk patients (VTE <12 wk, recurrent VTE, APS, AFib with prior stroke, mechanical heart valve): IV heparin or LMWH bridging may be considered before and after procedure while INR <2.0
- Bridging is NOT required for DOACs
- Avoid elective surgery in first 3 months after VTE event

Bridging anticoagulation aims to minimize the window of subtherapeutic anticoagulation in high-risk patients. DOACs have short half-lives and predictable offset, so bridging is unnecessary—it only increases bleeding risk.

**Q:** What is the definition of Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) and what percentage of blast cells is required for diagnosis?

**A:** AML is a rapidly progressive malignancy characterized by failure of myeloid cells to differentiate beyond the blast stage. Diagnosis (WHO definition) requires ≥20% blast cells in the peripheral blood or bone marrow at presentation.

The 20% blast threshold distinguishes AML from myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS). AML accounts for 80% of acute adult leukemias with a median age of onset of 65 years. The inability of blasts to differentiate leads to suppression of normal hematopoiesis.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for AML?

**A:** Risk factors for AML include: male sex, older age, smoking, obesity, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), benzene exposure, radiation exposure, Down syndrome, prior alkylating agent therapy, and radiation therapy as treatment for a previous malignancy.

Secondary AML arising from prior hematologic malignancies (MDS, myeloproliferative disorders) or from prior chemotherapeutic agents (especially alkylating agents) generally carries a worse prognosis than de novo AML. Down syndrome (trisomy 21) increases leukemia risk due to GATA1 mutations.

**Q:** What is the pathognomonic finding of AML on peripheral blood film?

**A:** Auer rods (azurophilic granules) in circulating blasts are pathognomonic for AML.

Auer rods are crystalline lysosomal granules that appear as needle-like pink inclusions within myeloblasts on Wright-Giemsa stain. Their presence definitively identifies the blast as myeloid in origin, distinguishing AML from ALL. They are particularly associated with acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL).

**Q:** What are the clinical manifestations of leukostasis/hyperleukocytosis syndrome in AML?

**A:** Leukostasis is a medical emergency in AML where large numbers of blasts interfere with microcirculation causing hypoxia and hemorrhage. Manifestations include: diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, CNS bleeding, respiratory distress, altered mental status, and priapism. It is more commonly associated with AML than ALL.

Leukostasis occurs when extremely elevated WBC counts (often >100 x 10⁹/L) cause sludging in small vessels. Blasts are larger and less deformable than mature cells, further impairing flow. Immediate cytoreductive therapy with hydroxyurea is required.

**Q:** What metabolic complications are associated with AML and its treatment (Tumor Lysis Syndrome)?

**A:** Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS) features:
- Increased uric acid → nephropathy and gout
- Released phosphate → decreased calcium and magnesium
- Released procoagulants → DIC (higher risk in APL)
- Hyperkalemia (from blastic proliferation and spontaneous TLS pre-treatment, further worsened post-treatment from lysed cells)
- Note: some AML forms present with hypokalemia due to muramidase secretion causing renal tubular K⁺ wasting

TLS results from rapid destruction of tumor cells releasing intracellular contents. The electrolyte derangements (hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, hyperkalemia) can cause renal failure, cardiac arrhythmias, and seizures. Prevention with allopurinol or rasburicase is critical before and during induction.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for Acute Promyelocytic Leukemia (APL) and why is it an emergency?

**A:** APL is an emergency because DIC is often present at diagnosis. Treatment approach:
- ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid) should be started ASAP if APL is in the differential; ATRA induces differentiation
- ATRA + arsenic trioxide combination is SUPERIOR to traditional chemotherapy for APL
- Supportive transfusions: target platelet count >30 (no bleeding) or >50 (with bleeding) and fibrinogen >1.5
- APL has (15;17) translocation (different from other AML subtypes in treatment approach)

APL arises from t(15;17) producing PML-RARα fusion protein that blocks myeloid differentiation at the promyelocyte stage. ATRA targets the RARα component to force differentiation of malignant promyelocytes. Coagulopathy (DIC) from release of procoagulants from azurophilic granules is life-threatening.

**Q:** What is the prognosis for AML and what factors determine it?

**A:** AML prognosis:
- Achievement of first remission: 70-80% if <60 years old; 50% if >60 years old
- Median survival: 12-24 months
Prognostic factors: cytogenetics (most important), age, performance status, prior cytotoxic agents or radiation therapy. Patients with poor response to initial induction have worse prognosis.

Cytogenetics is the most critical prognostic factor in AML. Favorable cytogenetics (e.g., t(8;21), inv(16), t(15;17)) confer better outcomes. Adverse cytogenetics (e.g., monosomy 5/7, complex karyotype) are associated with high-risk disease. Complete cytogenetic analysis is essential at diagnosis for risk stratification.

**Q:** What is Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS) and what are its key peripheral blood film findings?

**A:** MDS is a heterogeneous group of malignant stem cell disorders characterized by dysplastic and ineffective blood cell production resulting in peripheral cytopenias with variable risk of transformation to acute leukemia.
Peripheral blood film:
- RBCs: usually macrocytic, oval-shaped (macro-ovalocytes), decreased reticulocyte count
- WBCs: decreased granulocytes with abnormal morphology (e.g., bi-lobed or unsegmented nuclei = Pelger abnormality)
- Platelets: thrombocytopenia with abnormalities of size and cytoplasm (e.g., giant hypogranular platelets)

Despite having adequate progenitor cells in the bone marrow (often hypercellular), MDS is characterized by intramedullary apoptosis and disordered maturation, leading to peripheral cytopenias. The bone marrow produces cells that look abnormal (dysplastic) and function poorly.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for low-risk vs. high-risk Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS)?

**A:** Low-risk MDS (IPSS-R Very Low or Low):
- EPO-stimulating agents (first-line to reduce transfusion requirements; EPO level must be <500 IU/L)
- If 5q deletion: lenalidomide PO
- If ringed sideroblasts: luspatercept (after failing EPO)
- Supportive care: RBC and platelet transfusion (iron chelation if frequent RBC transfusions)

High-risk MDS (IPSS-R Intermediate, High, or Very High):
- Supportive care (transfusion)
- Epigenetic therapy: DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (e.g., 5-azacitidine)
- Consider stem cell transplantation (based on age, frailty, overall health)

The IPSS-R (Revised International Prognostic Scoring System) uses cytology, % BM blasts, Hb, platelets, and ANC to categorize risk. The 5q deletion syndrome is uniquely sensitive to lenalidomide. High-risk MDS benefits from hypomethylating agents which epigenetically re-activate tumor suppressor genes.

**Q:** What are the Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (MPNs) and how do they differ from MDS?

**A:** Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (MPNs) include: CML, Polycythemia Vera (PV), Myelofibrosis (MF), and Essential Thrombocythemia (ET). They are clonal myeloid stem cell abnormalities leading to OVERPRODUCTION of one or more cell lines.

Key distinction:
- MDS = ineffective maturation (peripheral cytopenias despite hypercellular BM)
- MPNs = overproduction of mature cells

MPNs share overlapping features (splenomegaly, potential progression to AML) but differ in which cell line is predominantly affected. CML overproduces granulocytes, PV overproduces erythrocytes, ET overproduces platelets, and MF features BM fibrosis with extramedullary hematopoiesis.

**Q:** What is the genetic basis of Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML) and how does it drive the disease?

**A:** CML is caused by the Philadelphia (Ph) chromosome: a translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 [t(9;22)]. The c-Abl proto-oncogene from chromosome 9 is translocated to the 'breakpoint cluster region' (BCR) of chromosome 22, producing the BCR-ABL fusion gene. BCR-ABL is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives uncontrolled granulocytic proliferation.

The t(9;22) translocation is necessary and sufficient to cause CML. BCR-ABL constitutively activates downstream signaling pathways (RAS, MAPK, PI3K/AKT) promoting proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. This makes BCR-ABL the molecular target of imatinib and other TKIs.

**Q:** Describe the three clinical phases of CML and their blast counts.

**A:** 1. Chronic phase (85% diagnosed here): <10% blasts in peripheral blood; few symptoms, slightly elevated eosinophils and basophils
2. Accelerated phase: 10-20% circulating blasts; increasing peripheral basophils (causing pruritus), thrombocytopenia <100 × 10⁹/L or thrombocytosis, cytogenetic clonal evolution, worsening constitutional symptoms and splenomegaly
3. Blast crisis (acute phase): ≥20% blasts in peripheral blood or BM; behaves like acute leukemia (1/3 ALL-like, 2/3 AML-like)

Most CML is diagnosed in chronic phase (often incidentally). Without treatment, CML progresses through accelerated phase to blast crisis within 3-5 years. Blast crisis carries poor prognosis and is largely unresponsive to remission induction. TKIs have dramatically changed the natural history of CML.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of CML at presentation?

**A:** 20-50% of patients are asymptomatic (incidental lab finding). Symptomatic patients may have:
- Nonspecific: fatigue, weight loss, malaise, excessive sweating, fever
- Splenic involvement: early satiety, LUQ pain/fullness, shoulder tip pain (referred pain from spleen)
- Splenomegaly (most common physical finding)
- Anemia
- Bleeding (platelet dysfunction)
- Pruritus, PUD (from increased blood histamine via basophils)
- Rarely: leukostasis, priapism, encephalopathy (very elevated WBC)

Splenomegaly occurs due to extramedullary hematopoiesis in CML. Basophilia (characteristic of CML and uncommon in other conditions) releases histamine causing pruritus and peptic ulceration. The bimodal distribution on WBC differential (myelocytes and neutrophils predominating) is characteristic.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for CML in chronic phase, and what are second/third-line options?

**A:** First-line: Imatinib mesylate (BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor); 2nd/3rd generation TKIs (dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib) can also be used as first-line.
If loss of response or intolerance (~40%): switch to 2nd or 3rd generation TKI (dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib).
Ponatinib: only for T315I mutation (resistant to other TKIs).
Interferon-α: may improve response; now mainly used in pregnant patients.
Hydroxyurea: palliative setting to reduce WBC count.
Allogeneic stem cell transplant: potentially curative in young patients not meeting therapeutic milestones.

Imatinib revolutionized CML treatment by specifically targeting the constitutively active BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase. 11-year follow-up data shows complete cytogenetic response in 83% of imatinib-treated patients with >90% overall survival in complete responders. The T315I gatekeeper mutation confers resistance to all TKIs except ponatinib.

**Q:** What therapeutic milestones are used to monitor CML treatment success?

**A:** Treatment milestones for CML monitoring:
1. Hematologic: improved WBC and platelet counts, reduced basophils
2. Cytogenetic: undetectable Ph chromosome in the bone marrow
3. Molecular: reduction/absence of BCR-ABL transcripts in periphery and marrow

Prognosis: patients achieving complete cytogenetic response (CCR) on imatinib by 18 months have 6-year overall survival >90%; those who do NOT achieve CCR have 6-year overall survival of 66%.

Response monitoring in CML follows a hierarchy from hematologic → cytogenetic → molecular response. Molecular monitoring by quantitative PCR for BCR-ABL transcripts is the most sensitive method. Failure to achieve milestones at defined time points (3, 6, 12, 18 months) should prompt switch of TKI.

**Q:** What are the WHO 2022 diagnostic criteria for Polycythemia Vera (PV)?

**A:** PV requires all 3 major criteria OR first 2 major + the minor criterion:

Major Criteria:
1. Hb >165 g/L in men or >160 g/L in women, OR Hct >49% in men or >48% in women
2. BM biopsy showing hypercellularity with trilineage growth (panmyelosis): erythroid, granulocytic, and megakaryocytic proliferation
3. Presence of JAK2 V617F or JAK2 exon 12 mutation

Minor Criterion:
1. Serum EPO level below reference range (must have first two major criteria if using EPO level)

JAK2 V617F mutation is present in >95% of PV cases. It causes constitutive activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, leading to EPO-independent erythropoiesis. The low EPO level distinguishes primary (PV) from secondary polycythemia (where EPO is elevated).

**Q:** What are the clinical features and complications of Polycythemia Vera (PV)?

**A:** Complications from high red cell mass and hyperviscosity:
- Thrombotic: DVT, PE, Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis), portal vein thrombosis, stroke/TIA, MI
- Bleeding: epistaxis, gingival bleeding, ecchymoses, GI bleeding (acquired VWD with high platelet counts)
- Erythromelalgia: burning pain in hands/feet with erythema (pathognomonic microvascular complication in PV and ET)
- Pruritus after warm bath/shower (40%): cutaneous mast cell degranulation and histamine release
- PUD: histamine from tissue basophils, altered gastric mucosal blood flow
- Gout: hyperuricemia from increased cell turnover
- Plethora (ruddy complexion, 70%), splenomegaly (70%), hepatomegaly (40%)

Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis) should raise suspicion for an underlying MPN, especially PV. Erythromelalgia is treated with low-dose ASA. Pruritus in PV is aquagenic (triggered by water contact) and is distinct from other causes of pruritus.

**Q:** What is the treatment for Polycythemia Vera (PV)?

**A:** 1. Phlebotomy: to keep hematocrit <45% (target supported by RCT showing lower thrombotic risk)
2. Hydroxyurea: for prior thrombosis, symptoms, severe CAD, refractory to phlebotomy, or age >60
3. Ruxolitinib (JAK2 inhibitor): for insufficient response or intolerance to hydroxyurea
4. Interferon-α: for cytoreduction in pregnancy
5. Low-dose ASA: for antithrombotic prophylaxis and treatment of erythromelalgia
6. Anticoagulation: if history of thrombosis
7. Allopurinol: as needed for hyperuricemia
8. Antihistamines: as needed for pruritus

RCT data demonstrates that maintaining Hct <45% significantly reduces thrombotic events compared to Hct 45-50% (HR 3.91 for primary thrombotic outcomes with higher Hct). Low-dose ASA (100 mg daily) reduces thrombotic complications without significantly increasing major bleeding in PV.

**Q:** What is Myelofibrosis and what are its characteristic blood film findings?

**A:** Myelofibrosis (MF) is excessive BM fibrosis leading to marrow failure. Characterized by: anemia, extramedullary hematopoiesis, leukoerythroblastosis, teardrop red cells (dacrocytes) in peripheral blood, and hepatosplenomegaly.

Characteristic peripheral blood film: leukoerythroblastosis (immature RBCs and granulocytes), teardrop RBCs (dacrocytes), nucleated RBCs, variable polychromasia, large platelets, megakaryocyte fragments.

Note: BM aspirate gives a 'dry tap' in up to 50% of patients.

Teardrop RBCs (dacrocytes) are deformed by fibrotic marrow stroma during attempted passage into sinusoids. The leukoerythroblastic picture reflects premature release of hematopoietic precursors due to marrow failure—this pattern also suggests BM infiltration by solid tumour metastases or fibrosis.

**Q:** What are the WHO 2022 major diagnostic criteria for Primary Myelofibrosis (MF)?

**A:** Major Criteria (all 3 required):
1. Megakaryocyte proliferation and atypia accompanied by reticulin and/or collagen fibrosis grade 2 or 3
2. Not meeting WHO criteria for CML, PV, ET, MDS, or other myeloid neoplasm
3. Presence of a clonal marker (JAK2, CALR, or MPL mutations); if absent, presence of another marker (ASXL1, EZH2, TET2, IDH, SRSF2, SF3B1), or absence of reactive causes of BM fibrosis

Minor Criteria (≥1 required):
1. Anemia
2. Leukocytosis
3. Palpable splenomegaly
4. Increased LDH

JAK2 mutation is present in ~70% of MF, CALR in ~25%, and MPL in 5-10%. Reactive BM fibrosis (from infections, autoimmune disease, granulomatous diseases) must be excluded before diagnosing primary MF. The fibrosis grade (0-3) correlates with prognosis.

**Q:** What is the treatment for Myelofibrosis and which patients should receive allogeneic stem cell transplantation?

**A:** Treatment options:
- Allogeneic stem cell transplant: only potentially curative; for HIGH-risk patients (DIPSS Intermediate-2 or High risk)
- JAK2 inhibitors: ruxolitinib, fedratinib (reduce splenomegaly and symptoms)
- Transfusion for anemia
- EPO: 30-50% respond
- Androgens (e.g., danazol): anemia (<30% response rate)
- Hydroxyurea: splenomegaly, thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, systemic symptoms
- Interferon-α: second-line
- Splenectomy: third-line (high morbidity/mortality)
- Radiation for symptomatic extramedullary hematopoiesis

RCT data shows ruxolitinib significantly reduces spleen volume >35% (42% vs. 0.1% placebo), improves symptoms (45% vs. 5%), and reduces mortality compared to placebo. Only allogeneic SCT offers potential cure, reserved for eligible high-risk patients given its transplant-related mortality.

**Q:** What is the DIPSS Plus scoring system for Myelofibrosis prognosis?

**A:** DIPSS Plus uses 5 clinical risk factors + karyotype + platelet count + transfusion status:
5 Risk factors:
1. Constitutional symptoms
2. Age >65 years
3. Hb <100 g/L
4. Leukocyte count >25,000/mm³
5. Circulating blast cells ≥1%

Risk categories and mean survival:
- Low: 185 months (~15.4 yr)
- Intermediate-1: 78 months (~6.5 yr)
- Intermediate-2: 35 months (~2.9 yr)
- High: 16 months (~1.3 yr)

Intermediate-2 or High risk: considered for allogeneic stem cell transplant.

DIPSS Plus improves on DIPSS by incorporating adverse karyotype, platelet count <100 x 10⁹/L, and RBC transfusion need. Risk of transformation to AML is 8-10%. Early identification of high-risk patients allows timely referral for SCT evaluation.

**Q:** What are the WHO 2022 diagnostic criteria for Essential Thrombocythemia (ET)?

**A:** ET diagnosis requires all 4 major criteria OR first 3 major + minor criterion:

Major Criteria:
1. Sustained platelet count ≥450 × 10⁹/L
2. BM biopsy showing proliferation mainly of megakaryocytic lineage with increased, enlarged, mature megakaryocytes with hyperlobulated nuclei; no significant increase or left shift of neutrophil or erythroid granulopoiesis
3. Not meeting WHO criteria for PV, primary MF, BCR-ABL CML, MDS, or other myeloid neoplasm
4. Presence of JAK2, CALR, or MPL mutation

Minor Criterion:
1. Presence of a clonal marker or exclusion of reactive thrombocytosis

Reactive thrombocytosis (from inflammation, iron deficiency, hemolytic anemia, post-splenectomy, malignancy) must be excluded before diagnosing ET. Unlike PV where JAK2 V617F predominates, ET shows more heterogeneous mutations: JAK2 (50%), CALR (30%), MPL (5-10%).

**Q:** What are the clinical features and complications of Essential Thrombocythemia (ET)?

**A:** ET is often asymptomatic. Clinical features:
- Vasomotor symptoms (40%): headache, dizziness, syncope
- Erythromelalgia: burning pain of hands and feet, dusky colour, worse with heat (caused by platelet activation → microvascular thrombosis)
- Thrombosis (arterial and venous)
- Bleeding: acquired VWD associated with platelets >1000 × 10⁹/L
- Constitutional symptoms, splenomegaly
- Pregnancy complications: increased risk of spontaneous abortion
- Risk of transformation to AML (0.6-5%) and myelofibrosis

Erythromelalgia is pathognomonic microvascular thrombotic complication in both PV and ET. Paradoxically, very high platelet counts (>1000 × 10⁹/L) cause bleeding via acquired von Willebrand disease (platelets absorb large VWF multimers, depleting them from circulation).

**Q:** What is the treatment for Essential Thrombocythemia (ET)?

**A:** - Low-dose ASA: for all patients without contraindications
- Cytoreductive therapy for thrombosis or thrombotic symptoms:
  - First-line: hydroxyurea (HU)
  - Second-line: anagrelide or interferon-α
- Asymptomatic 'benign' form with stable slowly rising platelet count: observation, ASA, sulfinpyrazone

ASA reduces thrombotic risk by inhibiting platelet cyclo-oxygenase and treating erythromelalgia. Hydroxyurea is cytoreductive first-line for high-risk ET (prior thrombosis, age >60, cardiovascular risk factors). Anagrelide selectively reduces platelet production but has more side effects (fluid retention, cardiac). Interferon-α is preferred in younger patients and pregnancy.

**Q:** What is Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) and how does it differ in pediatric vs. adult populations?

**A:** ALL is a malignant disease of the BM in which early lymphoid precursors proliferate and replace normal hematopoietic cells. WHO classifies ALL by cell of origin: B-cell (precursor B lymphoblastic) or T-cell (precursor T lymphoblastic).

Pediatric ALL (childhood): 75% long-term remission (>5 yr); bimodal peak at age 2-5 and age 40; higher cure rates due to better chemotherapy tolerance and lower prevalence of BCR-ABL fusion gene.

Adult ALL: 30-40% 5-year survival; achievement of first remission: 60-90%; Ph chromosome in ~25% of adult ALL cases (associated with chemoresistance).

BCR-ABL positive ALL (Ph+ ALL) is more common in adults and confers worse prognosis due to chemoresistance. Adding TKIs (imatinib, dasatinib) to chemotherapy significantly improves outcomes in Ph+ ALL. CAR-T cell therapy targets CD19 in relapsed/refractory ALL.

**Q:** What investigations are mandatory when evaluating a patient with suspected ALL?

**A:** 1. BM aspirate: >20% lymphoblasts with flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and molecular studies
2. CBC: often leukocytes >100 × 10⁹/L (50%), with neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia
3. TLS screen: uric acid, K⁺, PO₄²⁻, Ca²⁺, LDH
4. DIC screen: PT, aPTT, fibrinogen
5. CXR: mediastinal mass (especially T-cell ALL)
6. CT C/A/P and testicular ultrasound (extranodal disease)
7. Mandatory lumbar puncture: to assess CNS involvement (after blasts cleared from peripheral blood with adequate platelet count and PT/PTT)
8. Serologies: HIV, HBV, HCV, CMV antibody testing
9. Ph chromosome testing: in ~25% of adult ALL

Mediastinal mass on CXR is characteristic of T-cell ALL due to thymic involvement. CNS involvement is common in ALL (rare in AML) and requires intrathecal prophylaxis/treatment. LP should be delayed until blasts are cleared from peripheral blood to avoid inadvertent CNS seeding.

**Q:** What are the four phases of ALL treatment and their goals?

**A:** 1. Induction chemotherapy: induce complete remission (<5% blasts) and restore normal hematopoiesis
2. Consolidation and/or intensification: eliminate subclinical leukemic cells
   - Consolidation: same chemotherapy
   - Intensification: high doses of different drugs to overcome resistance
3. Maintenance chemotherapy: low-dose intermittent chemotherapy over prolonged period (1 year) to prevent relapse
4. CNS prophylaxis: intrathecal or systemic methotrexate/cytarabine/dexamethasone (CNS radiation no longer used for prophylaxis)

For relapsed/refractory: CAR T-cell therapy, inotuzumab, or blinatumomab

Unlike AML, maintenance chemotherapy has proven benefit in ALL (no proven benefit in AML). CNS prophylaxis is mandatory in ALL due to high rates of CNS involvement (CNS is a 'sanctuary site' protected by the blood-brain barrier from systemic chemotherapy). Radiation is avoided due to long-term neurocognitive effects.

**Q:** What is CAR T-cell therapy and what are its current approved indications in hematology?

**A:** CAR T-cell therapy involves: extracting the patient's own T-cells, genetically modifying them to produce Chimeric Antigen Receptors (CARs) that recognize antigens selectively presented on cancer cell surfaces (often CD19 in ALL), then reinfusing them.

Most effective for:
- Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)
- CD19-positive ALL

First approved in Canada in 2018. Currently of limited utility for other hematologic malignancies and solid tumours.

CARs consist of an extracellular antigen-binding domain (derived from antibody) fused to intracellular T-cell signaling domains. CD19 is expressed on most B-cell malignancies but not on normal stem cells, providing a therapeutic window. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity are major adverse effects.

**Q:** What good prognostic factors are associated with ALL?

**A:** Good prognostic factors in ALL:
- Young age (childhood better than adult)
- WBC <30 × 10⁹/L at presentation
- T-cell phenotype
- Absence of Ph chromosome (BCR-ABL negative)
- Early attainment of complete remission

Poor prognostic factors: older age, high WBC, B-cell phenotype with adverse cytogenetics, Ph chromosome positive, failure to achieve complete remission.

Ph chromosome (BCR-ABL) in ALL confers chemoresistance. However, adding TKIs (imatinib or dasatinib) to chemotherapy has significantly improved outcomes in Ph+ ALL. T-cell ALL paradoxically has better prognosis than B-cell ALL in adults despite presenting with high WBC and mediastinal mass.

**Q:** What mandatory initial workup must be completed for ALL hematologic malignancies before starting treatment?

**A:** 1. ALL WOMEN OF CHILDBEARING AGE: must have β-HCG (pregnancy test) before initiating any cancer treatment
2. ALL PATIENTS: must have Hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb), Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and Hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb)—regardless of cancer diagnosis—to prevent HBV reactivation during immunosuppressive treatment
3. All aggressive lymphoma patients: must be screened for HIV
4. All patients: must be screened for TB risk factors

HBV reactivation can be fatal during immunosuppressive chemotherapy. Even patients with 'resolved' HBV (HBcAb positive, HBsAg negative) can reactivate. Antiviral prophylaxis (entecavir) is required for HBsAg-positive patients. HIV increases risk of opportunistic infections during chemotherapy.

**Q:** What genetic mutations are associated with the different myeloproliferative neoplasms?

**A:** - CML: BCR-ABL mutation (t(9;22), Philadelphia chromosome) — diagnostic, 100%
- Polycythemia Vera (PV): JAK2 V617F or JAK2 exon 12 — present in ~96% (JAK2 V617F ~95%)
- Myelofibrosis (MF): JAK2 (~70%), CALR (~25%), MPL (5-10%)
- Essential Thrombocythemia (ET): JAK2 (~50%), CALR (~30%), MPL (~5-10%)

Key: BCR-ABL is specific to CML; JAK2/CALR/MPL are found in the non-CML MPNs (PV, MF, ET)

JAK2 mutations cause constitutive activation of JAK-STAT signaling pathway, promoting cytokine-independent proliferation. CALR (calreticulin) mutations activate MPL (thrombopoietin receptor). Testing for BCR-ABL first excludes CML, then JAK2/CALR/MPL testing is performed for the non-CML MPNs.

**Q:** What is erythromelalgia and in which conditions is it pathognomonic?

**A:** Erythromelalgia is a pathognomonic microvascular thrombotic complication characterized by burning pain in the hands and feet with erythema (dusky colour), usually worse with heat. It is caused by platelet activation leading to microvascular thrombosis. It is pathognomonic for Polycythemia Vera (PV) and Essential Thrombocythemia (ET), where it is associated with platelets >400 × 10⁹/L. Treatment: low-dose ASA.

Erythromelalgia results from platelet-mediated arteriolar inflammation and occlusion. The dramatic response to aspirin (within hours) is characteristic and can itself be diagnostic. It should not be confused with erythema in other contexts—the burning quality and ASA responsiveness are key distinguishing features.

**Q:** Why does warfarin use for VTE require overlap with heparin for 2 days, and what is the risk if this is not done?

**A:** Warfarin has an initial PROTHROMBOTIC effect because:
1. Protein C and S (natural anticoagulants) have shorter half-lives than procoagulant factors and are inhibited first
2. A 10 mg loading dose of warfarin causes a precipitous decline in protein C levels, creating a hypercoagulable state
3. Factor VII levels fall first (shortest half-life), prolonging the INR, but full antithrombotic effect requires reduction of factors IX, X, and II (~4 days)

Risk without heparin overlap: warfarin-induced skin necrosis (from protein C/S depletion causing microvascular thrombosis in skin) and inadequate anticoagulation during initial period.

Warfarin-induced skin necrosis is a rare but serious complication, most common in patients with protein C or S deficiency. It manifests as painful necrotic skin lesions, typically over fatty areas (breasts, buttocks, thighs). Heparin provides immediate anticoagulation coverage while waiting for the anticoagulant effect of warfarin to develop.

**Q:** What is the significance of basophilia in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML) and what symptoms does it cause?

**A:** Basophilia is characteristic of CML and is uncommon in other medical conditions. Increasing peripheral basophils is a feature of the ACCELERATED PHASE of CML. Basophils release histamine, causing:
- Pruritus
- Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)
Basophils releasing large amounts of histamine can also rarely cause systemic symptoms. Basophilia (alongside leukocytosis) helps differentiate the leukoerythroblastic picture of CML from AML.

The presence of basophilia alongside a wide spectrum of maturing granulocytes (bimodal distribution with myelocytes and neutrophils) on CBC differential is characteristic of CML. In accelerated phase, basophils increase progressively. This contrasts with AML where basophilia is absent and blasts predominate.

**Q:** What are absolute vs. relative contraindications to anticoagulant therapy?

**A:** Absolute Contraindications:
- Active bleeding
- Severe bleeding diathesis or platelet count <20 × 10⁹/L (<20,000/mm³)
- Major abdominal surgery within past 2 days
- GI/GU bleeding within 14 days
- Endocarditis

Relative Contraindications:
- Mild-moderate bleeding diathesis or thrombocytopenia
- Recent major trauma
- Recent stroke

In patients with absolute contraindications to anticoagulation who have active VTE, temporary IVC filters are an alternative to prevent PE. Once contraindications resolve, anticoagulation should be initiated promptly. The risk-benefit calculation must be individualized.

**Q:** What is the approach to cancer-associated thrombosis management according to Thrombosis Canada guidelines?

**A:** - DOACs (apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) and LMWH are effective for most cancer-associated thrombosis
- LMWH more effective than warfarin at preventing recurrence in cancer patients
- DOACs as effective as LMWH overall (with more bleeding observed for GI cancer patients on rivaroxaban or edoxaban)
- Exercise caution with DOACs in: primary or metastatic brain lesions, hematological malignancies, GI/GU tumours (not well-studied)
- Cancer-associated DVT: consider indefinite therapy while active malignancy present; usually 3-6 months post last treatment if in remission
- Thorough review of relative risks, patient preference, and values required before prescribing

The CLOT trial established LMWH superiority over warfarin in cancer-associated thrombosis. More recent trials (Hokusai VTE Cancer, SELECT-D) demonstrated non-inferiority of DOACs with increased mucosal bleeding in GI cancers. Thromboprophylaxis (apixaban, rivaroxaban, or LMWH) is recommended for high-risk ambulatory cancer patients receiving chemotherapy.

**Q:** What is the MDS IPSS-R scoring system and what are its prognostic categories?

**A:** IPSS-R (Revised International Prognostic Scoring System) uses 5 factors:
1. Cytology (dysplasia)
2. % BM blasts
3. Hemoglobin
4. Platelet count
5. Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC)

Prognostic categories and mean survival:
- Very Low: 8.7 years
- Low: 5.3 years
- Intermediate: 3.0 years
- High: 1.6 years
- Very High: 0.8 years

Note: IPSS-M integrates mutational analysis for better prognostic information.

IPSS-R replaced the original IPSS and provides more refined risk stratification. Cytogenetics has been expanded in IPSS-R with 5 cytogenetic risk groups. High-risk cytogenetics (partial or total loss of chromosome 7) and complex karyotype (>3 abnormalities) confer worst prognosis.

**Q:** What are the key differences between AML and ALL that help distinguish them clinically and on investigations?

**A:** AML vs. ALL distinguishing features:
- Age: AML peak in elderly (median 65 yr, 80% of adult acute leukemia); ALL peak in children (bimodal: age 2-5 and 40)
- Peripheral blood film: Auer rods = pathognomonic for AML (absent in ALL)
- Mediastinal mass on CXR: ALL (especially T-cell), NOT AML
- CNS involvement requiring prophylaxis: ALL (mandatory LP), not routine in AML
- Maintenance chemotherapy: proven benefit in ALL, NO proven benefit in AML
- Ph chromosome: AML rare; ALL ~25% of adults
- DIC: high risk with APL (AML subtype); less common in ALL

The correct classification (AML vs. ALL) is critical as treatment protocols differ substantially. Immunophenotyping by flow cytometry is essential for definitive lineage determination. Auer rods immediately identify myeloid origin. The presence of a mediastinal mass in a young patient with leukemia strongly suggests T-cell ALL.

**Q:** What are the features of gingival hypertrophy in AML and what subtype is most associated with it?

**A:** Gingival hypertrophy (gingival infiltration with leukemic blasts) is particularly associated with myelomonocytic leukemia (AML-M4 and monocytic AML-M5 in the older FAB classification). Patients may present to the dentist first before the diagnosis of leukemia is established. It results from organ infiltration by blast cells.

Gingival hypertrophy from leukemic infiltration is a classic MCCQE1 clinical vignette—a patient presenting to the dentist with bleeding gums and gum swelling, where the underlying diagnosis is acute myelomonocytic leukemia. Other extramedullary manifestations include leukemia cutis, hepatosplenomegaly, and CNS involvement.

**Q:** What is the role of non-pharmacological measures in hospital VTE prophylaxis?

**A:** Non-pharmacological VTE prophylaxis measures:
- Early ambulation
- Elastic compression stockings (TEDs — thrombo-embolic deterrent stockings)
- Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC)

Pharmacological measures (LMWH preferred):
- Enoxaparin 40 mg SC once daily, or dalteparin 5000 U SC once daily
- Rarely: UFH 5000 IU SC BID or TID
- DOACs for orthopedic surgery thromboprophylaxis
- LMWH dose adjustments for patients <40 kg and >100 kg

Mechanical measures (TED stockings, IPC) are used when pharmacological prophylaxis is contraindicated (e.g., active bleeding). IPC is particularly useful perioperatively. Pharmacological prophylaxis should be considered for all hospitalized patients with moderate to high VTE risk without contraindications.

**Q:** What organisms most commonly cause bacterial contamination of blood products, and which product has a higher overall risk?

**A:** Gram-positive (S. aureus, S. epidermidis, B. cereus) and Gram-negative (Klebsiella, Serratia, Pseudomonas, Yersinia). Platelets have a higher risk (1/10,000) compared to RBCs (1/100,000).

Platelets are stored at room temperature, making them more susceptible to bacterial growth. Never store blood >4 hours after leaving the blood bank. Treatment: stop transfusion, blood cultures, IV antibiotics, IV fluids.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of citrate toxicity in massive transfusion, and how is it treated?

**A:** Citrate (a blood preservative) binds free calcium (Ca2+), causing hypocalcemia signs/symptoms and worsening coagulopathy. Occurs with massive transfusion or hepatic failure (impaired citrate clearance). Treatment: IV calcium gluconate 1 g.

The liver normally metabolizes citrate rapidly. In liver disease or with massive transfusion, citrate accumulates and chelates ionized calcium. Hypocalcemia reduces cardiac contractility and worsens clotting factor function.

**Q:** What is dilutional coagulopathy in massive transfusion, and how is it treated?

**A:** Occurs with massive transfusion (>10 units pRBC). pRBC contains no coagulation factors, fibrinogen, or platelets, so large-volume transfusion dilutes these components. Treatment: fresh frozen plasma (FFP), cryoprecipitate, and platelets.

Massive transfusion protocols typically include a ratio of pRBC:FFP:platelets (e.g., 1:1:1) to prevent dilutional coagulopathy. Cryoprecipitate replaces fibrinogen and factor VIII specifically.

**Q:** What is a delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction? When does it occur and what antigens are involved?

**A:** Caused by alloantibodies to minor antigens (Rh, Kell, Duffy, Kidd). Antibody levels at time of transfusion are too low to detect; secondary immune stimulus increases antibody levels causing extravascular hemolysis 3-14 days post-transfusion. Presents with anemia and mild jaundice. No specific treatment required.

This is distinguished from acute hemolytic reactions (ABO incompatibility) by timing and causative antigens. Important to document for future transfusions. Serologic transfusion reactions = alloantibody development without frank hemolysis.

**Q:** What is transfusion-associated graft versus host disease (TA-GvHD)? Who is at risk, when does it occur, and how is it prevented?

**A:** Transfused donor T-lymphocytes recognize and react against the host. Occurs 4-30 days post-transfusion. At-risk patients: severely immunocompromised (e.g., Hodgkin lymphoma, leukemia). Presentation: fever, diarrhea, liver function abnormalities, pancytopenia. Prevention: irradiated blood products.

Unlike GvHD from stem cell transplant, TA-GvHD affects the skin, liver, gut, and bone marrow and is often fatal. Irradiation inactivates donor T-lymphocytes, preventing their proliferation in the host.

**Q:** What are the approximate risks of viral transmission per unit of blood transfused in Canada?

**A:** HBV: 1/7,000,000; HCV: 1/12,000,000; HIV: 1/20,000,000; HTLV: 1/600,000,000. Other infections include EBV, CMV, West Nile Virus.

Modern blood screening has dramatically reduced viral transmission risk. These figures are useful for counselling patients and for MCCQE1 questions about transfusion safety in the Canadian context.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of aspirin (ASA) as an antiplatelet agent, and how long does its effect last?

**A:** Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX, inhibiting thromboxane A2 (TXA2) synthesis, thereby inhibiting platelet aggregation. Effect lasts 7-10 days (lifespan of the platelet). Typical maintenance dose: 75-100 mg PO daily.

Because platelets have no nucleus, they cannot regenerate COX. New platelets must be produced to restore normal aggregation. Aspirin is indicated for stroke/MI prophylaxis and decreases mortality in post-MI patients. Contraindicated with GI ulcers.

**Q:** How does clopidogrel (Plavix) work, and what is a clinically important pharmacogenomic consideration?

**A:** Clopidogrel irreversibly inhibits ADP binding to platelets (P2Y12 receptor), decreasing platelet aggregation. It is a prodrug requiring two-step activation by CYP2C19. CYP2C19 poor metabolizers have diminished antiplatelet response. Effect lasts ~5 days.

CYP2C19 polymorphisms are clinically significant — poor metabolizers may have inadequate antiplatelet effect, increasing cardiovascular risk. Caution with hepatic/renal impairment. May cause TTP (rare but serious).

**Q:** Compare ticagrelor and clopidogrel: mechanism, prodrug status, and key drug interactions.

**A:** Ticagrelor reversibly inhibits ADP binding to P2Y12 platelets. Unlike clopidogrel, it does NOT require metabolic activation (not a prodrug). Higher potency than clopidogrel. Drug-drug interactions with CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers. Key side effect: dyspnea/shortness of breath.

Because ticagrelor is not a prodrug, its effect is more predictable across patients regardless of CYP2C19 status. The dyspnea side effect is not bronchospasm and does not require drug discontinuation in most cases.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa) inhibitors and when are they used?

**A:** Block GPIIb/IIIa receptor, inhibiting fibrinogen and vWF binding, leading to decreased platelet aggregation. Used most commonly during cardiac catheterization. Examples: abciximab (ReoPro), eptifibatide (Integrilin). Contraindicated in PUD. Monitor aPTT/activated clotting time.

GPIIb/IIIa is the final common pathway for platelet aggregation. These agents are given IV in the cardiac catheterization lab for high-risk PCI. Key side effects: hypotension, thrombocytopenia.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of unfractionated heparin (UFH), how is it monitored, and what are its major adverse effects?

**A:** UFH inhibits Factor Xa and Factor IIa (thrombin), mediated via antithrombin. Monitored by aPTT (intrinsic pathway). Reversed with protamine sulfate. Major adverse effects: hemorrhage, HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia), osteoporosis (long-term use). Safe in pregnancy (does not cross placenta).

UFH requires aPTT monitoring to adjust dosing. HIT is a hematologic emergency characterized by paradoxical thrombosis despite thrombocytopenia. Osteoporosis occurs with prolonged use (>1 month).

**Q:** What is warfarin's mechanism, monitoring parameter, and reversal strategy? Why is it not used in pregnancy?

**A:** Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist inhibiting production of Factors II, VII, IX, X, and Proteins C and S. Monitored by INR (target 2-3; 2.5-3.5 for mechanical valves). Reversed by Vitamin K, PCC, FFP. NOT used in pregnancy because it crosses the placenta and is teratogenic.

Onset is delayed (24-72h) because existing clotting factors must be depleted. LMWH is the preferred anticoagulant in pregnancy. Warfarin can cause cholesterol embolism syndrome as a rare complication.

**Q:** What are the advantages of LMWH over UFH?

**A:** LMWH advantages: increased bioavailability, longer duration of action, subcutaneous administration, no aPTT monitoring required, fewer adverse reactions, less common HIT. However: only partially reversible with protamine sulfate and can accumulate in renal failure (CrCl <30 mL/min). Standard treatment of VTE in pregnancy and cancer patients.

LMWH predominantly inhibits Factor Xa with less Factor IIa activity than UFH. Anti-Xa levels may be checked in special populations (pediatrics, extremes of weight, renal insufficiency). Examples: enoxaparin, dalteparin, tinzaparin.

**Q:** What is fondaparinux's mechanism, and why is it contraindicated in renal failure?

**A:** Fondaparinux is a selective Factor Xa inhibitor mediated via antithrombin. Not reversible. Long half-life (17-21 hours). Contraindicated in renal failure because it is renally cleared, leading to drug accumulation and increased bleeding risk.

Fondaparinux is administered SC daily. It has no risk of HIT (no affinity for platelet factor 4). Monitoring is typically not required but no reversal agent exists.

**Q:** Which direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) target Factor Xa vs. Factor IIa (thrombin)? What reversal agent is available for each?

**A:** Factor Xa inhibitors: rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban — reversed by andexanet alfa. Direct thrombin inhibitors: argatroban, dabigatran — dabigatran reversed by idarucizumab; argatroban not reversible.

Andexanet alfa is a recombinant modified Factor Xa that binds and sequesters Factor Xa inhibitors. Idarucizumab is a monoclonal antibody fragment that binds dabigatran with high affinity. All DOACs are contraindicated with mechanical heart valves.

**Q:** A patient on dabigatran requires urgent surgery. What reversal agent is used, and what monitoring confirms drug presence?

**A:** Idarucizumab (Praxbind) is the reversal agent for dabigatran; it normalizes anticoagulation within minutes. Dilute thrombin time (dTT) may be used to detect drug presence; TT is also sensitive. Dabigatran requires CrCl >30 mL/min and should be stored in original packaging.

The RE-VERSE AD trial demonstrated idarucizumab normalized test results in 88-98% of patients within minutes. Dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor approved for VTE treatment (after 5-10 days parenteral therapy), VTE prevention, and stroke prophylaxis in non-valvular AFib.

**Q:** What are the renal function requirements for the DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, dabigatran)?

**A:** Rivaroxaban: CrCl >30 mL/min; Apixaban: CrCl >25 mL/min; Edoxaban: avoid if CrCl <15 mL/min, dose reduce in renal insufficiency; Dabigatran: CrCl >30 mL/min.

DOACs are renally eliminated to varying degrees. Dabigatran has the highest renal clearance (~80%) and is most affected by renal impairment. Apixaban has the least renal dependence. All are contraindicated in mechanical heart valves.

**Q:** What is the recommended management for a supratherapeutic INR >10 without bleeding?

**A:** Hold warfarin and administer oral vitamin K 2.5-5 mg; monitor INR more frequently and administer more vitamin K as needed; resume warfarin at a lower dose when INR returns to therapeutic range.

For INR >4.5 to 10 without bleeding: omit 1-2 doses and monitor, or omit a dose and give oral vitamin K 1-2.5 mg if increased bleeding risk. For any serious/life-threatening bleeding: hold warfarin, IV vitamin K 10 mg slow infusion PLUS four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate.

**Q:** How is a serious life-threatening bleed managed in a patient on warfarin?

**A:** Hold warfarin. Administer IV vitamin K 10 mg by slow IV infusion plus four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). Monitor and repeat as needed.

Four-factor PCC (contains Factors II, VII, IX, X) provides immediate reversal while IV vitamin K ensures sustained reversal over hours. FFP can also be used but requires larger volumes. This applies regardless of the INR level when bleeding is serious or life-threatening.

**Q:** What are the main classes of chemotherapeutic agents and their mechanisms? List key examples.

**A:** Alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide, cisplatin): DNA damage via alkylation/crosslinking. Antimetabolites (methotrexate, 5-FU): inhibit DNA synthesis. Antibiotics (anthracyclines/adriamycin, bleomycin): interfere with DNA/RNA synthesis. Taxanes (paclitaxel): stabilize microtubules. Vinca alkaloids (vincristine, vinblastine): inhibit microtubule assembly. Topoisomerase inhibitors (irinotecan/topo I; etoposide/topo II).

Each class targets different phases of cell division. Alkylating agents and antimetabolites are cell-cycle non-specific and specific, respectively. Understanding mechanisms helps predict toxicities (e.g., bleomycin → pulmonary fibrosis; anthracyclines → cardiomyopathy).

**Q:** What are the targets of the key monoclonal antibodies used in hematologic malignancies?

**A:** Rituximab, ofatumumab, obinutuzumab: CD20 (B-cell lymphomas/CLL). Daratumumab: CD38 (multiple myeloma). Trastuzumab (Herceptin): HER2. Bevacizumab (Avastin): VEGF. Cetuximab (Erbitux): EGFR.

CD20 is expressed on B cells and is absent on plasma cells, which is why anti-CD20 therapies are not effective against plasma cell disorders. Daratumumab targets CD38, which is highly expressed on myeloma cells. Obinutuzumab showed superior PFS vs. rituximab in follicular lymphoma (GALLIUM trial).

**Q:** What are the BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) used in CML, and what is the landmark trial supporting imatinib?

**A:** BCR-ABL TKIs: imatinib (Gleevec), dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib (all BCR-ABL inhibitors). The IRIS trial showed imatinib achieved 83% complete cytogenetic response rate at 6 years; 10-year OS was 93% in patients with major molecular response at 18 months.

Imatinib was the first targeted TKI and transformed CML from a fatal to a chronic manageable disease. Second-generation TKIs (dasatinib, nilotinib) are used for resistance or intolerance. Venetoclax is a Bcl-2 inhibitor (not BCR-ABL).

**Q:** What did the POLLUX trial show about daratumumab in multiple myeloma?

**A:** POLLUX (NEJM 2016): Adding daratumumab to lenalidomide + dexamethasone in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma significantly improved PFS (83.2% vs. 60.1% at 12 months, p<0.001) and overall response rate, but was associated with higher rates of neutropenia (51.9% vs. 37.0%).

Daratumumab targets CD38 on myeloma cells. The improved PFS supports its use in combination regimens for relapsed/refractory MM. Neutropenia is the key additional toxicity to monitor.

**Q:** What did the GALLIUM trial show, and what is the clinical implication for follicular lymphoma treatment?

**A:** GALLIUM (NEJM 2017): Obinutuzumab-based chemotherapy resulted in significantly longer PFS than rituximab-based therapy in previously untreated advanced follicular lymphoma (3-year PFS 80.0% vs. 73.3%, HR 0.66, p=0.001), but was associated with more frequent high-grade adverse events (74.6% vs. 67.8%).

Obinutuzumab is a type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody with enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity compared to rituximab. The benefit must be weighed against the higher toxicity profile.

**Q:** What were the key findings of the TRICC trial regarding transfusion thresholds in critically ill patients?

**A:** TRICC (NEJM 1999): In critically ill patients, a restrictive transfusion strategy (transfuse if Hb <7 g/dL, maintain 7-9 g/dL) was as effective as a liberal strategy (transfuse if Hb <10 g/dL). Among less acutely ill patients and those <55 years, mortality was significantly lower with restrictive strategy (8.7% vs. 16.1%).

This landmark Canadian trial established that restrictive transfusion is safe and may be superior in less critically ill and younger patients. It shifted practice toward avoiding unnecessary transfusions in the ICU setting.

**Q:** What did the CRASH-2 trial demonstrate about tranexamic acid (TXA) in trauma?

**A:** CRASH-2: Early TXA (within 3 hours of injury) significantly reduced all-cause mortality (14.5% vs. 16.0%, p=0.0035) and death from bleeding (4.9% vs. 5.7%) in bleeding trauma patients. Treatment beyond 3 hours was associated with increased risk of death from bleeding.

TXA is an antifibrinolytic that inhibits plasmin activation. The 3-hour window is critical — delayed administration is not only ineffective but potentially harmful. TXA is now standard of care in trauma resuscitation when given early.

**Q:** What did the EINSTEIN-PE trial demonstrate about rivaroxaban for pulmonary embolism treatment?

**A:** EINSTEIN-PE (NEJM 2012): Fixed-dose rivaroxaban was non-inferior to standard therapy (enoxaparin followed by vitamin K antagonist) for acute symptomatic PE with DVT. Primary efficacy (recurrent VTE): 2.1% vs. 1.8%. Clinically relevant bleeding: 10.3% vs. 11.4% (potentially better benefit-risk profile for rivaroxaban).

This trial validated oral rivaroxaban as a single-drug approach for PE treatment, simplifying management by eliminating the need for initial parenteral therapy. Rivaroxaban must be taken with food and requires CrCl >30 mL/min.

**Q:** What did the AMPLIFY trial show about apixaban for acute VTE treatment?

**A:** AMPLIFY (NEJM 2013): Fixed-dose apixaban alone was non-inferior to conventional therapy (enoxaparin followed by warfarin) for acute VTE. Major or clinically relevant non-major bleeding was significantly less with apixaban (4.3% vs. 9.7%, RR 0.44, p<0.001).

Apixaban showed a significant bleeding advantage over conventional therapy while maintaining equivalent efficacy. This supports apixaban as a preferred DOAC for VTE treatment, particularly in patients at higher bleeding risk.

**Q:** What did the CHOIR trial demonstrate about erythropoietin (EPO) target hemoglobin in CKD patients?

**A:** CHOIR (NEJM 2006): In anemic CKD patients, targeting a higher Hb (13.5 g/dL) with epoetin alfa was associated with significantly more composite events (death, MI, CHF hospitalization, stroke) compared to targeting 11.3 g/dL (hazard ratio 1.34, p=0.03). Therefore, a lower Hb target is safer.

This trial led to revised guidelines recommending against targeting near-normal hemoglobin with ESAs in CKD. Current guidelines recommend target Hb of ~10-11.5 g/dL to avoid cardiovascular complications while minimizing transfusion dependence.

**Q:** What was the key finding of the CACTUS trial regarding anticoagulation for symptomatic calf DVT?

**A:** CACTUS (Lancet Haematol 2016): In low-risk outpatients with symptomatic calf DVT (no cancer, no prior VTE), nadroparin (LMWH) was non-superior to placebo in reducing proximal extension or VTE at 6 weeks, but increased bleeding risk (4% vs. 0%). Conclusion: anticoagulation not clearly beneficial in this population.

Isolated calf (distal) DVT has a lower risk of proximal propagation and PE. For low-risk patients, serial ultrasound monitoring may be preferred over anticoagulation. High-risk patients (cancer, prior VTE) still warrant anticoagulation.

**Q:** What is CAR-T cell therapy and what target does tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah) use? What are key results from the CD19 CAR-T trial in ALL?

**A:** CAR-T (Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell) therapy involves engineering autologous T cells to express a receptor targeting a tumor antigen. Tisagenlecleucel targets CD19 (B-cell antigen). In relapsed B-cell ALL: 83% complete remission rate, but median OS was 12.9 months; severe cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurred in 26% of patients.

Axicabtagene ciloleucel (Yescarta) also targets CD19. CRS is the major toxicity of CAR-T therapy, managed with tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor antagonist). Patients with high disease burden have more adverse events. CAR-T represents a paradigm shift in treating refractory hematologic malignancies.

**Q:** What did the ibrutinib vs. ofatumumab trial (NEJM 2014) show for previously treated CLL?

**A:** Ibrutinib (BTK inhibitor) significantly improved PFS (HR 0.22), overall survival (HR 0.43, p=0.005), and overall response rate (42.6% vs. 4.1%) compared to ofatumumab (anti-CD20) in relapsed/refractory CLL or SLL. 12-month OS: 90% ibrutinib vs. 81% ofatumumab.

Ibrutinib is an irreversible BTK (Bruton's tyrosine kinase) inhibitor that blocks BCR signaling critical for CLL cell survival. This trial established ibrutinib as superior to prior standard anti-CD20 therapy in relapsed CLL.

**Q:** What is the RE-VERSE AD trial, and what did it demonstrate about idarucizumab?

**A:** RE-VERSE AD (NEJM 2015): In 90 patients with serious bleeding or requiring urgent procedures due to dabigatran, idarucizumab normalized coagulation test results in 88-98% of patients within minutes. Unbound dabigatran concentrations remained <20 ng/mL at 24 hours in 79% of patients. Conclusion: dabigatran's anticoagulant effect is completely reversed by idarucizumab within minutes.

Idarucizumab (Praxbind) is a humanized monoclonal antibody fragment with 350-fold higher affinity for dabigatran than thrombin. It is given as two 2.5 g IV bolus doses (5 g total). This allows urgent surgery or management of life-threatening bleeding in dabigatran-treated patients.

**Q:** What is iron overload as a delayed transfusion reaction? Who is at risk and how is it managed?

**A:** Iron overload results from repeated transfusions over a long period (e.g., beta-thalassemia major). Can cause secondary hemochromatosis with organ damage (liver, heart, endocrine glands). Treatment: iron chelation therapy (e.g., deferoxamine, deferasirox) or phlebotomy if the patient no longer requires transfusions and is not anemic.

Each unit of pRBC contains approximately 200-250 mg of iron. Patients requiring chronic transfusion (thalassemia, myelodysplastic syndrome) accumulate iron as the body has no physiologic mechanism for iron excretion. Chelation prevents end-organ damage.

**Q:** What is the significance of transfusion-associated hyperkalemia, and who is most at risk?

**A:** Stored RBCs release potassium (K+) over time. Risk increases with storage duration and blood irradiation; decreases with fresh blood. Occurs in ~5% of massively transfused patients. Treatment: manage per hyperkalemia protocol (see Nephrology). At highest risk: neonates, patients with renal failure, massive transfusion recipients.

During storage, RBCs leak intracellular K+ into the plasma. Irradiation accelerates K+ release. In massive transfusion or neonates (small volume of distribution), this can cause life-threatening hyperkalemia and cardiac arrhythmias.

**Q:** What are the indications, contraindications, and key monitoring parameter for rivaroxaban?

**A:** Indications: acute VTE treatment, secondary VTE prevention, orthopedic thromboprophylaxis, stroke prophylaxis in non-valvular AFib. Contraindications: mechanical heart valves, CrCl <30 mL/min. Must be taken with food. Monitoring: Anti-Xa levels (validated for rivaroxaban, used to detect drug presence only — not for dose adjustment). Reversal: andexanet alfa.

Rivaroxaban's food requirement increases bioavailability from 66% to ~100% for the 20 mg dose. Andexanet alfa is a recombinant modified factor Xa decoy that reverses all factor Xa inhibitors. Key side effects: GI hemorrhage, menorrhagia.

## Hematology Basics

**Q:** What are the major sites of hematopoiesis in adults?

**A:** In adults, hematopoiesis occurs in the pelvis, sternum, vertebral bodies, and cranium (axial skeleton and flat bones).

Over 10^11 blood cells are produced daily. During fetal life, hematopoiesis occurs in the yolk sac, liver, and spleen before shifting to the bone marrow. In adults, the red marrow of flat bones and the proximal ends of long bones are the primary sites. This is relevant to understanding where bone marrow biopsy is performed.

**Q:** What is the lifespan of erythrocytes, neutrophils, and platelets in peripheral blood?

**A:** Erythrocytes: 90-120 days; neutrophils: approximately 1 day; platelets: 7-10 days.

These lifespans are clinically important for understanding recovery after chemotherapy or bone marrow suppression (neutrophils recover fastest), transfusion timing, and interpreting CBC changes. Memory lymphocytes persist for years. The differential recovery times explain why neutropenic fever is the earliest and most dangerous complication post-chemotherapy.

**Q:** What is the role of the spleen in hematology?

**A:** The spleen is part of the reticuloendothelial system: it sequesters aged RBCs, removes opsonized cells, and is a site of antibody production.

The spleen filters blood and removes damaged or senescent erythrocytes. After splenectomy, patients develop Howell-Jolly bodies (nuclear remnants) in RBCs that would normally be removed by the spleen. The spleen also serves as a reservoir for platelets and lymphocytes. Loss of splenic function increases susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria.

**Q:** What are the normal reference ranges for hemoglobin in adult males and females?

**A:** Adult females: 115-155 g/L; adult males: 125-170 g/L.

Anemia is defined as Hb <120 g/L in females and Hb <130 g/L in males. The MCV normal range is 80-100 fL, and the normal platelet count is 130-380 x 10^9/L. Normal WBC count is 3.5-10.5 x 10^9/L. These values are essential for interpreting CBC results.

**Q:** What are the indications for bone marrow aspiration and biopsy?

**A:** Indications: unexplained CBC abnormalities; diagnosis/evaluation of plasma cell disorders, leukemias, solid tumour infiltration; staging of lymphoma; evaluating iron metabolism; suspected deposition/storage disease (e.g., amyloidosis, Gaucher's); fever of unknown origin; suspected mycobacterial/fungal/parasitic infections; unexplained splenomegaly; donor evaluation for allogeneic transplant.

Bone marrow aspiration evaluates cellular morphology and provides material for flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and microbiology. Biopsy evaluates architecture and immunohistochemistry. Only aspiration (not biopsy) can be done at the sternal site. A 'dry tap' (no aspirate) suggests myelofibrosis, hairy cell leukemia, or BM infiltration.

**Q:** What is the difference between bone marrow aspiration and biopsy, and where can each be performed?

**A:** Aspiration: fluid marrow sample for cellular morphology, flow cytometry, cytogenetics, molecular studies, microbiology. Biopsy: intact BM core for histology (architecture) and immunohistochemistry. Sites: both at posterior iliac crest/spine; aspiration only at sternum.

Aspiration and biopsy are usually performed together. The key distinction is that aspiration evaluates individual cells and their markers, while biopsy evaluates tissue architecture (critical for myelofibrosis staging). A 'dry tap' on aspiration, where no marrow fluid is obtained, is itself diagnostically significant and should prompt biopsy — causes include myelofibrosis, hairy cell leukemia, and BM infiltration.

**Q:** What are the main cell types in blood and their primary roles in the immune system?

**A:** Neutrophils: innate immunity, main cell in acute inflammation. Eosinophils: response to parasites (helminths) and allergic response. Basophils: allergy and parasitic infection. Lymphocytes: adaptive immunity. Monocytes: innate immunity, differentiate into macrophages or dendritic cells. Platelets: primary hemostasis.

Understanding the functional roles of each blood cell type is foundational for interpreting CBC differentials. Neutrophilia suggests acute bacterial infection or stress; eosinophilia suggests allergy or parasites; lymphocytosis suggests viral infection or lymphoproliferative disorder; monocytosis suggests chronic infection or myeloproliferative disease. Plasma is the acellular fluid component; serum = plasma minus coagulation factors.

**Q:** What distinguishes plasma from serum?

**A:** Serum is equivalent to plasma minus coagulation factors. Plasma is the acellular fluid component of blood containing water, coagulation factors, and immunoglobulins; serum is what remains after blood has clotted and clot has been removed.

This distinction is important for laboratory testing: serum is used for most chemistry tests (electrolytes, proteins, antibodies), while plasma (with anticoagulant) is used for coagulation studies (PT, aPTT). Hematology panels (CBC, blood film) use EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood.

**Q:** What are the causes of a 'dry tap' on bone marrow aspiration?

**A:** Causes of a dry tap (no marrow fluid obtained): myelofibrosis (MF), hairy cell leukemia, and bone marrow infiltration.

A dry tap occurs when the marrow is replaced by fibrotic tissue or densely packed cells that do not aspirate. In myelofibrosis, progressive collagen fibrosis replaces normal hematopoietic tissue. Hairy cell leukemia causes reticulin fibrosis. Metastatic cancer infiltrating the BM can also cause a dry tap. When a dry tap occurs, biopsy becomes even more critical for diagnosis.

## Hematology Anemia

**Q:** What is the clinical utility of RDW in distinguishing iron deficiency anemia from beta-thalassemia minor?

**A:** In iron deficiency anemia, RDW is increased (cells of varying sizes). In beta-thalassemia minor, RDW is normal (cells are uniformly small, similar size) and RBC count is typically elevated.

Both conditions present with microcytic anemia, but RDW differentiates them. In iron deficiency, the progressive depletion of iron stores leads to increasingly smaller and more variable cells (high RDW). In beta-thalassemia minor, all RBCs are uniformly smaller than normal (normal RDW). The combination of microcytosis + normal/high RBC count + normal RDW suggests thalassemia.

**Q:** What is the definition of anemia in adult males and females?

**A:** Adult males: Hb <130 g/L or Hct <38%. Adult females: Hb <120 g/L or Hct <37%.

These thresholds reflect a decrease in RBC mass detectable by laboratory measures. Hemoglobin thresholds differ by sex due to the influence of androgens on erythropoiesis. Values also change during pregnancy (trimester-specific). Anemia is a clinical finding, not a diagnosis — identifying the underlying cause (e.g., iron deficiency, chronic disease, hemolysis) is essential.

**Q:** What are the key physical signs of anemia and at what hemoglobin levels do they appear?

**A:** Pallor in mucous membranes/conjunctiva at Hb <90 g/L; ocular bruits at Hb <55 g/L; pallor in palmar skin creases at Hb <75 g/L. Other signs: tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension, systolic flow murmur, wide pulse pressure, angular cheilitis, koilonychia (nail spooning), glossitis.

The hemoglobin-specific thresholds for physical signs are high-yield for clinical examination questions. Pallor in the palmar creases (Hb <75 g/L) requires significant anemia. In hemolytic anemia, additional signs include jaundice, icterus, and splenomegaly. The systolic flow murmur is due to high cardiac output in compensation for decreased oxygen-carrying capacity.

**Q:** How does the reticulocyte count help classify anemia?

**A:** Reticulocytosis (elevated reticulocyte count) in anemia suggests appropriate bone marrow response (e.g., hemolysis, blood loss). Normal or low reticulocyte count in anemia suggests decreased production (BM infiltration, nutritional deficiency, or other hypoproliferative cause).

With acute blood loss, reticulocytes should increase 2-3x initially, then 5-7x over subsequent days. The reticulocyte count must always be interpreted relative to Hb concentration. A normal absolute reticulocyte count in the context of significant anemia represents an inappropriately low response. The reticulocyte index (RI) corrects for anemia severity.

## Hematology Blood Film

**Q:** What RBC morphology is pathognomonic for microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)/TMA?

**A:** Schistocytes (helmet cells, fragmented RBCs) are the hallmark of MAHA/TMA.

Schistocytes form due to traumatic disruption of the RBC membrane as cells pass through damaged microvasculature. Conditions causing schistocytes include HUS, aHUS, TTP, DIC, preeclampsia, HELLP syndrome, malignant hypertension, vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, and prosthetic heart valves. Identifying schistocytes on blood film is essential for diagnosing these life-threatening conditions.

**Q:** What conditions are associated with spherocytes on blood film?

**A:** Hereditary spherocytosis and immune hemolytic anemia (autoimmune hemolytic anemia).

Spherocytes form due to membrane defects or loss of membrane, resulting in a spheroidal RBC with loss of central pallor. In hereditary spherocytosis, the cause is a membrane protein defect (e.g., ankyrin, spectrin). In immune hemolytic anemia, antibody-mediated membrane damage leads to spherocyte formation. Both present with hemolytic anemia with positive osmotic fragility test and positive DAT (in immune HA).

**Q:** What conditions are associated with target cells (codocytes) on blood film?

**A:** Liver disease, HbSC disease, thalassemia, iron deficiency anemia, and asplenia.

Target cells (codocytes) have a 'bull's eye' appearance due to excess RBC membrane relative to cell volume. This occurs when there is excess membrane lipid (liver disease) or reduced Hb content (thalassemia, iron deficiency). Asplenia removes the normal process of clearing these abnormal cells. The mnemonic HALT (HbSC/liver/asplenia/thalassemia) can help recall the causes.

**Q:** What conditions are associated with dacrocytes (teardrop cells) on blood film?

**A:** Myelofibrosis (MF), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), beta-thalassemia, megaloblastic anemia, and bone marrow infiltration.

Dacrocytes (teardrop cells) have a single pointed end and form when RBCs are deformed as they squeeze past bone marrow fibrosis or infiltration. Myelofibrosis is the classic association. The presence of teardrop cells along with nucleated RBCs and immature myeloid cells is called leukoerythroblastosis and strongly suggests BM infiltration or fibrosis.

**Q:** What is the clinical significance of rouleaux formation on blood film?

**A:** Rouleaux (RBC stacks resembling coins) is most commonly caused by pregnancy; other causes include inflammatory conditions (polyclonal immunoglobulins) and plasma cell dyscrasias (monoclonal paraproteinemia, e.g., multiple myeloma, macroglobulinemia).

Rouleaux formation occurs due to increased plasma concentration of high molecular weight proteins (fibrinogen, immunoglobulins). In pregnancy, physiological increase in fibrinogen is the most common cause. In non-pregnant patients, unexplained rouleaux should raise suspicion for plasma cell dyscrasia (multiple myeloma), especially with other features like hypercalcemia, renal failure, or bone pain.

**Q:** What are Howell-Jolly bodies and when are they seen?

**A:** Howell-Jolly bodies are small spherical nuclear remnants in RBCs, normally removed by the spleen. They are seen post-splenectomy, in hyposplenism (e.g., sickle cell disease), in neonates, and in megaloblastic anemia.

During normal erythrocyte maturation, nuclear remnants are expelled and cleared by the spleen. When splenic function is absent or impaired, these remnants persist and appear as small dark inclusions near the periphery of the RBC. In sickle cell disease, autosplenectomy from repeated infarctions leads to Howell-Jolly bodies. Their presence on blood film is a useful indicator of functional asplenia.

**Q:** What are Heinz bodies and what conditions produce them?

**A:** Heinz bodies are denatured and precipitated hemoglobin inside RBCs, seen in G6PD deficiency (post-oxidant exposure), thalassemia, and unstable hemoglobin variants.

Heinz bodies require special stains (crystal violet or brilliant cresyl blue) to visualize, as they are not visible on routine Giemsa-stained blood films. In G6PD deficiency, oxidative stress overwhelms the cell's antioxidant capacity (reduced GSH), precipitating Hb. The RBCs with Heinz bodies are then cleared by the spleen, and on routine film, 'bite cells' (degmacytes) may be seen instead.

**Q:** What is basophilic stippling and what conditions cause it?

**A:** Basophilic stippling consists of deep blue granulations in RBCs indicating ribosome aggregation. Causes: thalassemia, heavy metal poisoning (Pb, Zn, Ag, Hg), megaloblastic anemia, MDS, and pyrimidine 5'-nucleotidase deficiency.

Normally, reticulocyte ribosomes are degraded as cells mature. When ribosome degradation is impaired (lead poisoning, enzyme deficiency) or ribosome synthesis is accelerated (thalassemia), ribosomes clump and stain blue. Lead poisoning is a classic high-yield cause of basophilic stippling, alongside thalassemia. This is important in the differential for microcytic anemia in children.

**Q:** What is the significance of smudge cells on blood film?

**A:** Smudge cells are lymphocytes damaged during blood film preparation. They are associated with CLL and other lymphoproliferative disorders due to increased lymphocyte fragility.

In CLL, the malignant lymphocytes are mechanically fragile and rupture during the smearing process, producing smudge cells (also called basket cells). The presence of numerous smudge cells on blood film is characteristic of CLL, though their absence does not rule it out. CLL also presents with lymphocytosis, often incidentally found on CBC.

**Q:** What are hypersegmented neutrophils and what do they suggest?

**A:** Hypersegmented neutrophils have >5 nuclear lobes and suggest a megaloblastic process (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency).

Normally, neutrophils have 3-4 lobed nuclei. Hypersegmentation occurs in megaloblastic anemia due to impaired DNA synthesis affecting nuclear segmentation. Finding even a single neutrophil with 5 or more lobes is abnormal; finding neutrophils with 6+ lobes is virtually diagnostic of megaloblastic anemia. The CBC typically also shows macrocytosis (MCV >100 fL).

**Q:** What is a 'left shift' in hematology and what causes it?

**A:** A left shift is increased granulocyte precursors (bands, metamyelocytes, myelocytes, promyelocytes, blasts) in the peripheral blood. Causes include leukemoid reactions (acute infections, pregnancy, neonates, hypoxia, shock), MPNs (CML, MF), and G-CSF administration.

The term 'left shift' refers to the leftward shift on the maturation sequence, meaning earlier (more immature) cells are appearing in the blood. It is most commonly seen in severe bacterial infection driving the bone marrow to release immature neutrophil precursors. A leukoerythroblastic picture (left shift + nucleated RBCs) suggests bone marrow infiltration or fibrosis.

**Q:** What elliptocyte morphology is associated with iron deficiency anemia specifically?

**A:** In iron deficiency anemia, elliptocytes appear as narrow elongated 'pencil forms' (pencil cells), which are a specific variant of elliptocytes.

Elliptocytes/ovalocytes are oval-shaped elongated RBCs. While hereditary elliptocytosis causes classic elliptocytes, iron deficiency anemia specifically produces pencil-form elliptocytes as a result of severe iron depletion. Other conditions causing elliptocytes include megaloblastic anemia, myelofibrosis, and MDS. This morphologic distinction can help narrow the differential.

**Q:** What are acanthocytes (spur cells) and in what conditions are they found?

**A:** Acanthocytes are distorted RBCs with irregularly distributed thorn-like projections due to abnormal membrane lipid or protein composition. Found in: severe liver disease (spur cell anemia), starvation/anorexia, and post-splenectomy.

Acanthocytes differ from echinocytes (burr cells): echinocytes have numerous regularly-spaced small projections (from uremia, HUS, storage), while acanthocytes have irregularly distributed larger projections. In severe liver disease, altered plasma lipids are incorporated into the RBC membrane, creating spur cell anemia — a poor prognostic sign. Abetalipoproteinemia also causes acanthocytes.

**Q:** What conditions cause echinocytes (burr cells) on blood film?

**A:** Echinocytes (burr cells) — RBCs with numerous regularly spaced small spiny projections — are associated with uremia, HUS, burns, cardiopulmonary bypass, post-transfusion, and storage artifact.

Echinocytes can be a storage artifact (false positive) but when found in clinical context, uremia is the most important cause. Distinguishing echinocytes from acanthocytes is important: echinocytes have regular, uniform, small projections, while acanthocytes have irregular, larger projections. In uremia, echinocytes may be accompanied by thrombocytopenia and increased risk of bleeding.

**Q:** What are atypical lymphocytes on blood film and what causes them?

**A:** Atypical lymphocytes have pale blue cytoplasm with pink granules and cytoplasm indented by RBC edges. They are associated with viral infections (particularly EBV/infectious mononucleosis) and T-cell large granular lymphocyte (T-LGL) leukemia.

In infectious mononucleosis (EBV), atypical lymphocytes represent activated cytotoxic T cells responding to EBV-infected B cells. They are large with abundant cytoplasm that appears to wrap around adjacent RBCs. The presence of atypical lymphocytes with pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, and splenomegaly in a young person is classic for infectious mononucleosis.

**Q:** What is polychromasia on blood film and what does it indicate?

**A:** Polychromasia is the presence of pinkish-blue RBCs on blood film, indicating increased reticulocytes (immature erythrocytes). It suggests increased RBC production by the bone marrow.

Reticulocytes contain residual RNA that stains pinkish-blue with Romanowsky stains, giving polychromatic appearance. Polychromasia indicates an appropriate bone marrow response to anemia — seen in hemolytic anemia, acute blood loss recovery, or post-treatment of nutritional deficiency. It should be correlated with the absolute reticulocyte count.

## Hematology Malignancy

**Q:** What WBC finding is pathognomonic for acute myeloid leukemia (AML)?

**A:** Auer rods — cytoplasmic inclusions that form long needle-like structures in the cytoplasm of myeloblasts — are pathognomonic for AML.

Auer rods are crystallized primary (azurophilic) granules found in malignant myeloblasts. They are specifically seen in AML (not ALL or other leukemias). In acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL, AML M3), Auer rods are particularly numerous and may form 'faggot cells.' Identifying Auer rods on blood film or bone marrow aspirate indicates AML as a diagnosis.

**Q:** What percentage of blasts on WBC differential defines acute leukemia and why is it a medical emergency?

**A:** >20% blasts on WBC differential defines acute leukemia. It is a medical emergency due to rapid proliferation of immature cells causing bone marrow failure (infection, bleeding, anemia) and potentially leukostasis.

The WHO threshold for acute leukemia diagnosis is ≥20% blasts in bone marrow or peripheral blood. Clinically, this results in pancytopenia (from marrow replacement) plus circulating blasts. Leukostasis can cause CNS and pulmonary complications if the WBC is very high. Prompt diagnosis, stabilization, and initiation of treatment are required.
