# Dermatology

## Common Dermatoses & Rashes

**Q:** A 35-year-old woman has an intensely pruritic, erythematous rash with vesicles on flexor surfaces and neck after starting a new laundry detergent. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Allergic contact dermatitis (ACD)

ACD is a type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction. The distribution corresponds to contact area. Patch testing identifies the allergen. Treatment: remove trigger, topical corticosteroids, antihistamines for itch.

**Q:** What are the ABCDE criteria used for in dermatology?

**A:** Screening pigmented lesions for malignant melanoma: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter >6mm, Evolution (change over time)

The ABCDE mnemonic is a clinical screening tool. Any single criterion or "ugly duckling" sign (lesion that looks different from others) warrants dermatology referral. Dermoscopy increases diagnostic accuracy.

**Q:** A 22-year-old man has comedones, inflammatory papules, and nodules on the face and back. What is first-line treatment for moderate inflammatory acne?

**A:** Topical benzoyl peroxide + topical retinoid (e.g., adapalene); oral doxycycline if inadequate response

Combination topical therapy (retinoid + benzoyl peroxide) is most effective for inflammatory acne. Oral antibiotics are added for moderate-severe disease. Isotretinoin is reserved for nodular/cystic acne or antibiotic-resistant moderate acne.

**Q:** What is the pathognomonic skin finding of psoriasis?

**A:** Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding when scale is removed from plaque) and Koebner phenomenon (lesions develop at trauma sites)

Psoriasis presents as well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale on extensor surfaces, scalp, and nails. Nail pitting and onycholysis also occur. About 30% develop psoriatic arthritis.

**Q:** A 6-month-old infant has oozing, crusted erythematous patches on cheeks and scalp with intense itch. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Atopic dermatitis (infantile eczema)

Atopic dermatitis affects 20% of children. Infantile pattern: face, scalp, and extensor surfaces. Treatment: emollients (moisturizers) as cornerstone, topical corticosteroids for flares, avoidance of triggers. Often improves by school age.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for tinea corporis (ringworm)?

**A:** Topical antifungal (e.g., clotrimazole, terbinafine) for 2–4 weeks

Tinea corporis is caused by dermatophytes. Diagnosis is confirmed by KOH preparation showing hyphae. Topical terbinafine is more effective than azoles. Oral antifungals (terbinafine, itraconazole) are used for extensive, tinea capitis, or nail involvement.

**Q:** A 65-year-old man presents with a pearly nodule with rolled edges and telangiectasia on his nose. He is a farmer with extensive sun exposure. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

BCC is the most common skin cancer. Nodular BCC has classic pearly rolled border with telangiectasia. It rarely metastasizes but causes local tissue destruction. Treatment: surgical excision or Mohs micrographic surgery.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate plaque psoriasis?

**A:** Topical corticosteroids (moderate-to-high potency) combined with topical vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol)

The calcipotriol/betamethasone combination (Dovobet) is the most effective topical treatment for plaque psoriasis. It reduces scale and erythema with fewer steroid side effects than monotherapy. Phototherapy and biologics are used for moderate-severe disease.

**Q:** Which condition presents as silvery-white lacy patches on the buccal mucosa that are asymptomatic and require no treatment?

**A:** Lichen planus (reticular pattern — Wickham striae)

Oral lichen planus is a chronic inflammatory condition. Reticular (white lacy) pattern is benign. Erosive pattern causes pain and has slight malignant potential. Diagnosis: biopsy. Treatment of erosive type: topical or systemic corticosteroids.

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman develops a painful, burning vesicular rash in a dermatomal distribution on her left thorax. What is the treatment?

**A:** Antiviral therapy: valacyclovir 1000 mg TID × 7 days, initiated within 72 hours of rash onset

Herpes zoster (shingles) reactivation of VZV. Antivirals reduce duration and severity, and prevent post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN). Adjunctive gabapentin or amitriptyline treats neuropathic pain. Shingrix (recombinant zoster vaccine) prevents recurrence.

**Q:** What distinguishes eczema from psoriasis clinically?

**A:** Eczema: pruritic, flexural surfaces, oozing/crusting, poorly defined borders. Psoriasis: silvery scales, extensor surfaces, well-demarcated plaques, Auspitz sign, nail pitting

Eczema: atopic triad (eczema, asthma, allergic rhinitis). Treatment: emollients, topical steroids, calcineurin inhibitors. Psoriasis: Koebner phenomenon. Treatment: topical steroids, vitamin D analogs, phototherapy, biologics.

**Q:** How do eczema and psoriasis differ clinically?

**A:** Eczema: poorly defined, weeping/crusting, flexural, intensely pruritic, atopic triad. Psoriasis: well-defined silvery scale, extensor surfaces, Auspitz sign, nail pitting, Koebner phenomenon

Eczema: filaggrin mutation, Th2-mediated. Psoriasis: Th17-mediated. Eczema Rx: moisturizers+topical steroids+calcineurin inhibitors. Psoriasis Rx: topical steroids+vitamin D+phototherapy+biologics for moderate-severe.

**Q:** What is the stepwise management of atopic dermatitis?

**A:** 1) Daily moisturizers + trigger avoidance. 2) Topical steroids (low-potency face, moderate body). 3) Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus). 4) Phototherapy. 5) Systemic: dupilumab, MTX, cyclosporine

Moisturize within 3min of bath. Avoid soap. Wet wraps for flares. Dupilumab (anti-IL4/13) transformed severe AD. Avoid systemic steroids (rebound). Steroid phobia common - educate patients/parents.

**Q:** What is the management of moderate-severe plaque psoriasis?

**A:** Topical: steroids + calcipotriol. Phototherapy: nbUVB. Systemic: MTX, cyclosporine, acitretin, apremilast. Biologics: TNFi, IL-17i (secukinumab), IL-23i (guselkumab), IL-12/23i (ustekinumab)

Screen for PsA, metabolic syndrome, depression, CV risk. Biologics achieve PASI 75-90 in >80%. Pre-biologic: TB/HBV screen, vaccines. MTX: monitor CBC/LFTs. Acitretin: teratogenic 3 years.

**Q:** What is the stepwise management of acne vulgaris?

**A:** Mild comedonal: topical retinoid +/- BPO. Moderate papulopustular: + topical clindamycin with BPO. Moderate-severe: + oral doxycycline x3mo. Severe nodular/scarring: isotretinoin

Isotretinoin: teratogenic (iPLEDGE, 2 contraception, pregnancy tests). SE: dryness, lipids, LFTs. OCP/spironolactone for female hormonal acne. Never use topical antibiotic monotherapy (resistance).

**Q:** How is chronic urticaria managed?

**A:** Acute <6wk: 2nd-gen antihistamine. Chronic >6wk: step up antihistamine to 4x dose, then add omalizumab (anti-IgE). Avoid ASA/NSAIDs (worsen 30%). CSU is autoimmune, rarely allergic

No allergy testing for CSU. Lesions lasting >24h with bruising: consider urticarial vasculitis (biopsy). Omalizumab very effective for antihistamine-refractory CSU. Cyclosporine as last resort.

**Q:** What dermatological emergencies require urgent recognition?

**A:** SJS/TEN (skin pain, mucosal erosions, Nikolsky+, >30% BSA=TEN). Necrotizing fasciitis (pain out of proportion, systemic toxicity). Pemphigus vulgaris (flaccid blisters)

SJS/TEN drugs: allopurinol, carbamazepine, sulfonamides, phenytoin. Immediate drug withdrawal + burn unit. TEN mortality 25-30%. SCORTEN predicts. NF: surgical emergency + broad-spectrum abx.

**Q:** How are fungal skin infections diagnosed and treated?

**A:** Tinea: KOH shows hyphae. Topical terbinafine/clotrimazole 2-4wk. Tinea capitis: oral terbinafine 6-8wk (topical insufficient). Onychomycosis: oral terbinafine 12wk toes, 6wk fingers

Tinea versicolor (Malassezia): spaghetti-and-meatballs KOH. ID reaction: distant eczematous reaction to fungal infection. Tinea capitis: kerion is inflammatory mass requiring oral antifungal + pred.

**Q:** What is the ABCDE approach to evaluating pigmented lesions?

**A:** A: Asymmetry. B: Border irregularity. C: Color variation. D: Diameter >6mm. E: Evolution (most important). Ugly duckling sign. Any concerning lesion: excisional biopsy (not shave)

Nodular melanoma may not follow ABCDE (EFG: elevated, firm, growing). New changing pigmented lesion in >30 needs evaluation. Dermoscopy reduces unnecessary biopsies by 42%.

**Q:** What are common bacterial skin infections and their treatment?

**A:** Impetigo: mupirocin (local) or cephalexin. Cellulitis: cephalexin/cloxacillin. Abscess: I&D +/- antibiotics. MRSA: TMP-SMX or doxycycline. Erysipelas: penicillin/amoxicillin

Purulent cellulitis: cover MRSA. Non-purulent: streptococcal. Mark borders. Recurrent cellulitis: treat predisposing factors (edema, tinea pedis). Bullous impetigo always S.aureus.

**Q:** What are the topical corticosteroid potency classes?

**A:** Superpotent (I): clobetasol. Potent (II-III): betamethasone dipropionate. Moderate (IV-V): triamcinolone. Mild (VI-VII): hydrocortisone 1%. Face/groin: mild only. Trunk: moderate-potent

SE: atrophy, striae (irreversible), telangiectasia, perioral dermatitis. Limit potent to 2-4wk. Ointments > creams in potency. Weekend-only maintenance reduces SE. Steroid holidays prevent tachyphylaxis.

**Q:** What is the dermatology morphology vocabulary?

**A:** Macule: flat <1cm. Patch: flat >1cm. Papule: raised <1cm. Plaque: raised >1cm. Vesicle: fluid <1cm. Bulla: fluid >1cm. Nodule: deep solid. Wheal: transient edematous papule/plaque

Additional: pustule (pus-filled), erosion (partial thickness loss), ulcer (full thickness), excoriation (scratched), lichenification (thickened from rubbing), scale, crust. Accurate description essential for consultation.

**Q:** What is Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN and how is it managed?

**A:** SJS: <10% BSA detachment. TEN: >30% BSA. Overlap: 10-30%. Offending drugs: allopurinol, sulfonamides, anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), NSAIDs. Management: stop drug, burn unit, supportive care, IVIG controversial

Mortality: SJS 5%, TEN 30%. Nikolsky sign positive. Mucosal involvement (oral, ocular, genital). HLA-B*5801 screening before allopurinol in high-risk ethnicities (Southeast Asian, African American).

**Q:** Define the following dermatologic terms: macule, papule, plaque, vesicle, bulla, nodule, and wheal.

**A:** Macule: flat, <1 cm, color change only (e.g., freckle). Patch: flat, >1 cm. Papule: raised, solid, <1 cm (e.g., molluscum). Plaque: raised, flat-topped, >1 cm (e.g., psoriasis). Vesicle: fluid-filled, <1 cm (e.g., herpes). Bulla: fluid-filled, >1 cm (e.g., bullous pemphigoid). Nodule: solid, >1 cm, extends into dermis/subcutis (e.g., erythema nodosum). Wheal: transient, edematous papule/plaque (e.g., urticaria).

Accurate morphologic description is the foundation of dermatologic diagnosis. The Canadian Dermatology Association emphasizes systematic skin examination: describe the primary lesion, secondary changes (scale, crust, erosion), distribution, and configuration. This standardized approach allows precise communication between clinicians.

**Q:** Describe the stepwise management of atopic dermatitis (eczema) from mild to severe.

**A:** Step 1 (all severities): emollients (fragrance-free), trigger avoidance, gentle skin care. Step 2 (mild): low-potency topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1% for face/folds). Step 3 (moderate): medium-potency topical corticosteroids (betamethasone valerate 0.05%), topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) for steroid-sparing. Step 4 (moderate-severe): wet wrap therapy, phototherapy (narrowband UVB). Step 5 (severe/refractory): systemic agents — dupilumab (anti-IL-4/IL-13, first-line biologic), cyclosporine, methotrexate, or JAK inhibitors (abrocitinib, upadacitinib).

Atopic dermatitis affects 10–20% of Canadian children. Dupilumab was approved by Health Canada for moderate-to-severe AD in adults and adolescents and has transformed management. The CPS emphasizes that emollients are the cornerstone of therapy and that topical corticosteroid phobia leads to undertreated disease.

**Q:** What are the clinical subtypes of psoriasis, and what nail changes are characteristic?

**A:** Subtypes: 1) Plaque (most common, 80–90%): well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale on extensor surfaces, scalp, sacrum. 2) Guttate: small drop-like lesions, often triggered by streptococcal pharyngitis. 3) Pustular: sterile pustules (localized palmoplantar or generalized von Zumbusch — medical emergency). 4) Erythrodermic: >90% body involvement, medical emergency (thermoregulatory failure). Nail changes: pitting, oil-drop spots, onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis, nail crumbling.

Psoriasis affects 2–3% of Canadians. Nail involvement occurs in ~50% and is associated with psoriatic arthritis (check for it in all psoriasis patients). The Canadian Dermatology Association and Canadian Psoriasis Network advocate for early systemic therapy in moderate-to-severe disease, including biologics (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors).

**Q:** Outline the treatment ladder for acne vulgaris from mild comedonal to severe nodulocystic.

**A:** Mild comedonal: topical retinoid (tretinoin, adapalene). Mild inflammatory: add topical benzoyl peroxide ± topical antibiotic (clindamycin). Moderate inflammatory: topical retinoid + benzoyl peroxide + oral antibiotic (doxycycline or minocycline, limit to 3 months). Moderate-severe (nodular): consider oral combined OCP (females) + above. Severe nodulocystic/treatment-resistant: oral isotretinoin (Accutane) 0.5–1 mg/kg/day for 4–6 months (cumulative dose 120–150 mg/kg).

The Canadian Acne Clinical Practice Guidelines emphasize topical retinoids as the foundation of acne therapy for all severities. Isotretinoin requires iPLEDGE-equivalent pregnancy prevention in Canada, monthly pregnancy tests, and monitoring of lipids and liver function. It is the only therapy that can induce prolonged remission in severe acne.

**Q:** Differentiate allergic contact dermatitis from irritant contact dermatitis. How is the diagnosis confirmed?

**A:** Irritant contact dermatitis: direct damage to skin from an irritant (soaps, solvents, water), affects anyone with enough exposure, well-demarcated to area of contact, burning/stinging pain, more common than allergic. Allergic contact dermatitis: type IV (delayed) hypersensitivity, requires prior sensitization, can spread beyond contact area, intensely pruritic, vesicular/eczematous. Common allergens: nickel, poison ivy (urushiol), fragrances, rubber chemicals. Diagnosis confirmed by patch testing (standardized allergen panels applied to back for 48 hours, read at 48 and 96 hours).

Patch testing is the gold standard for diagnosing allergic contact dermatitis and is performed in Canadian dermatology clinics using the TRUE test or expanded panels. The most common contact allergen in North America is nickel, followed by fragrance mix and balsam of Peru. Management includes allergen avoidance and topical corticosteroids.

**Q:** A patient presents with annular, erythematous, scaly plaques with central clearing on the trunk. A KOH prep shows septate branching hyphae. What is the diagnosis and treatment?

**A:** Tinea corporis (ringworm), a dermatophyte infection. Treatment: topical antifungals (terbinafine cream or azole cream for 2–4 weeks) for localized disease. For extensive, recurrent, or immunocompromised patients: oral terbinafine (250 mg/day for 2–4 weeks) or oral itraconazole.

Dermatophyte infections are classified by body site: tinea corporis (body), tinea pedis (feet), tinea cruris (groin), tinea capitis (scalp — requires oral therapy as topicals do not penetrate hair follicle). KOH preparation is the rapid, inexpensive diagnostic test. In Canada, tinea capitis is most commonly caused by Trichophyton tonsurans, which does not fluoresce under Wood lamp.

**Q:** A child presents with a scaly, alopecic patch on the scalp with broken hairs and posterior cervical lymphadenopathy. KOH prep of hair shows endothrix spores. What is the diagnosis and why is oral therapy required?

**A:** Tinea capitis. Oral therapy is required because topical antifungals cannot penetrate the hair follicle to eradicate the infection. First-line: oral griseofulvin (20–25 mg/kg/day microsize for 6–8 weeks) or oral terbinafine. Adjunct: antifungal shampoo (selenium sulfide or ketoconazole) to reduce transmission.

Tinea capitis is most common in prepubertal children. In Canada, Trichophyton tonsurans is the predominant pathogen, unlike in Europe (Microsporum canis). A kerion (boggy, inflammatory mass) is an intense inflammatory response and still requires oral antifungal, not incision and drainage. The CPS recommends oral griseofulvin as first-line for children.

**Q:** Differentiate a maculopapular drug eruption from Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN). What are the most common offending drugs?

**A:** Maculopapular drug eruption: most common drug rash (>90%), diffuse symmetric erythematous macules/papules, mild pruritus, no mucosal involvement, self-limited after drug cessation. SJS: mucosal involvement (≥2 sites), targetoid lesions, epidermal detachment <10% BSA, fever, painful skin. TEN: same spectrum as SJS but epidermal detachment >30% BSA; mortality 25–35%. SJS/TEN overlap: 10–30% BSA. Common offenders: sulfonamides (TMP-SMX), anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), allopurinol, NSAIDs, nevirapine.

SJS/TEN is a dermatologic emergency requiring ICU/burn unit care. Management includes immediate discontinuation of the offending drug, supportive care (wound care, fluids, pain management), and ophthalmology consultation (ocular involvement is common and can cause blindness). In Canada, the DRESS/SJS/TEN pharmacovigilance registry helps track these serious adverse drug reactions.

**Q:** What is the SCORTEN prognostic score used for, and what are its parameters?

**A:** SCORTEN predicts mortality in SJS/TEN. Seven parameters (1 point each): age >40, malignancy, heart rate >120, initial BSA epidermal detachment >10%, BUN >28 mg/dL (>10 mmol/L), glucose >252 mg/dL (>14 mmol/L), bicarbonate <20 mEq/L. Mortality by score: 0–1 = 3.2%, 2 = 12.1%, 3 = 35.3%, 4 = 58.3%, ≥5 = >90%.

SCORTEN should be calculated within 24 hours of admission and again on day 3, as initial BSA detachment often underestimates final extent. Early transfer to a burn unit or ICU with experience in TEN management improves outcomes. Canadian centres such as the Ross Tilley Burn Centre (Sunnybrook, Toronto) are referral sites for severe TEN cases.

**Q:** What are the different types of wound closure, and when is each appropriate?

**A:** Primary closure (primary intention): wound edges approximated immediately; clean wounds <6–8 hours old (face <24 hours due to good blood supply). Secondary intention: wound left open to heal by granulation; contaminated wounds, abscess cavities, bite wounds with infection risk. Delayed primary closure (tertiary intention): wound left open initially, closed at 3–5 days once infection risk has passed; contaminated wounds requiring debridement. Techniques: sutures, staples, tissue adhesive (Dermabond), adhesive strips (Steri-Strips).

The choice of closure depends on wound age, contamination, location, and tension. Canadian emergency medicine guidelines recommend tissue adhesive for low-tension lacerations in cooperative patients. All wounds require irrigation (normal saline under pressure) prior to closure to reduce infection rates. Assess for neurovascular and tendon injury before closing.

**Q:** When is tetanus prophylaxis indicated for wound management in Canada?

**A:** Clean minor wounds: Td/Tdap booster only if >10 years since last dose. All other wounds (contaminated, puncture, crush, devitalized tissue): Td/Tdap if >5 years since last dose. If primary series incomplete or unknown: give Td/Tdap PLUS tetanus immunoglobulin (TIG 250 IU IM) for tetanus-prone wounds. Complete the primary series (3 doses total).

The Canadian Immunization Guide provides the tetanus prophylaxis algorithm. In Canada, routine boosters are given at ages 14–16 (Tdap) and then Td every 10 years. TIG provides passive immunity and should be given at a separate anatomic site from the vaccine. All puncture wounds, wounds with devitalized tissue, and wounds >6 hours old are considered tetanus-prone.

**Q:** A patient presents with a cat bite wound on the hand. What organisms are of concern and what is the management?

**A:** Cat bites have a high infection rate (30–50%) due to deep puncture inoculation of bacteria. Primary concern: Pasteurella multocida (rapid onset cellulitis within 24 hours). Also: S. aureus, Streptococci, anaerobes, Bartonella henselae (cat scratch disease). Management: thorough irrigation, do NOT suture (high infection risk, except face), prophylactic antibiotics: amoxicillin-clavulanate (first-line), update tetanus if needed, assess rabies risk.

Cat bite infections progress rapidly due to Pasteurella. Hand bites are particularly dangerous due to proximity to joints, tendons, and bone. The Public Health Agency of Canada recommends assessing rabies risk for all animal bites, and local public health units maintain protocols for rabies post-exposure prophylaxis. Dog bites have lower infection rates (~5–10%) but more tissue damage.

**Q:** A patient with psoriasis develops painful, swollen DIP joints with nail pitting. What is the diagnosis and the key clinical features distinguishing it from rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** Psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Key distinguishing features from RA: DIP joint involvement (RA spares DIP), dactylitis (sausage digits), enthesitis (Achilles, plantar fascia), asymmetric oligoarthritis (though can be symmetric), nail changes (pitting, onycholysis), negative RF (seronegative), association with skin psoriasis, axial involvement (sacroiliitis). X-ray: pencil-in-cup deformity, periostitis.

Psoriatic arthritis affects up to 30% of psoriasis patients. The Canadian Rheumatology Association recommends screening all psoriasis patients for joint symptoms. Treatment includes NSAIDs for mild disease, DMARDs (methotrexate) for moderate, and biologics (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors such as secukinumab, IL-23 inhibitors) for moderate-to-severe PsA.

**Q:** Describe the isotretinoin prescribing requirements in Canada and key side effects to monitor.

**A:** Requirements: pregnancy prevention (two forms of contraception in females of childbearing potential), monthly pregnancy tests (before, during, and 1 month after treatment), informed consent, baseline and periodic labs (lipids, liver enzymes, CBC). Key side effects: teratogenicity (category X — absolute contraindication in pregnancy), mucocutaneous dryness (lips, skin, eyes), elevated triglycerides, transaminitis, myalgias, mood changes (monitor for depression), pseudotumor cerebri (especially with concurrent tetracyclines — contraindicated combination).

Isotretinoin is the most effective treatment for severe nodulocystic acne, with 60–80% achieving long-term remission. Health Canada mandates pregnancy prevention programs. The association with depression/suicide remains debated; the CDA recommends counselling patients about mood changes and monitoring. Concurrent vitamin A supplementation is contraindicated.

**Q:** A patient presents with painful grouped vesicles on an erythematous base in a dermatomal distribution on the trunk. What is the diagnosis, treatment, and the indication for the shingles vaccine in Canada?

**A:** Herpes zoster (shingles), caused by reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV). Treatment: oral valacyclovir 1000 mg TID or acyclovir 800 mg 5×/day for 7 days, ideally within 72 hours of rash onset. Pain management: acetaminophen, NSAIDs, gabapentin/pregabalin for post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN). Vaccination: Shingrix (recombinant adjuvanted vaccine) recommended for adults ≥50 years (2 doses, 2–6 months apart); also recommended for immunocompromised adults ≥18 years.

The National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) recommends Shingrix over the older live Zostavax vaccine due to superior efficacy (>90% vs ~50%) and durability. Post-herpetic neuralgia is the most common complication, occurring in 10–18% of cases (higher risk with age). Ophthalmic zoster (V1 distribution) requires urgent ophthalmology referral.

**Q:** What layers does the skin epidermis derive from embryologically, and which layer does the dermis derive from?

**A:** The epidermis derives from ectoderm. The dermis derives from mesoderm (mesenchymal cells).

Understanding embryological skin origins is relevant for explaining skin disease patterns. Ectoderm gives rise to keratinocytes, hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and eccrine glands; mesoderm gives rise to collagen-producing fibroblasts, blood vessels, and dermal structures.

**Q:** At what gestational age does keratinization of fetal skin begin, and when is the hair follicle primordium formed?

**A:** Keratinization begins at approximately 9 weeks gestation. The primordial hair follicle forms from the basal cell bud at approximately 14 weeks gestation.

Fetal skin development proceeds in a structured sequence: 2-layer skin at 4 weeks → keratinization at 9 weeks → hair follicle at 14 weeks → sebaceous gland at 18 weeks → eccrine gland at 23 weeks. Prematurity affects skin barrier function and permeability.

**Q:** What are the five layers of the epidermis from deepest to most superficial?

**A:** Stratum basale → stratum spinosum → stratum granulosum → stratum lucidum → stratum corneum. Mnemonic: 'Canadians Lie Good Sushi Boxes' (Basale, spinosum, Granulosum, Lucidum, Corneum).

The epidermis is avascular and keratinocytes migrate from the stratum basale to the surface in approximately 4 weeks. The stratum lucidum is only present in thick skin (palms/soles).

**Q:** Which cells are found in the stratum basale of the epidermis?

**A:** Keratinocytes (mitotic, youngest), melanocytes (keratinocyte:melanocyte ratio 10:1), and Merkel cells (touch sensation).

Melanocyte number is equal among all races; differences in skin colour are due to the quantity and distribution of melanin produced. Merkel cells are mechanoreceptors involved in light touch sensation.

**Q:** What is the function of Langerhans cells in the skin?

**A:** Langerhans cells are dendritic cells located in the epidermis that function in immune surveillance (antigen presentation).

Langerhans cells are part of the skin's innate immune system and are important in allergic contact dermatitis and other immune-mediated skin conditions.

**Q:** What is the difference between the papillary and reticular layers of the dermis?

**A:** Papillary dermis: superficial, contains capillaries supplying nutrients to the epidermis. Reticular dermis: deeper, provides structural strength via collagen bundles, elastic fibres, fibroblasts, and macrophages.

The dermis also contains mast cells (releasing histamine in type I hypersensitivity), blood vessels, nerves, pilosebaceous units, and sweat glands.

**Q:** What is the difference between eccrine and apocrine sweat glands?

**A:** Eccrine glands: found over entire skin surface (except lips, nail beds, glans penis); important for temperature regulation via sweating. Apocrine glands: found in axillae and perineum; empty into hair follicles; vestigial in humans (pheromone function in other species).

Eccrine glands are the primary mechanism of heat dissipation through evaporative cooling. Apocrine glands are NOT part of the pilosebaceous unit, unlike sebaceous glands.

**Q:** What are the four key functions of the skin?

**A:** 1. Protection (UV, mechanical/chemical, pathogens, dehydration) 2. Thermal regulation (vasoconstriction, sweating) 3. Sensation (touch, pain, temperature) 4. Metabolic function (vitamin D synthesis, energy storage as triglycerides).

Melanin provides UV protection; lipid-rich barrier prevents dehydration; Langerhans cells provide immune surveillance. Vitamin D synthesis in the skin is a key metabolic function.

**Q:** How do you classify primary skin lesions by size and character?

**A:** Flat: macule (<1 cm), patch (≥1 cm). Raised superficial: papule (<1 cm), plaque (≥1 cm). Deep palpable: nodule (<1 cm), tumour (≥1 cm). Elevated fluid-filled: vesicle (<1 cm), bulla (≥1 cm).

Describing lesions uses the SCALDA mnemonic: Size, Colour, Arrangement, Location, Distribution, Associated findings. Primary lesions are de-novo and unaltered.

**Q:** What is lichenification and what condition is it classically associated with?

**A:** Lichenification is thickening of the skin with accentuation of normal skin markings, classically associated with chronic atopic dermatitis (eczema) due to repeated rubbing/scratching.

Lichenification is a secondary lesion resulting from chronic mechanical trauma to skin. It reflects epidermal hyperplasia and is a hallmark of chronically pruritic conditions.

**Q:** What is the difference between petechiae, purpura, and ecchymosis?

**A:** Petechiae: pinpoint (<3 mm), non-blanchable hemorrhagic lesions. Purpura: 3 mm–1 cm, non-blanchable. Ecchymosis (bruise): >1 cm. All are non-blanchable due to extravasated blood in the dermis.

Non-blanchability distinguishes these from vascular lesions (telangiectasias, hemangiomas) which blanch with pressure. Petechiae can signal thrombocytopenia, vasculitis, or meningococcemia.

**Q:** What is a wheal and what condition is it classically associated with?

**A:** A wheal is a transient (<24 h), blanchable, raised lesion with a halo and central clearing caused by dermal oedema. It is classically associated with urticaria.

Wheals are caused by histamine release from mast cells leading to local oedema. Their transient nature (<24 h) and blanchability distinguish them from fixed lesions like plaques.

**Q:** What is the Koebner phenomenon and which dermatoses exhibit it?

**A:** The Koebner (isomorphic) phenomenon is the appearance of new skin lesions at sites of trauma or injury. Classic examples: lichen planus, psoriasis, and vitiligo.

The Koebner phenomenon is important clinically as it means trauma (scratching, surgery, sunburn) can induce new lesions. Recognizing this pattern helps identify the underlying dermatosis.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of seborrheic keratosis?

**A:** Well-demarcated, waxy papule/plaque with a classic 'stuck on' appearance; may be pigmented, warty, or greasy over time. Sites: face, trunk, upper extremities (NOT palms or soles). Benign keratinocyte/melanocyte proliferation.

Seborrheic keratoses are very common in those over 30 years, more common in men, and have autosomal dominant inheritance. Biopsy only if diagnosis is uncertain. Cosmetic treatment: cryotherapy, electrodessication, shave excision.

**Q:** How do you differentiate a corn, wart, and callus on the foot?

**A:** Corn: firm, translucent keratinous core; painful with direct pressure; preserves dermatoglyphics. Wart: bleeds with paring, black speckles (thrombosed capillaries), destroys dermatoglyphics. Callus: layers of yellowish keratin; no disruption of epidermal ridges.

Dermatoglyphics (fingerprint ridges) are preserved in corns and calluses but destroyed by warts. This distinction is diagnostically helpful when examining hyperkeratotic foot lesions.

**Q:** What distinguishes a keloid from a hypertrophic scar?

**A:** Keloid: extends BEYOND the margins of the original injury, with claw-like extensions; can continue expanding for years. Hypertrophic scar: confined to the original margins of injury.

Keloids are most common in Black patients and Asian patients, typically between ages 10–30. Management includes intralesional steroids, silicone gel sheets, and excision (though keloids often recur after excision).

**Q:** What is an epidermal cyst and how does it present?

**A:** Epidermal cyst: round, yellow/flesh-coloured, slow-growing, mobile, firm, fluctuant nodule with a central punctum. Contents are keratinous debris with foul cheesy odour on rupture. The most common cutaneous cyst.

Epidermal cysts form from displaced epithelial cells in the dermis. Rupture leads to a foreign body inflammatory reaction. Elective excision is definitive; no treatment needed unless symptomatic.

**Q:** What is a pilar (trichilemmal) cyst and how does it differ from an epidermal cyst?

**A:** Pilar cyst: multiple, hard nodules under the SCALP; lacks central punctum; filled with dense keratin. Second most common cutaneous cyst. More commonly in women; hereditary tendency.

Unlike epidermal cysts, pilar cysts have no central punctum and are most commonly found on the scalp. They are lined with stratified squamous epithelium without a granular layer.

**Q:** What is a dermatofibroma and what clinical sign confirms the diagnosis?

**A:** Dermatofibroma: firm, skin-coloured to red-brown dermal papule/nodule, often on legs. The dimple sign (Fitzpatrick's sign): lateral compression causes dimpling of the lesion.

Dermatofibromas are benign fibroblast proliferations, more common in adult women, often following minor trauma or insect bites. Eruptive dermatofibromas may be associated with SLE. No treatment required; excision if bothersome.

**Q:** What are skin tags (acrochordons) and who is at risk?

**A:** Skin tags: small (1–10 mm), soft, pedunculated papules on eyelids, neck, axillae, inframammary, and groin. Risk factors: middle-aged/elderly, obesity, female sex, pregnancy.

Skin tags are benign outgrowths of skin and increase in size/number during pregnancy. They can be associated with insulin resistance/type 2 diabetes. Management: snip excision, electrodessication, or cryosurgery.

**Q:** What is the significance of café-au-lait macules and how many are required to suggest neurofibromatosis type 1?

**A:** Café-au-lait macules are flat, light-brown lesions. Six or more café-au-lait macules (each >5 mm prepubertal, >15 mm postpubertal) is suggestive of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Also associated with McCune-Albright syndrome.

Café-au-lait macules result from increased melanogenesis. In NF1, they are one of the major diagnostic criteria (along with Lisch nodules, axillary freckling, neurofibromas, optic glioma, and family history).

**Q:** What are the three stages of acquired nevomelanocytic nevi evolution?

**A:** 1. Junctional NMN (childhood): flat, melanocytes at dermal-epidermal junction. 2. Compound NMN (any age): domed papule, melanocytes at junction AND migrating into dermis. 3. Dermal NMN (adults): soft dome-shaped papule, melanocytes exclusively in dermis.

Acquired nevi evolve from junctional to compound to dermal over time. Compound and dermal nevi are NOT found on palms or soles. New or changing nevi require evaluation for atypical features.

**Q:** What is melasma, who is it most common in, and how is it treated?

**A:** Melasma: symmetrical hyperpigmentation on sun-exposed areas of the face (forehead, upper lip, cheeks, chin). Most common in women, during pregnancy, or with OCP/HRT use. Treatment: tinted mineral sunscreen, hydroquinone, azelaic acid, retinoic acid, TCS, chemical peels.

Melasma is driven by oestrogen and progesterone stimulating melanocyte activity, worsened by sun exposure. It is very difficult to treat and can recur. Camouflage makeup may be helpful adjunctively.

**Q:** What is the difference between ephelides (freckles) and solar lentigines (liver spots)?

**A:** Ephelides (freckles): small (<5 mm) light-brown macules, common in skin phototypes I–II, darken with sun, fade in winter, no treatment required. Solar lentigines: well-demarcated brown/black macules in adults >40 years, benign, treated with laser or cryotherapy.

Both represent sun-related pigmented changes, but solar lentigines persist regardless of sun exposure (unlike freckles) and are more common in fair-skinned adults. Both must be distinguished from lentigo maligna.

**Q:** What is a halo nevus and is it associated with any systemic condition?

**A:** Halo nevus: typical nevus surrounded by a ring of depigmentation. Common in children. Uncommonly associated with vitiligo. No treatment required unless irregular colour or borders are noted.

The depigmented halo around a nevus reflects immune-mediated destruction of melanocytes. While usually benign, its association with vitiligo means that investigating for other autoimmune conditions may be warranted.

**Q:** What distinguishes vascular tumours from vascular malformations?

**A:** Vascular tumours (e.g., hemangiomas): endothelial hyperplasia, usually postnatal onset, M:F = 1:3–5, undergo phases (proliferating → involuting → involuted). Vascular malformations: congenital malformation with normal endothelial turnover, 100% present at birth, M:F = 1:1, proportionate growth.

This distinction is critical for management: hemangiomas often regress spontaneously (propranolol can accelerate involution), while vascular malformations do not involute and may require intervention.

**Q:** What is a bullous lesion and which conditions characteristically cause bulla formation?

**A:** Bulla: elevated fluid-filled lesion ≥1 cm in diameter. Autoimmune causes: bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus vulgaris. Infectious: bullous impetigo. Drug reactions: fixed drug eruption, SJS/TEN. Other: dermatitis herpetiformis, porphyria cutanea tarda.

Differentiating the level of cleavage (intraepidermal vs. subepidermal) and serology (anti-desmoglein for pemphigus, anti-BP230/BP180 for pemphigoid) guides diagnosis and management.

**Q:** What are the key features that differentiate an open comedone (blackhead) from a closed comedone (whitehead)?

**A:** Open comedone (blackhead): pore is open, oxidized sebum/keratin gives dark appearance. Closed comedone (whitehead): pore is closed by skin, contents appear white/flesh-coloured. Both are non-inflammatory acne lesions.

Comedones are the primary lesions of acne vulgaris, representing collections of sebum and keratin in the pilosebaceous unit. Comedonal acne responds to topical retinoids.

**Q:** What pattern of skin lesion distribution is seen in cutaneous larva migrans?

**A:** Cutaneous larva migrans shows a serpiginous ('snake-like') pattern of lesions, representing the migratory track of hookworm larvae through the skin.

Serpiginous distribution is a key pattern to recognize. Cutaneous larva migrans (typically Ancylostoma braziliense) is treated with ivermectin or albendazole.

**Q:** A patient develops targetoid (bullseye) skin lesions. What is the differential diagnosis?

**A:** Target/targetoid concentric ring lesions: erythema multiforme (EM), urticaria, SJS/TEN.

Erythema multiforme (often triggered by HSV or mycoplasma) classically shows target lesions. SJS/TEN are severe drug reactions. Urticaria can also show target-like wheals. Distinguishing these is critical for management.

**Q:** What skin lesion follows a 'drop-like' pattern and what classic dermatosis is it associated with?

**A:** Guttate pattern ('drop-like'): classic in guttate psoriasis, typically triggered by streptococcal pharyngitis in children and young adults.

Guttate psoriasis presents with numerous small (<1 cm) scaly papules scattered over the trunk and extremities after a streptococcal throat infection. It may be self-limiting or progress to chronic plaque psoriasis.

**Q:** What is Becker's nevus and who does it typically affect?

**A:** Becker's nevus: hairy, light-brown macule/patch with papular verrucous surface on the trunk and shoulders. Affects males more than females, becoming noticeable at puberty. A pigmented hamartoma with increased basal cell melanin.

Becker's nevus is usually too large to remove and is managed cosmetically. Hair growth follows onset of pigmentation. It should be distinguished from hairy congenital melanocytic nevi.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of Bullous Pemphigoid and what antibodies are involved?

**A:** IgG antibodies against dermal-epidermal basement membrane hemidesmosome proteins BP180 and/or BP230, leading to subepidermal bullae formation.

Bullous pemphigoid is the most common autoimmune blistering disease in older adults (mean age 60-80). Immunofluorescence shows linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane zone.

**Q:** How do you distinguish Bullous Pemphigoid from Pemphigus Vulgaris clinically?

**A:** Bullous Pemphigoid: tense subepidermal bullae, no Nikolsky sign, elderly patients, less mucosal involvement. Pemphigus Vulgaris: flaccid intraepidermal bullae, positive Nikolsky sign, 15-50 yr, extensive mucosal involvement (90% oral).

The mnemonic 'PemphigoiD = Deeper, tense lesions at the dermal-epidermal junction' vs 'Vulgaris = Superficial, intraepidermal, flaccid lesions' helps differentiate. The level of split determines the clinical appearance.

**Q:** What is the first-line management for Bullous Pemphigoid?

**A:** Prednisone 0.5-1 mg/kg/day until clear, then taper, plus steroid-sparing agents (azathioprine, cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil). Potent topical corticosteroids (clobetasol) may be as effective for limited disease.

For refractory cases, IVIg, rituximab, dupilumab, or omalizumab may be used. Bullous pemphigoid typically heals without scarring and is rarely fatal.

**Q:** What antibodies are involved in Pemphigus Vulgaris and what is the immunofluorescence pattern?

**A:** IgG antibodies against epidermal desmoglein-1 and desmoglein-3, causing loss of intercellular adhesion. Immunofluorescence shows intraepidermal IgG and C3 deposition ('tombstone' or 'chicken wire' pattern).

Circulating serum anti-desmoglein IgG antibodies can also be detected. Lesions heal with hyperpigmentation but without scarring; may be fatal without immunosuppression.

**Q:** What is the management of Pemphigus Vulgaris?

**A:** Prednisone 1-2 mg/kg until no new blisters, then 1-1.5 mg/kg until clear, then taper. Add steroid-sparing agents: azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine, IVIg, mycophenolate mofetil, or rituximab.

Pemphigus Vulgaris is potentially fatal if untreated; aggressive immunosuppression is required. Rituximab is increasingly used as a steroid-sparing agent.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and cutaneous finding in Dermatitis Herpetiformis?

**A:** Transglutaminase IgA deposits in the skin (alone or in immune complexes), leading to eosinophil and neutrophil infiltration. Presents as grouped, bilaterally symmetrical pruritic papules/vesicles on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees, sacrum, buttocks, scalp).

Almost all patients carry HLA-DQ2 or DQ8. 90% have gluten-sensitive enteropathy; untreated comorbid celiac disease increases risk of intestinal lymphoma.

**Q:** How is Dermatitis Herpetiformis managed?

**A:** Dapsone (or sulfapyridine if intolerant) for skin symptoms, plus lifelong gluten-free diet to reduce risk of intestinal lymphoma.

Immunofluorescence of perilesional skin shows IgA deposits. A gluten-free diet can reduce lymphoma risk associated with untreated celiac disease.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and pathophysiology of Porphyria Cutanea Tarda?

**A:** Skin fragility with tense vesicles/bullae on photo-exposed areas (dorsum of hands, nose, upper trunk), scarring, milia, periorbital violaceous discoloration, facial hypertrichosis. Caused by uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase deficiency leading to excess heme precursors.

Associated triggers include alcohol, estrogen therapy, NSAIDs, HIV, hepatitis C, hemochromatosis, and DM. Urine shows orange-red fluorescence under Wood's lamp.

**Q:** What is the management of Porphyria Cutanea Tarda?

**A:** Discontinue aggravating substances (alcohol, estrogen therapy), phlebotomy to decrease body iron load, and low-dose hydroxychloroquine.

Phlebotomy reduces iron stores which exacerbate the enzyme deficiency. Hydroxychloroquine binds uroporphyrins and promotes their excretion. Investigations include 24h urine elevated uroporphyrins and elevated stool coproporphyrins.

**Q:** What is Ichthyosis Vulgaris and how is it inherited?

**A:** Autosomal dominant disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in the filaggrin gene (FLG), causing reduced filaggrin protein and abnormal retention of keratinocytes (hyperkeratosis). Presents as xerosis with fine scaling and large adherent 'fish scales'; spares flexural creases.

Incidence 1:100-1:250. Associated with atopic dermatitis and keratosis pilaris. Improves in summer and with humidity. Management: emollients, humectants, urea or hydroxy acid-containing creams; intermittent systemic retinoids for severe cases.

**Q:** What systemic conditions are associated with Neurofibromatosis Type 1?

**A:** Pheochromocytoma, astrocytoma, bilateral acoustic neuromas, bone cysts, scoliosis, precocious puberty, developmental delay, and renal artery stenosis.

NF1 results from pathogenic variants in the neurofibromin gene, causing abnormal proliferation of neural crest elements (Schwann cells, melanocytes). Annual follow-up is recommended for brain tumours (astrocytoma).

**Q:** What autoimmune conditions should be screened for in vitiligo?

**A:** Thyroid disease, pernicious anemia, Addison's disease, and Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM).

Vitiligo affects 1:100 with 30% positive family history. It results from autoimmune destruction of melanocytes, presenting as sharply demarcated depigmented white patches. Wood's lamp examination shows porcelain white discoloration (amelanosis).

**Q:** What are the treatment options for vitiligo?

**A:** Sun avoidance/protection, topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) or topical corticosteroids, PUVA or narrowband UVB (NB-UVB), topical ruxolitinib (JAK inhibitor) for nonsegmental vitiligo ≥12 years. Camouflage and bleaching with monobenzyl ether of hydroquinone 20% for widespread depigmentation.

Systematic reviews favor NB-UVB over PUVA and combination therapy over monotherapy. Topical ruxolitinib is newly approved (pending Canadian approval as of this writing). NB-UVB has fewer side effects than PUVA.

**Q:** How does erysipelas differ from cellulitis clinically and microbiologically?

**A:** Erysipelas: upper dermis, GAS only, confluent erythematous SHARPLY RAISED well-demarcated plaque, very painful, spreads via lymphatics, often facial/leg. Cellulitis: lower dermis/subcutaneous fat, GAS + S. aureus, POORLY demarcated flat lesion with vesicles, more commonly legs.

Both are clinical diagnoses. Key distinction is the raised, sharp border of erysipelas vs the flat, poorly defined border of cellulitis. First-line treatment for erysipelas is penicillin or cephalexin; for cellulitis, cloxacillin or cefazolin/cephalexin.

**Q:** What is the first-line antibiotic treatment for non-purulent cellulitis?

**A:** Cloxacillin or cefazolin/cephalexin (covers GAS and S. aureus). For MRSA-positive or hospitalized patients: vancomycin. In children: cefuroxime. For diabetic foot infections: TMP/SMX and metronidazole.

Cellulitis involves the lower dermis and subcutaneous fat, most commonly caused by GAS and S. aureus. Haemophilus influenzae is a cause in periorbital cellulitis. Pasteurella multocida is seen in dog/cat bite infections.

**Q:** What is the management of impetigo?

**A:** Localized: topical antibiotics (mupirocin 2% or fusidic acid 2% TID for 7-10 days after resolution, or ozenaxacin 1% cream BID for 5 days). Extensive: systemic antibiotics (dicloxacillin or cephalexin for 7-10 days).

Impetigo is caused by GAS, S. aureus, or both. It presents as golden yellow 'honey-crusted' lesions on the face, arms, legs, and buttocks. Fusidic acid 2% is available in Canada (not available in the USA).

**Q:** What are the differences among superficial folliculitis, furuncles, and carbuncles?

**A:** Superficial folliculitis: superficial infection of hair follicle, dome-shaped pustule at follicle mouth, treated with topical antibiotics. Furuncles (boils): deeper, red tender nodule with central pustule involving subcutaneous tissue, caused by S. aureus, treat with incision and drainage ± oral antibiotics. Carbuncles: multiple coalescing furuncles draining through multiple openings, systemic symptoms possible.

Hot tub folliculitis is caused by Pseudomonas. Furuncles near the external auditory canal or nose, or with surrounding cellulitis, require oral cloxacillin or cephalexin for 1-2 weeks.

**Q:** When are antibiotics indicated for skin abscesses after incision and drainage?

**A:** Antibiotics are indicated for: severe local infection, systemic signs of infection, history of multiple/recurrent abscesses, underlying comorbidities, immunosuppression, failure of initial therapy, extremes of age, special exposures (e.g. animal bites), or prophylaxis against infective endocarditis.

Clinical trial evidence shows clindamycin or TMP-SMX after incision and drainage improves short-term cure rates for simple abscesses (83% vs 68% placebo). Culture is not required in healthy patients not receiving antibiotics.

**Q:** What is the KOH preparation used for in dermatology and what does it detect?

**A:** Potassium hydroxide (KOH) preparation of skin scrapings, hair, or nail clippings dissolves keratin and allows microscopic visualization of fungal hyphae and mycelia, confirming dermatophyte infections.

KOH prep is the primary investigation for dermatophytoses (tinea infections). Wood's lamp shows green fluorescence only for Microsporum infections (not Trichophyton). Culture on Sabouraud's agar can identify species.

**Q:** What are the first-line treatments for tinea corporis, tinea cruris, and tinea pedis?

**A:** Topical antifungals as first-line: clotrimazole, ketoconazole, terbinafine, or ciclopirox olamine cream applied BID for 2-4 weeks.

Oral therapy is reserved for onychomycosis and tinea capitis (which requires oral agents to penetrate the hair root). Topical agents are inadequate for nail/hair infections.

**Q:** What is the treatment of tinea capitis and why must it be oral?

**A:** Oral terbinafine (Lamisil) for 4 weeks. Oral agents are required because the dermatophyte resides in the hair root where topical agents cannot penetrate. Adjunctive antifungal shampoos (selenium sulfide 2.5%, ketoconazole, ciclopirox) can help prevent spread.

Tinea capitis primarily affects children (mainly Black children) and immunocompromised adults. Wood's lamp shows green fluorescence only for Microsporum (not Trichophyton). Kerion (boggy purulent nodule) can lead to scarring.

**Q:** What is the treatment of onychomycosis (tinea unguium)?

**A:** Oral: terbinafine (Lamisil) — 6 weeks for fingernails, 12 weeks for toenails; or itraconazole (Sporanox) pulse dosing (7 days on, 3 weeks off: 2 pulses for fingernails, 3 pulses for toenails). Topical: ciclopirox nail lacquer or efinaconazole (Jublia) for 48 weeks.

T. rubrum causes 90% of toenail infections. Terbinafine is a CYP2D6 inhibitor with potential liver toxicity; itraconazole is a CYP3A4 inhibitor with potential liver toxicity. Topical agents alone are often insufficient.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and treatment of Cutaneous Larva Migrans?

**A:** Erythematous pruritic papules at site of initial infection progressing to serpiginous (winding) red pruritic lines as larvae migrate under the skin. Most common on feet, legs, buttocks. Caused by hookworm larvae (dog/cat feces). Self-limiting; consider anthelmintics and antihistamines.

Most common in tropical or subtropical regions. The serpiginous track is pathognomonic. Anthelmintics such as ivermectin or albendazole can accelerate resolution.

**Q:** A 25-year-old presents with grouped, intensely pruritic vesicles on bilateral elbows and sacrum. Biopsy shows IgA deposits in perilesional skin. What is the diagnosis and associated systemic condition?

**A:** Dermatitis Herpetiformis. Associated with gluten-sensitive enteropathy (celiac disease) — 90% have it, though only 20% have intestinal symptoms. HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 present in almost all cases.

Management is dapsone for skin disease and lifelong gluten-free diet to control both conditions and reduce the risk of intestinal lymphoma. Also check for associated thyroid disease (30%), iron/folate deficiency.

**Q:** What organisms cause erysipelas and what are its first-line antibiotics?

**A:** Caused by GAS (Group A Streptococcus). First-line: penicillin, cephalexin, cloxacillin, or cefazolin. Second-line: clindamycin. If allergic to penicillin: erythromycin.

Erysipelas involves the upper dermis and lymphatics, presenting as a sharply raised, well-demarcated, confluent erythematous plaque ('St. Anthony's fire'). Common sites: face and legs.

**Q:** What are the key clinical features distinguishing a hemangioma of infancy from a port-wine stain (nevus flammeus)?

**A:** Hemangioma of infancy appears shortly after birth, increases in size over months then regresses (50% resolve by age 5); port-wine stain is present at birth, follows a dermatomal distribution, never spontaneously regresses, and grows proportionally with the child.

Hemangiomas are benign vascular tumours with endothelial proliferation that involute; port-wine stains are congenital vascular malformations of dermal capillaries that are permanent. Sturge-Weber syndrome should be considered when port-wine stain involves V1/V2 distribution.

**Q:** When does a hemangioma of infancy require treatment?

**A:** ~10% require treatment due to functional impairment (visual compromise, airway obstruction, high-output cardiac failure) or cosmesis. Options: topical timolol, systemic propranolol, systemic corticosteroids, laser, or surgery. Provide early specialist referral for high-risk hemangiomas.

Propranolol is the mainstay of systemic treatment for problematic hemangiomas in infancy. High-risk locations (periorbital, subglottic, beard distribution) warrant early intervention to prevent complications.

**Q:** What is a spider angioma (Campbell Telangiectasia) and what conditions are associated with increased numbers?

**A:** Central red arteriole with slender blanchable branches. Associated with hyperestrogenic states (liver disease, pregnancy, OCP use), but more often has no underlying cause. Blanches when pressure applied to the central arteriole.

The blanchable nature distinguishes spider angiomas from petechiae. Hyperestrogenism explains their increase in pregnancy and chronic liver disease (impaired estrogen metabolism). Electrodesiccation or laser if cosmetically desired.

**Q:** How do you differentiate pyogenic granuloma from cherry angioma?

**A:** Pyogenic granuloma: rapidly developing (<30 y/o), friable dome-shaped pedunculated/sessile nodule (fingers, lips, mouth, trunk, toes) that bleeds easily, grows rapidly over weeks-months. Cherry angioma (>30 y/o): bright red to deep maroon dome-shaped papules (1-5mm) on trunk, less friable, lesions do not fade but are stable.

Pyogenic granuloma requires surgical excision with histologic examination to rule out mimics (nodular melanoma, SCC, nodular BCC). Cherry angiomas usually require no treatment but can be treated with laser or electrocautery.

**Q:** What is nevus simplex (salmon patch) and how does it differ from nevus flammeus (port-wine stain)?

**A:** Nevus simplex: pink-red irregular patches, congenital dilation of dermal capillaries; present in 3/newborns; midline on glabella ('Angel Kiss') or nuchal region ('Stork Bite'); majority regress spontaneously — no treatment required. Port-wine stain: dermatomal, unilateral, never regresses, may associate with Sturge-Weber.

Salmon patches are the most common vascular birthmark and are benign with no associated syndromes. Port-wine stains require laser treatment and neurologic evaluation if in V1/V2 territory for Sturge-Weber syndrome.

**Q:** What are xanthomas and what are the main variants?

**A:** Localized lipid deposits in the skin. Variants: (1) eruptive xanthoma — 1-5mm erythematous-to-yellow papules (hypertriglyceridemia); (2) tuberous xanthoma — <3cm yellow-to-orange nodules over joints; (3) tendinous xanthoma — firm, mobile, skin-coloured nodules in tendons; (4) plane xanthoma — soft, yellow thin plaques (may associate with monoclonal gammopathy).

Most xanthomas are associated with hyperlipidemia; plane xanthomas can occur with multiple myeloma or monoclonal gammopathy without hyperlipidemia. A fasting lipid panel is indicated for most xanthoma types.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and classification of acne vulgaris?

**A:** Type I: comedonal, sparse, no scarring. Type II: comedonal, papular, moderate + little scarring. Type III: comedonal, papular, pustular, with scarring. Type IV: nodulocystic, risk of severe scarring. Pathophysiology: (1) hyperkeratinization at follicular ostia blocks sebum → microcomedones; (2) androgens → excess sebum; (3) C. acnes metabolizes sebum → pro-inflammatory mediators.

Acne vulgaris is the most common inflammatory pilosebaceous disease. Understanding the classification drives treatment intensity — Type IV requires isotretinoin consideration.

**Q:** What is the management approach for mild, moderate, and severe acne?

**A:** Mild: OTC benzoyl peroxide (BPO) ± topical retinoid (backbone of all regimens) ± topical antimicrobial (clindamycin — avoid monotherapy due to resistance). Moderate: add oral tetracycline/doxycycline/minocycline (3 months before assessing efficacy); consider cyproterone acetate-ethinyl estradiol (Diane-35) or spironolactone for females. Severe: isotretinoin (Accutane®/Epuris®).

All acne regimens should include a retinoid unless not tolerated. Antibiotics should be combined with BPO to minimize resistance. Isotretinoin requires baseline lipids, LFTs, and pregnancy test (teratogen — two forms of contraception required).

**Q:** What are the key contraindications and monitoring requirements for isotretinoin (Accutane)?

**A:** Contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogen — facial dysmorphism, cognitive impairment, spontaneous abortion). Requires: baseline lipid profile (risk of hypertriglyceridemia), LFTs, and β-hCG before treatment. Patients must use two forms of contraception. May transiently worsen acne before improvement.

Isotretinoin is the most effective treatment for severe/nodulocystic acne. Do not combine with tetracyclines (risk of pseudotumour cerebri). Evidence does NOT support a causal link between isotretinoin and IBD or depression/suicidality.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of perioral dermatitis and how is it managed?

**A:** Discrete erythematous papulopustular eruptions becoming confluent on perioral, perinasal, and/or periorbital skin; symmetrical with rim of sparing around vermillion border of lips. Predominantly females 15-40 y/o. Management: stop all TCS, avoid ointment/oil-based products and cosmetics; topical metronidazole 0.75% BID; systemic doxycycline (anti-inflammatory).

Topical corticosteroids are a key trigger/perpetuator of perioral dermatitis — stopping TCS is essential but may cause initial flare. Topical calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus) can be used adjunctively.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of rosacea and how does it differ from acne vulgaris?

**A:** Rosacea: dome-shaped inflammatory papules ± pustules, flushing, non-transient erythema, telangiectasia on central face (forehead, nose, cheeks, chin). Key differentiator from acne: ABSENCE of comedones, predilection for central face, symptoms of flushing. Affects fair-skinned people, 30-50 y/o, F>M.

Rhinophyma (phymatous change of nose) is a late complication. Ocular manifestations include blepharoconjunctivitis and keratitis. Triggers include heat, cold, wind, sun, alcohol, spicy food, and stress.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for rosacea?

**A:** First line: oral tetracyclines (e.g. doxycycline modified-release capsule — APPRILON) and/or topical metronidazole or topical azelaic acid. Topical ivermectin is also first line. Second line: oral metronidazole, topical clindamycin. Third line: oral retinoids.

Year-round sunscreen and avoidance of triggers are foundational. Telangiectasias are treated with vascular lasers or intense pulsed light. Phymatous changes require physical ablation (electrosurgery, CO2 laser).

**Q:** What are the clinical features and pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis (AD)?

**A:** Subacute/chronic eczematous reaction with prolonged severe pruritus; lichenification and excoriations secondary to scratching. Filaggrin gene mutation → impaired barrier function. Associated with atopy triad (asthma, allergic rhinitis, AD), eosinophilia. Distribution varies by age: infants — face/trunk/extensors; children — antecubital/popliteal fossae; adults — flexural pattern.

AD is a T-cell driven inflammatory process with epidermal barrier dysfunction. Affects 10-20% of children <5 yr in developed countries; 1/3 persist into adulthood. Associated with psychiatric comorbidities including depression.

**Q:** What is the stepwise pharmacologic management of atopic dermatitis (AD)?

**A:** Mild flares: topical corticosteroids (TCS) BID x ≥2 weeks; topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.03%/0.1%, pimecrolimus 1%) as steroid-sparing agents for face/neck. Moderate: topical PDE-4 inhibitor (crisaborole), topical JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib). Severe refractory: oral JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib); biologics (dupilumab anti-IL-4/13, tralokinumab anti-IL-13, lebrikizumab anti-IL-13); methotrexate, cyclosporine, oral steroids.

All patients need moisturizers applied liberally daily. Topical calcineurin inhibitors have no skin atrophy risk and are preferred for sensitive areas. Health Canada removed prior black box warnings on tacrolimus/pimecrolimus.

**Q:** What are the local side effects of topical corticosteroids (TCS)?

**A:** Common: skin atrophy, purpura, telangiectasia, striae, acneiform eruption. Rare: hypopigmentation (more common in patients of colour with high-potency TCS, prolonged use without breaks).

The choice of TCS potency depends on age, body site, and duration of use. For acute flares, apply one adult fingertip unit (0.5g) to an area the size of two adult palms BID for at least 2 weeks.

**Q:** What differentiates irritant contact dermatitis from allergic contact dermatitis?

**A:** Irritant CD: toxic (non-immune) mechanism; occurs in anyone with sufficient irritant concentration; acute (sharp margins from acids/alkali) or cumulative (slow, poorly defined margins from soap); hands most common. Allergic CD: type IV delayed hypersensitivity; patient acquires sensitivity that persists indefinitely; erythema with papulovesicular eruption; requires patch testing to identify allergen. Common allergens: nickel, fragrance, tattoo pigments.

Patch testing is the gold standard for diagnosing allergic contact dermatitis. Management of irritant CD focuses on barrier moisturizers and avoidance; allergic CD requires allergen avoidance and cross-reactant avoidance.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and management of dyshidrotic dermatitis?

**A:** Papulovesicular or bullous dermatitis of palms, soles, and dorsal hands/feet ('tapioca pudding' appearance), coalescing into plaques with painful fissuring; acute stage very pruritic. NOT caused by hyperhidrosis. Management: high-potency TCS with plastic cling wrap occlusion; prednisone for severe cases; alitretinoin (Toctino) for chronic hand dermatitis; antibiotics for secondary S. aureus infection.

Dyshidrotic eczema (pompholyx) is a distinct form of hand/foot dermatitis. Alitretinoin (oral retinoid) is specifically approved in Canada for chronic hand dermatitis unresponsive to TCS.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and management of seborrheic dermatitis?

**A:** Greasy, erythematous, yellow, scaling papules/plaques in sebaceous gland-rich areas: scalp, nasolabial folds, eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, post-auricular, sternum. Caused by Malassezia spp. yeast. Increased severity with HIV and Parkinson's disease. Management: face — ketoconazole cream BID ± mild steroid cream ± roflumilast foam 0.3%; scalp — ketoconazole 2% shampoo (Nizoral), selenium sulfide, zinc pyrithione shampoos.

In infants, seborrheic dermatitis presents as 'cradle cap.' In adults, it can cause dandruff (pityriasis sicca). Can overlap with psoriasis on scalp (sebopsoriasis) and rosacea on face (seborosacea).

**Q:** What are the features and management of stasis dermatitis?

**A:** Erythematous, scaly, pruritic plaques on lower legs (particularly medial ankle), usually bilateral with swelling, hemosiderin deposition, woody fibrosis, and lipodermatosclerosis in late stages. Caused by chronic venous insufficiency. Management: compression stockings (most important — if no peripheral arterial disease), rest/elevate legs, moisturizer, mid-high potency TCS. Complications: ulceration (medial malleolus), secondary bacterial infections.

Check ankle-brachial index before applying compression stockings to rule out peripheral arterial disease. Doppler ultrasound if DVT suspected.

**Q:** What are the '6 Ps' of lichen planus and what is pathognomonic for oral lichen planus?

**A:** 6 Ps: Purple, Pruritic, Polygonal, Planar (flat-topped), Papules, Plaques. Wickham's striae (lacy, whitish reticular network) on oral mucosa is pathognomonic. Common sites: wrists, nails, scalp, mucous membranes (mouth, vulva, glans). Associated with Koebner phenomenon and rarely hepatitis C.

Lichen planus is immune-mediated (lymphocyte activation → keratinocyte apoptosis). Erosive oral lichen planus carries increased risk of SCC. Treatment: topical/intralesional steroids; severe cases may need phototherapy, oral retinoids, or immunosuppressants.

**Q:** What is pityriasis rosea and how does it present?

**A:** Acute, self-limiting eruption with red/flesh-coloured oval plaques with central scale that does not extend to edge. Preceded by 'herald patch' 1-2 weeks before secondary eruption. Lesions follow Langer lines parallel to ribs ('Christmas tree pattern' on back). Common on trunk and proximal limbs. Suspected HHV-6 or HHV-7 reactivation. Resolves spontaneously in 6-12 weeks — no treatment required.

Pityriasis rosea can resemble secondary syphilis (must exclude). In darker skin tones, lesions may appear dusky violet and leave post-inflammatory hypo/hyperpigmentation. Symptomatic treatment with TCS or emollients if pruritic.

**Q:** What are the five clinical subtypes of psoriasis?

**A:** 1. Plaque psoriasis (most common) — well-circumscribed erythematous plaques with silvery-white scales on extensor surfaces, scalp, nails. 2. Guttate psoriasis — raindrop-like salmon-pink papules, often post-streptococcal. 3. Erythrodermic psoriasis — >90% BSA erythema, potentially life-threatening. 4. Pustular psoriasis — sudden onset pustules, may follow steroid withdrawal. 5. Inverse psoriasis — flexural surfaces (axillae, inguinal, inframammary).

Psoriasis affects 1.5-2% of the population (M=F). Pathophysiology involves genetic and immunologic factors (Th1/Th17-mediated inflammation with shortened keratinocyte cell cycle). Risk factors: smoking, obesity, alcohol, drugs, infections, Koebner phenomenon.

**Q:** What is Auspitz sign and what does it indicate?

**A:** Auspitz sign: pinpoint bleeding observed when psoriatic scale is removed. It is characteristic of plaque psoriasis and results from the dilated capillary loops (elongated rete ridges) in the dermis that are revealed upon removing the overlying scale.

Auspitz sign reflects the pathologic changes of psoriasis: epidermal acanthosis, parakeratosis, and dilated tortuous capillaries in dermal papillae. It is not pathognomonic but is highly characteristic.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for plaque psoriasis based on severity?

**A:** Mild (BSA <3%, PASI <3): TCS + topical vitamin D analogue (calcipotriol) — first line; roflumilast cream (steroid-sparing, safe on face/folds); emollients. Moderate-Severe (BSA ≥3%, PASI ≥3): phototherapy (NB-UVB preferred over PUVA for carcinogenicity); systemic — methotrexate, cyclosporine (intermittent, <1 year), acitretin, apremilast (Otezla), deucravacitinib; biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL17, anti-IL23).

NB-UVB (311nm) is much less carcinogenic than PUVA; PUVA lifetime limit is <200 sessions due to melanoma/NMSC risk. Betamethasone + calcipotriol (Dovobet) is a convenient combination but cannot be used on face or skin folds.

**Q:** What are the systemic treatment options for psoriasis and their key adverse effects?

**A:** Acitretin: alopecia, cheilitis, teratogenicity, hepatotoxicity, hypertriglyceridemia. Cyclosporine: renal toxicity, hypertension, immunosuppression, lymphoma — use intermittently <1 year. Methotrexate: bone marrow toxicity, hepatic cirrhosis, teratogenicity. Apremilast (Otezla — PDE4 inhibitor): GI upset, headache, weight loss — very safe. Deucravacitinib (TYK2 inhibitor): acne, increased infection risk.

Acitretin requires two forms of contraception and pregnancy avoidance for 3 years after stopping (alcohol converts it to etretinate which is stored in fat). PUVA increases melanoma risk with >200 lifetime sessions.

**Q:** What biologics are approved in Canada for psoriasis and what are their targets?

**A:** Anti-TNF: etanercept (Enbrel), adalimumab (Humira), infliximab (Remicade), certolizumab (Cimzia). Anti-IL-12/23: ustekinumab (Stelara). Anti-IL-17A: secukinumab (Cosentyx), ixekizumab (Taltz). Anti-IL-17RA: brodalumab (Siliq). Anti-IL-17A/F: bimekizumab (BIMZELX). Anti-IL-23: guselkumab (Tremfya), risankizumab (Skyrizi), tildrakizumab (ILUMYA). Anti-IL-36R: spesolimab (SPEVIGO) — generalized pustular psoriasis only.

IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors are among the most effective biologics for psoriasis. Most anti-TNF and anti-IL-17/23 agents can also treat psoriatic arthritis. Biologics generally require TB screening before initiation.

**Q:** What triggers and manages guttate psoriasis?

**A:** Often preceded by streptococcal pharyngitis. Presents as discrete salmon-pink small scaling papules diffusely on trunk and legs, sparing palms and soles. Management: broadband UVB phototherapy (preferred over narrowband), sunlight, emollients; penicillin V, erythromycin, or azithromycin if GAS on throat culture.

Guttate psoriasis may be the first presentation of psoriasis or a flare of pre-existing disease. Anti-streptococcal treatment may help resolve the eruption but does not definitively prevent psoriasis progression.

**Q:** What makes erythrodermic psoriasis a medical emergency and how is it managed?

**A:** Potentially life-threatening: involves >90% BSA with erythema and fine desquamative scale. Complications: arthralgia, pruritus, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, thermoregulation failure. Aggravated by lithium, beta-blockers, NSAIDs, antimalarials, infection, phototoxic reactions. Management: hospitalization, IV fluids, electrolyte monitoring, treat underlying cause; systemic: cyclosporine, acitretin, methotrexate, UV, biologics.

Erythrodermic psoriasis can arise from inadequate treatment of other psoriasis subtypes or abrupt withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids. Risk of high-output cardiac failure, sepsis, and hypothermia.

**Q:** What is inverse psoriasis and how does its management differ from plaque psoriasis?

**A:** Inverse psoriasis: erythematous plaques on flexural surfaces (axillae, inframammary folds, gluteal fold, inguinal folds); lesions may be macerated. Management differs: use LOW-potency TCS (not high-potency due to skin friction/occlusion); topical vitamin D analogues (calcipotriene, calcitriol); topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus); topical PDE-4 inhibitors (roflumilast); phototherapy.

Occlusion in flexural areas increases TCS absorption and risk of atrophy — hence low-potency steroids are preferred. Roflumilast foam is steroid-sparing and can be used on folds and face.

**Q:** A 25-year-old female develops erythematous papulopustules symmetrically around her mouth with a clear zone around the vermillion border. She has been using topical betamethasone cream for 3 months. What is the diagnosis and first step in management?

**A:** Perioral dermatitis exacerbated by topical corticosteroid (TCS) use. First step: stop all TCS immediately (may cause initial flare/rebound). Then: avoid all ointment/oil-based products and cosmetics; apply topical metronidazole 0.75% BID; consider systemic doxycycline for its anti-inflammatory properties.

TCS are both a trigger and perpetuator of perioral dermatitis. The rim of sparing around the vermillion border and symmetrical perioral distribution are characteristic. This condition predominantly affects women aged 15-40.

**Q:** What is nummular dermatitis and how is it managed?

**A:** Coin-shaped (nummular), pruritic, dry, scaly, erythematous plaques with common secondary infection. Pathophysiology involves xerosis from epidermal lipid barrier dysfunction, allowing permeation of environmental agents. Management: moisturization; mid-high potency TCS ointment BID.

Nummular dermatitis is often confused with tinea corporis (ringworm) — KOH preparation and fungal culture can differentiate. Secondary bacterial infection (S. aureus) is common and should be treated with antibiotics.

**Q:** What is lichen simplex chronicus and how does it differ from atopic dermatitis?

**A:** Lichen simplex chronicus: well-defined plaque(s) of lichenified skin with increased skin markings and excoriations (neck, scalp, extremities, urogenital area). Results from skin hyperexcitability to itch → continued rubbing → lichenification. Often associated with atopy, anxiety disorders, chronic stress. Treatment: OTC antipruritics (topical pramoxine), topical steroids. Differs from AD: more localized, associated with chronic psychological stress, not a systemic atopic condition.

The itch-scratch cycle is central to lichen simplex chronicus. Breaking this cycle with antipruritics and TCS is the therapeutic goal. Punch biopsy confirms diagnosis if uncertain; KOH if fungal infection suspected.

**Q:** What is the association between seborrheic dermatitis and HIV/Parkinson's disease?

**A:** Both HIV and Parkinson's disease are associated with increased incidence AND severity of seborrheic dermatitis. Seborrheic dermatitis can be a marker of advanced HIV disease or newly diagnosed Parkinson's. The mechanism relates to immune dysregulation (HIV) and autonomic/sebaceous gland dysfunction (Parkinson's) promoting Malassezia overgrowth.

Severe, refractory, or suddenly worsening seborrheic dermatitis in a young adult should prompt consideration of HIV testing. In Parkinson's disease, increased sebum production and reduced facial movement may contribute.

**Q:** What are the topical vitamin D analogues used in psoriasis and what is their mechanism?

**A:** Calcipotriene/calcipotriol (Dovonex, Silkis): reduce keratinocyte hyperproliferation. Betamethasone + calcipotriene (Dovobet): combined TCS and vitamin D analogue — NOT to be used on face or folds. First-line for mild plaque psoriasis in combination with TCS. More effective than either agent alone.

Vitamin D analogues can cause local skin irritation and hypercalcemia with excessive use. Studies show that combinations of vitamin D analogues and TCS are more effective than either alone for chronic plaque psoriasis.

**Q:** What acne myths should be debunked when counselling patients?

**A:** Myths: (1) Eating greasy food/chocolate causes acne — FALSE; (2) Blackheads are caused by dirt — FALSE (oxidized fatty acids); (3) Acne is caused by poor hygiene — FALSE (excessive washing can aggravate). Evidence shows benzoyl peroxide is more effective than placebo but not superior to adapalene or clindamycin as monotherapy.

Patient education is essential in acne management. Diet does not cause acne, though some evidence links high glycemic index diets and dairy to acne severity. Gentle twice-daily cleansing is appropriate — over-washing disrupts the skin barrier.

**Q:** What biologics target IL-17 for psoriasis, and why is brodalumab unique in this class?

**A:** Anti-IL-17A: secukinumab (Cosentyx), ixekizumab (Taltz). Anti-IL-17A/F dual: bimekizumab (BIMZELX). Anti-IL-17RA receptor: brodalumab (Siliq) — unique because it targets the IL-17 receptor subunit A rather than IL-17A itself, blocking multiple IL-17 family cytokines (IL-17A, IL-17C, IL-17E, IL-17F).

IL-17 inhibitors are among the most effective biologics for moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Brodalumab has a black box warning for suicidal ideation and behaviour. Candida infections are a notable adverse effect of IL-17 inhibitors.

**Q:** A 45-year-old woman presents with bilateral lower leg erythema, scaling, pruritus, brownish discolouration, and woody fibrosis. What is the diagnosis and most important treatment?

**A:** Stasis dermatitis secondary to chronic venous insufficiency. Most important treatment: compression stockings (confirm no peripheral arterial disease by checking ankle-brachial index first). Additional: rest and leg elevation, daily moisturizer, mid-high potency TCS for inflammation.

Hemosiderin deposition (brown discolouration) and lipodermatosclerosis (woody fibrosis) are late-stage findings. The most feared complication is venous ulceration at the medial malleolus. Compression stockings address the underlying pathophysiology.

**Q:** What organism causes scabies, and what are its classic clinical features?

**A:** Sarcoptes scabiei (a mite). Classic features: superficial linear burrows, intense nocturnal pruritus, inflammatory papules/nodules in axilla and groin, secondary eczematous plaques and excoriations. Spares head and neck except in infants.

Scabies is transmitted by prolonged skin-to-skin contact and has an incubation of ~1 month before pruritus begins. Re-infection triggers hypersensitivity within 24 hours.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for scabies and how is it applied?

**A:** Permethrin 5% cream (Nix®): applied from neck down to soles of feet, left on 8–14 hours, with a second treatment 7 days after the first. Clothing and linens must be washed in hot water. Family and close contacts must also be treated.

Pruritus may persist for 2–3 weeks after effective treatment due to prolonged hypersensitivity; mid-potency topical corticosteroids and antihistamines can be used for symptom relief after permethrin treatment.

**Q:** What are the three species of lice that infest humans and their respective sites?

**A:** 1) Pediculus humanus capitis — scalp. 2) Phthirus pubis — pubic area. 3) Pediculus humanus humanus — body (nits/lice in clothing seams; excoriations on shoulders, belt-line, buttocks).

Body lice can transmit infectious agents including Bartonella quintana (trench fever) and Rickettsia prowazekii (epidemic typhus). Children are medically cleared to return to school after the first permethrin treatment.

**Q:** What is the characteristic bite pattern of bed bugs (Cimex lectularius) and how are they treated?

**A:** Bed bugs cause burning wheals turning to firm papules, characteristically in groups of three ('breakfast, lunch, and dinner'), on areas with easy access (face, neck, arms, legs, hands). Treatment: professional fumigation, insecticides to walls/furniture, removal of clutter; TCS and oral H1-antagonists for symptomatic relief.

Cimex lectularius hides in crevices in walls and furniture during the day and feeds at night. Lesional biopsy can confirm an insect bite reaction if needed.

**Q:** What virus causes herpangina and what are its clinical features?

**A:** Coxsackievirus A. Clinical features: small vesicles in the mouth evolving into painful shallow oral ulcers (1–5 mm), yellowish with erythematous base, associated with sore throat, dysphagia, and headache. Most common in children and young adults; self-limited.

Herpangina is highly contagious and managed with symptomatic treatment including acetaminophen for fever and pain. It is distinct from HSV gingivostomatitis by location (posterior oropharynx).

**Q:** What laboratory test shows multinucleated giant epithelial cells in HSV infection?

**A:** Tzanck smear with Giemsa stain shows multinucleated giant epithelial cells. Additional tests include viral culture, PCR, electron microscopy, and direct fluorescence antibody test from the unroofed base of a new lesion.

The Tzanck smear is not specific for HSV vs VZV. PCR is the most sensitive and specific test for HSV. Serologic antibody testing can confirm current or past infection.

**Q:** What are the complications of HSV infection?

**A:** Dendritic corneal ulcer, erythema multiforme (EM), herpes simplex encephalitis (infants at risk), and Kaposi's varicelliform eruption (eczema herpeticum) — HSV infection superimposed on atopic dermatitis.

Erythema multiforme most commonly follows HSV or Mycoplasma infection; prophylactic acyclovir for 6–12 months is indicated for HSV-associated EM with frequent recurrences.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of herpes zoster (shingles) and what is Hutchinson's sign?

**A:** Unilateral dermatomal eruption (vesicles, bullae, pustules on erythematous edematous base) occurring 3–5 days after pain/paresthesia of the affected dermatome. Most common distribution: thoracic (50%), trigeminal (10–20%), cervical (10–20%). Hutchinson's sign: shingles on the tip of the nose signifies ocular involvement (nasociliary branch of V1).

VZV reactivation is the cause; risk factors include old age, immunosuppression, and hematologic malignancy. Post-herpetic neuralgia is common in the elderly and is treated with TCAs and anticonvulsants (gabapentin, pregabalin).

**Q:** What is the management of herpes zoster (shingles)?

**A:** Compress with normal saline, Burow's or betadine solution. Oral antivirals (famciclovir, valacyclovir, or acyclovir) for 7 days — must start within 72 hours of rash onset. Analgesia: NSAIDs/acetaminophen for mild–moderate pain; opioids if severe. Post-herpetic neuralgia: TCAs, gabapentin or pregabalin.

Antivirals reduce duration and severity of acute infection and may reduce the incidence of post-herpetic neuralgia. Ramsay Hunt syndrome occurs when VZV reactivates in the geniculate ganglion (CN VII).

**Q:** How is molluscum contagiosum spread and how is it managed?

**A:** Spread via direct contact, auto-inoculation, or sexual contact. Self-limited (1–2 years to resolve). Management options: topical cantharidin, cryotherapy, curettage, topical retinoids, imiquimod (Aldara®) — an immunomodulator that induces pro-inflammatory cytokines. Giant/severe molluscum can be seen in HIV.

Molluscum contagiosum is caused by a poxvirus and is common in children and sexually active young adults. No treatment is required for immunocompetent patients as it is self-limiting.

**Q:** What are the first-line, second-line, and third-line treatments for warts (HPV)?

**A:** First-line: salicylic acid preparations, cryotherapy. Second-line: topical imiquimod, topical 5-fluorouracil, topical tretinoin, podophyllotoxin. Third-line: curettage, cautery, surgery (non-plantar), CO2 laser, oral cimetidine (children), intralesional bleomycin (plantar warts), trichloroacetic acid, diphencyprone.

HPV infects keratinocytes and causes benign epithelial growths. Plantar warts (verruca plantaris) interrupt epidermal ridges, helping distinguish them from callus; paring reveals red-brown specks (thrombosed capillaries).

**Q:** What organism causes candidal intertrigo and what are its predisposing factors?

**A:** Candida albicans (70–80% of infections). Predisposing factors: obesity, diabetes mellitus, systemic antibiotics, immunosuppression, and malignancy. Lesions are macerated/eroded erythematous patches in intertriginous areas (under breast, groin, interdigitally) with peripheral 'satellite' pustules.

Candidal intertrigo starts as non-infectious maceration from heat, moisture, and friction. Management includes keeping the area dry and applying topical antifungals (terbinafine, ciclopirox olamine, ketoconazole/clotrimazole cream BID).

**Q:** What is the causative organism of pityriasis (tinea) versicolor and why does the skin appear lighter in summer?

**A:** Caused by Malassezia species (formerly Pityrosporum). The organism produces azelaic acid which inhibits melanin synthesis — affected skin is lighter than surrounding skin in summer (doesn't tan) and darker in winter. Appears as asymptomatic brown/white scaling macules on upper chest and back.

Malassezia has an affinity for sebaceous glands and requires fatty acids to survive. It is associated with folliculitis and seborrheic dermatitis. Predisposing factors include summer/tropical climates, excessive sweating, Cushing's syndrome, and prolonged corticosteroid use.

**Q:** How is pityriasis versicolor diagnosed and managed?

**A:** Diagnosis: clinical; KOH prep of scales shows hyphae and spores (spaghetti-and-meatballs appearance). Management: ketoconazole 2% shampoo or cream daily for 3 days; selenium sulfide 2.5% lotion for 10 min for 7 days; ciclopirox olamine BID; systemic fluconazole or itraconazole for 7 days if extensive.

Pityriasis versicolor is caused by Malassezia species and is a superficial fungal infection. Recurrence is common, especially in humid conditions.

**Q:** What types of flat warts (verruca planae) are associated with which HPV types and what are their characteristic features?

**A:** Verruca planae are caused by HPV types 3 and 10. They present as multiple discrete, skin-coloured, flat-topped papules in grouped or linear configuration on the face, dorsal hands, shins, and knees. Common in children.

Flat warts are distinguished from syringoma, seborrheic keratosis, molluscum contagiosum, and lichen planus. They may spread by Koebner phenomenon (isomorphic response to trauma). Treatment follows the general wart management algorithm.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action and primary indication of calcipotriol (Dovonex®) topical therapy?

**A:** Calcipotriol is a vitamin D3 analogue applied BID for psoriasis. It is inactivated by light (do not apply before phototherapy). Maximum weekly doses vary by age: ≤5 yr = 25 g/wk, 6–10 yr = 50 g/wk, 11–14 yr = 75 g/wk, >14 yr = 100 g/wk.

Calcipotriol inhibits keratinocyte proliferation and promotes differentiation, making it effective for plaque psoriasis. It should be washed off hands after application and avoided on the face, mucous membranes, and eyes.

**Q:** How is permethrin used to treat scabies and pediculosis, and what are its contraindications?

**A:** Permethrin 5% cream (Kwellada-P) is applied overnight to all skin areas from the neck down and repeated 1 week later for scabies. Permethrin 1% (Nix) is used for pediculosis. Contraindicated in children <2 years and in hypersensitivity to permethrin or chrysanthemums.

Permethrin is a synthetic pyrethroid with low toxicity and excellent efficacy. A second application 7 days later is recommended for scabies to kill newly hatched mites that were not killed by the first treatment.

**Q:** What are the advantages of pimecrolimus (Elidel®) and tacrolimus (Protopic®) over topical corticosteroids in atopic dermatitis?

**A:** Both are calcineurin inhibitors that lack the adverse effects of topical steroids (skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia). They can be used on all skin surfaces including face, neck, and intertriginous areas. Pimecrolimus 1% cream is for mild–moderate AD (>3 months old); tacrolimus 0.03% (2–15 yr) or 0.1% (≥15 yr) ointment BID.

Topical calcineurin inhibitors inhibit T-cell activation without causing cutaneous atrophy. They are expensive but useful as steroid-sparing agents for sensitive areas where long-term steroid use is problematic.

**Q:** What are the major monitoring requirements and contraindications for isotretinoin (Accutane®) use in severe nodular acne?

**A:** Monitoring: fasting lipids and LFTs at baseline then q1–2 weeks; pregnancy test x2 at baseline. Contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic) — requires 2 forms of reliable contraception in sexually active females. Side effects include dry mucous membranes, decreased night vision, myalgia, and potential depression. Concurrent tetracyclines risk pseudotumour cerebri.

Isotretinoin 0.5–1 mg/kg/day for 20–24 weeks targets severe nodular/inflammatory acne; the total cumulative dose is 120 mg/kg. It may transiently worsen acne early in treatment. Vitamin A supplements must be discontinued.

**Q:** What are the monitoring requirements and major contraindications for methotrexate use in psoriasis?

**A:** Monitoring: pregnancy test at baseline; CBC q6 months (more frequent if dose change or high risk); BUN/Cr and LFTs at baseline then q4–8 weeks; serum albumin at baseline; liver biopsy at baseline if psoriasis with alcoholism, persistently abnormal LFTs, or chronic HBV/HCV. Contraindications: pregnancy, lactation, alcohol abuse, liver dysfunction, immunodeficiency, blood dyscrasias. Administer with folic acid 1 mg/day.

Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and is restricted to severe, recalcitrant, or disabling psoriasis not adequately responsive to other therapies. It may be combined with cyclosporine at lower doses of each to minimize toxicity.

**Q:** What are the monitoring requirements and key contraindications for cyclosporine (Neoral®) in dermatology?

**A:** Monitoring: BUN/Cr x2 at baseline then q2 weeks x3 months then monthly; BP x2, CBC, K+, Mg, lipid panel, uric acid at baseline then q2 weeks x3 months then monthly; LFTs. Contraindications: abnormal renal function, uncontrolled HTN, malignancy (except NMSC), uncontrolled infection, immunodeficiency (excluding autoimmune disease), hypersensitivity. Maximum treatment duration: 2 years.

Cyclosporine 2.5–4 mg/kg/day PO divided BID is used for psoriasis, lichen planus, erythema multiforme, recalcitrant urticaria, and recalcitrant atopic dermatitis. Concomitant magnesium supplementation may be renoprotective.

**Q:** What are the indications and key monitoring parameters for acitretin (Soriatane®)?

**A:** Acitretin is used for severe psoriasis and disorders of hyperkeratinization (ichthyosis, Darier's disease). Monitoring: CBC and LFTs at baseline then q1–2 weeks until stable; fasting lipids at baseline then q1–2 weeks until stable; urine/serum pregnancy test x2 at baseline, monthly during treatment, then q3 months for >3 years after discontinuation.

Acitretin is a systemic retinoid that is teratogenic; women of childbearing potential require strict contraceptive requirements. It can be combined with PUVA phototherapy (re-PUVA). Maximum dose is 75 mg/day.

**Q:** When should spironolactone be considered for acne, and what are its key side effects and contraindications?

**A:** Spironolactone 50–100 mg/day (alone or with OCPs) is used for hormonal acne (chin/jawline) and acne with endocrine abnormality. Contraindicated in pregnancy. Side effects: menstrual irregularities at higher doses (if not on OCPs), breast tenderness, mild diuresis, and risk of hyperkalemia (counsel to reduce potassium-rich foods like bananas).

Spironolactone is an anti-androgen that reduces sebum production. It is particularly useful for acne in the chin/jawline distribution reflecting hormonal influence. Regular monitoring of potassium is warranted given hyperkalemia risk.

**Q:** What are the indications and key G6PD-related consideration for dapsone in dermatology?

**A:** Dapsone 50–150 mg/day (tapered to 25–50 mg) is used for dermatitis herpetiformis and neutrophilic dermatoses. G6PD must be checked before starting treatment (hemolysis risk). Monitor CBC weekly x4, monthly x6, then q6 months; LFTs at baseline then periodically. Vitamin C and E supplementation can help prevent hemolysis.

Dapsone acts as an anti-inflammatory agent effective in dermatitis herpetiformis (associated with celiac disease). Dramatic response within hours is characteristic. It is a substrate of CYP2C8/9, 2C19, 2E1, and 3A4 — with multiple potential drug interactions.

**Q:** Which combined oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) are indicated for acne, and what are their key contraindications?

**A:** OCPs that have undergone RCTs for acne include TriCyclen®, Diane-35®, and Alesse. They are indicated for hormonal acne (chin/jawline) and acne associated with PCOS or endocrine abnormalities. Contraindications: smoking, hypertension, migraines with aura, and pregnancy.

All combined OCPs reduce androgen-driven sebum production and are helpful in acne, but those listed have RCT evidence. Diane-35 contains cyproterone acetate, a potent anti-androgen. Routine gynecological health maintenance should be up to date before initiating.

**Q:** What is perniosis (chilblains), and how is it clinically distinguished from Raynaud's phenomenon?

**A:** Perniosis (chilblains/pernio) is a localized inflammatory response to cold, damp, non-freezing conditions causing erythematous to blue-violet macules, nodules, or papules on distal toes/fingers, heels, ears, and nose. Symptoms of burning, itching, or pain occur within hours after cold exposure, lasting 1–3 weeks. Unlike Raynaud's, it involves persistent inflammatory lesions rather than episodic vasospasm.

Perniosis may be associated with cryoglobulins or cold agglutinins. Differential diagnosis includes chilblain lupus erythematosus and lupus pernio. Treatment includes nifedipine, nicotinamide, and avoidance of cold/damp conditions.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for perniosis (chilblains), and who is most commonly affected?

**A:** Risk factors include smoking, low BMI, poor nutrition, and occupational cold/damp exposure (outdoor workers). Most commonly affects women, the elderly, and children. Common in the UK and northwestern Europe, and in homes lacking central heating.

Perniosis is not due to frostbite (freezing injury); it occurs in above-freezing damp cold. Treatment beyond avoidance includes nifedipine (vasodilator), nicotinamide, phenoxybenzamine, sympathectomy, and erythemogenic UVB phototherapy.

**Q:** What was the key finding of the BE VIVID trial comparing bimekizumab to ustekinumab in moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis?

**A:** BE VIVID (Lancet 2021) showed bimekizumab (IL-17A/F inhibitor) achieved PASI90 in 85% of patients vs. 50% with ustekinumab and 5% with placebo at 16 weeks. IGA response rates were 84% vs. 53% vs. 5%. Higher rates of oral candidiasis and MACE in pre-existing CV risk patients were noted with bimekizumab.

Bimekizumab simultaneously blocks both IL-17A and IL-17F, providing superior efficacy over ustekinumab (IL-12/23 inhibitor) in psoriasis. The increased oral candidiasis risk reflects the role of IL-17 in mucosal immunity against Candida.

**Q:** What did the ADVANCE trial show about apremilast for mild-to-moderate plaque psoriasis?

**A:** ADVANCE (JAAD 2022) found that apremilast 30 mg BID (a phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitor) achieved a static Physician Global Assessment score of 0–1 in 21.6% vs. 4.1% of placebo patients at 16 weeks in mild-to-moderate psoriasis.

Apremilast is an oral small-molecule PDE4 inhibitor that reduces inflammatory cytokine production. It offers an alternative to biologics for patients who prefer oral therapy or have contraindications to immunosuppressants or injectables.

**Q:** What was demonstrated by the ECZTRA trial regarding tralokinumab in moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis?

**A:** ECZTRA (Br J Dermatol 2021) found tralokinumab 300 mg SC q2 weeks (IL-13 monoclonal antibody) plus topical corticosteroids achieved EASI-75 in 56% vs. 35.7% of placebo patients at 16 weeks.

Tralokinumab specifically targets IL-13, a key cytokine in type 2 inflammation driving atopic dermatitis. It is well-tolerated and provides an additional biologic option for moderate-to-severe AD alongside dupilumab.

**Q:** What did the Measure Up 1 and 2 trials demonstrate about upadacitinib in moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis?

**A:** Measure Up 1 and 2 (Lancet 2021) showed upadacitinib (selective JAK-1 inhibitor) 15 mg achieved EASI-75 in 70% and 30 mg in 80% at 16 weeks vs. 16% for placebo. vIGA-AD score 0–1 was achieved in 48% (15 mg) and 62% (30 mg) vs. 8% (placebo).

Upadacitinib is an oral JAK-1 inhibitor that reduces signalling through cytokine receptors (IL-4, IL-13, IL-31) key to AD pathogenesis. JAK inhibitors offer rapid onset but require monitoring for infections, thromboembolism, and lipid changes.

**Q:** What did the Advocate 1 and 2 trials show about lebrikizumab for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis?

**A:** Advocate 1 and 2 (NEJM 2023) showed lebrikizumab (IL-13 monoclonal antibody) SC q2 weeks achieved IGA score 0–1 in 43.1% vs. 12.7% placebo and EASI-75 in 58.8% vs. 16.2% placebo at 16 weeks in adolescents and adults.

Lebrikizumab selectively binds IL-13 and prevents its signalling, reducing type 2 inflammation in AD. It joins tralokinumab as an IL-13-specific biologic option for AD, with dupilumab (IL-4/IL-13) remaining the established standard.

**Q:** What did the TRuE-V1 and V2 trials demonstrate about ruxolitinib cream for vitiligo?

**A:** TRuE-V1 and V2 (NEJM 2022) found ruxolitinib 1.5% cream BID for 52 weeks achieved F-VASI75 (75% reduction in facial Vitiligo Area Scoring Index) in ~30% of patients vs. ~8–11% vehicle control in patients ≥12 years with non-segmental vitiligo. Application-site acne, nasopharyngitis, and pruritus were common adverse events.

Ruxolitinib cream (JAK1/2 inhibitor) blocks interferon-gamma-driven JAK-STAT signalling that destroys melanocytes in vitiligo. It is the first FDA-approved topical therapy specifically indicated for vitiligo repigmentation.

**Q:** What did the BRAVE-AA1 and AA2 trials demonstrate about baricitinib for severe alopecia areata?

**A:** BRAVE-AA1 and AA2 (NEJM 2022) showed baricitinib (JAK1/2 inhibitor) 4 mg daily achieved SALT scores ≤20 (≤20% scalp hair loss) in ~36–39% vs. ~3–6% placebo in adults with severe alopecia areata at 36 weeks. The 2 mg dose also outperformed placebo (~19–23% vs. 3–6%).

Baricitinib is an oral JAK inhibitor approved for severe alopecia areata. The SALT score ranges 0–100, with 0 representing no scalp hair loss. JAK inhibitors address the immune attack on hair follicles by blocking cytokine signalling.

**Q:** What is the Allegroll trial's finding regarding ritlecitinib for alopecia areata?

**A:** Allegroll (Lancet 2023) found ritlecitinib (kinase inhibitor) 200 mg loading + 50 mg maintenance daily achieved SALT score ≤20 (≤20% scalp hair loss) in 31% vs. 2% placebo at 24 weeks in patients ≥12 years with ≥50% scalp hair loss from alopecia areata.

Ritlecitinib is a JAK3/TEC kinase inhibitor approved for alopecia areata. It inhibits JAK-dependent cytokine signalling that drives the autoimmune attack on hair follicles, and unlike baricitinib, it is approved for adolescents ≥12 years.

**Q:** What is roflumilast (Zoryve®) used for in dermatology, and what are its advantages?

**A:** Roflumilast (Zoryve®) is a topical PDE4 inhibitor applied once daily as a cream for psoriasis and atopic dermatitis, or as a foam for seborrheic dermatitis. It lacks the adverse effects of topical steroids (skin atrophy, striae). Side effects include pain and burning at application site.

Roflumilast's topical formulation as both cream and foam allows targeted use across different inflammatory dermatoses. Unlike systemic roflumilast (used for COPD), the topical form minimizes systemic adverse effects while providing anti-inflammatory benefit.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology and management of androgenetic alopecia in males vs. females?

**A:** DHT acts on genetically susceptible hair follicles. Males: fronto-temporal to vertex pattern. Treatment: topical minoxidil, finasteride (5α-reductase inhibitor 1 mg/day). Females: widening of central part ('Christmas tree'). Treatment: topical minoxidil, spironolactone, cyproterone acetate.

Androgenetic alopecia is the most common form of hair loss. Finasteride is contraindicated in women of childbearing potential due to teratogenicity risk. Spironolactone provides anti-androgenic effect in females.

**Q:** What are the precipitants of telogen effluvium (mnemonic SEND)?

**A:** S - Stress (biological and psychological) and Scalp disease (surgery); E - Endocrine (hypothyroidism); N - Nutritional (iron and protein deficiency); D - Drugs (acitretin, heparin, lithium, IFN, β-blockers, valproic acid, SSRIs).

Telogen effluvium involves a uniform decrease in hair density 2-4 months after the precipitant, as hairs are forced from anagen into telogen. Regrowth begins in months but meaningful regrowth takes 6-12 months.

**Q:** What is the timing and mechanism of anagen effluvium in chemotherapy patients?

**A:** Hair loss occurs 7-14 days after single pulse chemotherapy, most apparent after 1-2 months. Mechanism: cytotoxic agents impair mitotic activity of hair follicles during the growth (anagen) phase. Effect is reversible after agent is stopped.

Anagen effluvium is dose-dependent and differs from telogen effluvium in timing and mechanism. Other causes include bismuth, levodopa, colchicine, and heavy metal exposure (thallium, arsenic).

**Q:** What are the characteristic features of alopecia areata, and what are its associations?

**A:** Autoimmune disorder with patches of complete hair loss; 'exclamation mark' hairs (tapered shafts); may have nail pitting/stippling. Associated with pernicious anemia, vitiligo, thyroid disease, Addison's disease.

Alopecia areata results from T-cell mediated attack on hair follicles. Alopecia totalis (all scalp hair) and universalis (all body hair) are severe subtypes. JAK inhibitors are newer treatments for refractory disease.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for isolated patches of alopecia areata?

**A:** Topical corticosteroids (TCS) and intralesional steroids (triamcinolone acetonide). Topical immunotherapy (diphencyprone, anthralin) and JAK inhibitors are options for refractory disease.

Localized alopecia areata has an excellent prognosis and may spontaneously regrow within months. Systemic immunosuppressants are reserved for extensive or refractory disease.

**Q:** What distinguishes scarring (cicatricial) alopecia from non-scarring alopecia?

**A:** Scarring alopecia involves irreversible loss of hair follicles with fibrosis; stem cells are destroyed, so regrowth is not possible. Non-scarring alopecia preserves stem cells, allowing spontaneous or treatment-induced regrowth.

Biopsy from the active border is required to diagnose scarring alopecias. Causes include radiation, burns, infections (fungal, TB, HSV), DLE, lichen planopilaris, and primary inflammatory conditions.

**Q:** What is central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA) and who is most affected?

**A:** CCCA is a scarring alopecia starting at the central scalp, seen in 2-7% of Black women; one of the most commonly diagnosed scarring alopecias, possibly associated with certain hair care practices.

CCCA is an important cause of permanent hair loss in women of African descent. It is a primary lymphocytic scarring alopecia. Early diagnosis and treatment with topical/intralesional steroids or anti-inflammatories is important.

**Q:** What is the follicular occlusion tetrad?

**A:** Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), acne conglobata, cicatricial alopecia (dissecting cellulitis of scalp), and pilonidal disease.

These four conditions share follicular occlusion as a unifying pathophysiologic mechanism. Recognizing this association is important as patients may have concurrent manifestations.

**Q:** What nail change is associated with psoriasis vs. alopecia areata in nail pitting?

**A:** Psoriasis: random pitting pattern. Alopecia areata: geometric/grid-shaped pitting pattern.

Nail pitting results from defective keratinization of the proximal nail matrix. The pattern of pitting helps differentiate psoriasis from alopecia areata and can be a clue to systemic diagnosis.

**Q:** What does koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails) suggest as an underlying systemic diagnosis?

**A:** Iron deficiency anemia, malnutrition, or diabetes mellitus.

Koilonychia is characterized by concave nail plates. In adults, iron deficiency is the most common cause. It represents a clue to systemic disease on physical examination.

**Q:** What systemic conditions are associated with Terry's nails?

**A:** Liver cirrhosis. Terry's nails: white proximal nail, darker distal nail with ground glass appearance, no lunula.

Terry's nails are a sign of severe systemic disease, most classically liver cirrhosis. They differ from leukonychia (diffuse white nails) seen in hypoalbuminemia and chronic renal failure.

**Q:** What is hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), and what are its characteristic skin findings?

**A:** Chronic scarring inflammatory skin condition from follicular occlusion in intertriginous zones. Findings: inflammatory nodules, sinus tracts, double tombstone comedones, hypertrophic scarring. Most common site: axillae.

HS affects 1-4% of the population, predominantly women, with onset in the 2nd-3rd decade. It is associated with smoking and excess weight. Hurley staging guides treatment selection.

**Q:** What biologic therapies are approved for refractory moderate-to-severe hidradenitis suppurativa?

**A:** Anti-TNF agents: adalimumab and infliximab. Adalimumab is Health Canada–approved for HS.

Biologics targeting TNF-α are used for HS that fails antibiotics (oral tetracyclines, clindamycin+rifampin combination) and other systemic therapies. CO₂ laser for deroofing is also used in moderate-severe disease.

**Q:** What is primary hyperhidrosis and how does it differ from secondary hyperhidrosis?

**A:** Primary: focal excessive sweating ≥6 months without apparent cause; bilateral, symmetrical; only during waking hours (diurnal); affects palms, soles, axillae. Secondary: caused by medications, menopause, autonomic dysfunction, spinal cord injury.

Primary hyperhidrosis affects 1-5% of the population with a strong family history. Pathophysiology involves an exaggerated eccrine sweat gland response to emotional stimuli. Diagnosis is clinical (starch-iodine test confirms location).

**Q:** What are the treatment options for primary hyperhidrosis, from conservative to surgical?

**A:** Antiperspirants → iontophoresis → botulinum toxin → topical glycopyrronium bromide → oral glycopyrrolate/oxybutynin → microwave thermolysis → suction curettage → endoscopic thoracic sympathectomy (ETS).

Botulinum toxin injections are highly effective for axillary hyperhidrosis, requiring repeat treatments every 6-12 months. ETS is reserved for palmar hyperhidrosis not responding to other treatments but carries risk of compensatory hyperhidrosis.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of recurrent aphthous stomatitis (canker sores)?

**A:** Painful, shallow, <5 mm, round-to-oval ulcers covered by creamy-white pseudomembrane with erythematous halo. Sites: labial/buccal mucosa, floor of mouth, ventral tongue, soft palate. More common in women, peak age 20-30.

Aphthous stomatitis is immunologically mediated epithelial damage. Triggers include trauma, stress, infections, HIV, and hormonal changes. Must be distinguished from HSV (vesicles, more keratinized surfaces) and Behçet disease.

**Q:** What conditions should be considered in the differential diagnosis of recurrent aphthous stomatitis?

**A:** Behçet syndrome, SLE, gluten-sensitive enteropathy (celiac disease), HSV infection.

Behçet syndrome is distinguished by painful oral AND genital ulcers plus other systemic features. Celiac disease may present with recurrent oral ulcers; testing for anti-tTG antibodies should be considered in recurrent/refractory cases.

**Q:** What is erythema nodosum and what are its most common causes?

**A:** Acute panniculitis (subcutaneous fat inflammation) with round, red, tender poorly demarcated nodules on extensor lower legs (shins). 40% idiopathic. Common causes: streptococcal infection (GAS), sarcoidosis, TB, IBD (Crohn's>UC), OCPs, drugs (sulfonamides), histoplasmosis, Yersinia.

Mnemonic 'NO DNOSUM': New drugs, Other infections (GAS), Sarcoidosis, UCer (IBD), Many infections. Investigations include CXR, throat culture, ASO titre, PPD. Lesions resolve spontaneously in 6 weeks.

**Q:** What are the epidemiological features of erythema nodosum?

**A:** Age 15-30 years old, F:M ratio 3:1. Lesions last for days and spontaneously resolve within 6 weeks.

The female predominance, especially in reproductive years, suggests hormonal factors including OCP use and pregnancy as precipitants. Treatment is symptomatic with NSAIDs, rest, and compressive bandages.

**Q:** What are the systemic causes of generalized pruritus without primary skin lesions? (mnemonic SCRATCHED)

**A:** S-Systemic (hepatic/cholestatic), C-Cancer (lymphoma, leukemia), R-Renal (CKD/uremia), A-Autoimmune/Anemia, T-Thyroid, C-Carcinoid, H-Hematologic, E-Endocrine, D-Drugs/Dry skin.

Generalized pruritus without primary skin lesions warrants investigation for systemic disease. Workup includes CBC, LFTs, creatinine, TSH, CXR. Hodgkin's lymphoma is a classic cause of severe generalized pruritus.

**Q:** What is the classification of urticaria based on duration?

**A:** Acute urticaria: attacks <6 weeks; most common causes are drugs (ASA, NSAIDs, antibiotics), foods, infections. Chronic urticaria: attacks >6 weeks; 90% are idiopathic; individual lesions still last <24h.

In pediatric patients, >80% of urticaria is infection-related. Chronic idiopathic urticaria is treated with non-sedating antihistamines (first-line), then updosing and omalizumab for refractory cases.

**Q:** What distinguishes urticarial vasculitis from ordinary urticaria?

**A:** Individual lesions last >24 hours, are often painful (less pruritic), and leave bruise-type discolouration. Biopsy is indicated. Associated with infections (hepatitis B/C), autoimmune diseases (SLE), and drug hypersensitivity (cimetidine, diltiazem).

Urticarial vasculitis requires biopsy showing leukocytoclastic vasculitis. Standard urticaria lesions last <24 hours and resolve without bruising. Workup should include complement levels; hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis is strongly associated with SLE.

**Q:** What is Darier's sign and in what condition is it seen?

**A:** Darier's sign: rubbing a lesion produces a wheal surrounded by intense erythema within minutes due to mast cell degranulation. Seen in mastocytosis (urticaria pigmentosa).

Urticaria pigmentosa/mastocytosis presents as reddish-brown macules and plaques from excessive mast cell infiltration. Systemic mastocytosis can cause anaphylaxis-like episodes from mass mast cell degranulation.

**Q:** What are the Fitzpatrick skin phototypes and their significance?

**A:** Type I: white skin, always burns, never tans. Types II-III: burns then tans. Types IV-V: minimal burning, tans easily. Type VI: dark brown/black, never burns, dark tan. Higher types have more UV protection but are at risk for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

Fitzpatrick classification is clinically relevant for UV risk assessment, treatment selection (e.g., laser settings), and skin cancer screening counseling. It also guides sunscreen recommendations and photoaging risk.

**Q:** What wavelength of UV radiation is primarily responsible for sunburn vs. photoaging?

**A:** UVB (290-320 nm): primarily responsible for sunburn and vitamin D synthesis; absorbed by outer dermis; blocked by glass. UVA (320-400 nm): responsible for photoaging, photosensitivity reactions, and premature skin aging; penetrates glass and clouds.

Broad-spectrum sunscreens protect against both UVA and UVB. UVC (200-290 nm) is filtered by the ozone layer. Both UVA and UVB contribute to photocarcinogenesis.

**Q:** What is the SPF formula and when should sunscreen be reapplied?

**A:** SPF = burn time with cream ÷ burn time without cream. Chemical sunscreens must be applied 15-30 minutes before exposure and reapplied every 2 hours (more often when sweating or swimming).

Physical sunscreens (titanium dioxide, zinc oxide) reflect/scatter UV light, are effective against the full spectrum, and have less risk of sensitization. Chemical sunscreens absorb UV radiation and require adequate lead time for efficacy.

**Q:** What is the relative potency ranking of topical corticosteroids (least to most potent)?

**A:** Weak (×1): hydrocortisone. Moderate (×3): mometasone, desonide. Potent (×6): betamethasone valerate, amcinonide. Very potent (×9): fluocinonide, betamethasone dipropionate. Extremely potent (×25): clobetasol propionate (most potent).

Topical steroid potency selection depends on body site, skin condition, and patient age. Weak steroids are used for face, intertriginous areas, and children. The scrotum has 42× the percutaneous absorption of the forearm.

**Q:** A 45-year-old Black woman presents with progressive hair loss starting at the crown of the scalp with fibrosis on biopsy. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA) — a primary lymphocytic scarring alopecia starting at the central scalp, most common in Black women (2-7% prevalence).

CCCA is one of the most common scarring alopecias in women of African descent. It is irreversible due to fibrotic destruction of hair follicles. Early treatment with topical/intralesional steroids or anti-inflammatory antibiotics may slow progression.

**Q:** What is the treatment for chronic spontaneous urticaria that is refractory to antihistamines?

**A:** Updosing of non-sedating H1 antihistamines (up to 4× standard dose), then omalizumab (anti-IgE monoclonal antibody) for refractory cases.

Omalizumab is approved in Canada for chronic spontaneous urticaria refractory to antihistamines. It works by reducing free IgE levels, thereby decreasing mast cell activation. Cyclosporine is an alternative for very refractory cases.

**Q:** What nail changes are associated with psoriasis?

**A:** Pitting (random pattern), onycholysis (separation of nail from bed), oil spots (brown-yellow discolouration), subungual hyperkeratosis, splinter hemorrhages.

Nail changes in psoriasis occur in 50-80% of patients and may precede or accompany psoriatic arthritis. Distinguishing from onychomycosis (fungal) is important — nail clipping for KOH and fungal culture may be needed.

**Q:** What is Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS) and who does it affect?

**A:** Caused by exotoxin (exfoliatin) from coagulase-positive S. aureus. Mostly affects children. Presents with: fever, irritability, malaise, skin tenderness → sudden diffuse erythema (red, warm, tender) → flaccid bullae → desquamation in large sheets. Treatment: IV anti-staphylococcal antibiotics.

SSSS exotoxin targets desmoglein-1 in the superficial epidermis, causing intraepidermal cleavage. Unlike TEN (which involves full-thickness epidermal necrosis), SSSS has a good prognosis with appropriate antibiotics. Nikolsky's sign (skin peels with gentle pressure) is positive in both SSSS and TEN.

**Q:** What is DRESS syndrome and when does it present?

**A:** Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms. Presents 1-6 weeks after first exposure to causative drug; persists weeks after withdrawal. Features: morbilliform rash (face, trunk, arms), fever, malaise, cervical lymphadenopathy, internal organ involvement (hepatitis, nephritis, pneumonitis, arthralgia, thyroid abnormalities, hematologic abnormalities).

DRESS has a mortality of 10%. Common culprit drugs include aromatic anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin), allopurinol, sulfonamides, minocycline, and dapsone. Eosinophilia and atypical lymphocytosis on CBC are characteristic. Treatment: stop drug, systemic corticosteroids, supportive care.

**Q:** What is Erythema Multiforme (EM) and what is the most common cause?

**A:** Most commonly caused by herpes simplex virus. Less common: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, drug-induced. Presents with: viral prodrome 1-14 days before rash → target lesion (central grey bulla/wheal surrounded by concentric rings of erythema and normal skin). Mucous membrane involvement can cause difficulty with oral intake.

EM is a cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction. HSV-associated EM may recur with each HSV outbreak—prophylactic acyclovir can prevent recurrences. EM is distinguished from SJS by less mucous membrane involvement (<2 sites), less skin detachment, and targetoid (bullseye) lesions. Treatment: treat underlying cause, supportive care.

**Q:** What is Pyoderma Gangrenosum and what conditions is it associated with?

**A:** Pyoderma gangrenosum is associated with IBD (Crohn's/UC), rheumatoid arthritis, leukemia, and monoclonal gammopathies. Begins as painless macule/vesicle/pustule/bulla on a red/blue base that sloughs to leave a gangrenous ulcer. Common sites: arms, hands, feet, perineal region.

The pathogenesis involves neutrophil dysfunction and immune dysregulation (pathergy: wounds worsen with trauma). Key diagnostic feature: diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out infection, vasculitis, malignancy. Management: systemic corticosteroids, cyclosporine, or biologics (infliximab for IBD-associated cases). Avoid debridement.

**Q:** What organisms cause mastitis and what are the recommended antibiotics?

**A:** Mastitis is caused by Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes. First-line: cloxacillin 500 mg PO QID x 7 days OR cephalexin 500 mg PO QID x 7 days.

Mastitis most commonly occurs in breastfeeding women. S. aureus is the predominant pathogen. Anti-staphylococcal penicillins (cloxacillin) or first-generation cephalosporins are appropriate. Breastfeeding should continue if possible.

**Q:** What is the first-line antibiotic treatment for uncomplicated cellulitis in adults?

**A:** First-line: cephalexin 500 mg PO QID x 10–14 days. Second-line: cloxacillin 500 mg PO QID x 10–14 days OR clindamycin 300 mg PO x 10–14 days.

Uncomplicated cellulitis is caused by Group A Streptococcus and S. aureus. Cephalexin (a first-generation cephalosporin) provides adequate coverage. Clindamycin is an alternative for penicillin-allergic patients or suspected MRSA.

## Skin Cancer & Melanoma

**Q:** What is the most important prognostic factor for cutaneous melanoma?

**A:** Breslow thickness (depth of invasion in mm)

Breslow thickness is the single most important prognostic factor, determining T stage in AJCC staging. Tumors <0.8 mm have >95% 5-year survival; >4 mm have <50%. Clark level and ulceration are also prognostic.

**Q:** A 72-year-old farmer has a 1.5 cm scaly, indurated lesion on his lower lip with central ulceration. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)

SCC on the lip has higher metastatic potential than SCC at other sites. Risk factors: UV, chronic inflammation, HPV, tobacco. Sentinel node biopsy may be considered for high-risk lesions. Wide local excision is treatment of choice.

**Q:** What is the recommended surgical margin for a primary melanoma with Breslow thickness 1.01–2.0 mm?

**A:** 1 cm margin

AJCC and Canadian guidelines: <1 mm Breslow → 1 cm margin; 1–2 mm → 1 cm (some guidelines 1–2 cm); >2 mm → 2 cm; >4 mm → 2 cm. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is offered for tumors ≥0.8 mm.

**Q:** Which virus is strongly associated with Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC)?

**A:** Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCV)

MCV is present in ~80% of MCC cases and promotes tumorigenesis. MCC is a rare but aggressive neuroendocrine skin cancer occurring in elderly, immunocompromised patients. Treatment: wide excision + sentinel lymph node biopsy + adjuvant radiotherapy.

**Q:** What is actinic keratosis and why is it clinically significant?

**A:** Precancerous UV-induced keratotic lesion with risk of progression to SCC (1–10% per year)

AK (solar keratosis) appears as rough, scaly patches on sun-damaged skin. Treatment: cryotherapy, topical 5-FU, imiquimod, or diclofenac gel. Multiple AKs indicate field cancerization and high future SCC risk.

**Q:** What is the recommended follow-up interval after excision of a stage IB melanoma?

**A:** Clinical exam every 3–6 months for 3 years, then annually

Stage I-II melanoma has risk of local recurrence and systemic metastasis. Skin and lymph node examination is recommended. Routine imaging (CT, PET) is not recommended for stage I unless symptomatic.

**Q:** A patient presents with multiple BCCs, jaw cysts, and calcification of the falx cerebri. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome) — PTCH1 mutation

Gorlin syndrome is an autosomal dominant condition with multiple BCCs starting at a young age, odontogenic keratocysts, bifid ribs, and calcified falx cerebri. Avoid radiotherapy as it triggers additional BCCs. Vismodegib (hedgehog pathway inhibitor) is used for extensive disease.

**Q:** What is the first-line immunotherapy for unresectable or metastatic melanoma?

**A:** Pembrolizumab or nivolumab (anti-PD-1 checkpoint inhibitors)

Anti-PD-1 agents have revolutionized melanoma treatment, achieving durable responses in ~40% of patients. BRAF V600E-mutant melanoma can also receive BRAF+MEK inhibitors (dabrafenib+trametinib). Combination ipilimumab+nivolumab offers higher response rates with more toxicity.

**Q:** Which skin cancer has the highest metastatic potential among common skin cancers?

**A:** Merkel cell carcinoma (higher than SCC; much higher than BCC)

Among common skin cancers: MCC >SCC >BCC for metastatic potential. However, melanoma is the most lethal overall. High-risk SCC features: >2 cm, deep invasion, perineural invasion, immunosuppression, ear/lip location.

**Q:** What is the indication for sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) in melanoma?

**A:** Breslow thickness ≥0.8 mm (or ≥0.75 mm with ulceration or mitoses) — T1b and above

SLNB provides staging information and guides adjuvant therapy decisions. Positive SLNB may warrant adjuvant immunotherapy (pembrolizumab) or targeted therapy. It does not improve survival in node-negative patients but identifies those at higher risk.

**Q:** What is the ABCDE rule for melanoma?

**A:** Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter >6mm, Evolving (changing size/shape/color)

Breslow thickness is the most important prognostic factor. Excisional biopsy preferred. Sentinel lymph node biopsy if >1mm. Immunotherapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab) for advanced melanoma.

**Q:** What are the types of skin cancer and clinical features?

**A:** BCC (80%): pearly papule, telangiectasia, rolled borders. SCC (15%): scaly keratotic plaque on sun-damaged skin. Melanoma (5%): ABCDE features, most lethal

BCC subtypes: nodular, superficial, morpheaform (aggressive). SCC risk: UV, immunosuppression, chronic wounds. AK precursor to SCC. Transplant recipients: 65-250x SCC risk.

**Q:** How does Breslow thickness guide melanoma management?

**A:** In situ: 0.5cm margin. <=1mm: 1cm. 1-2mm: 1-2cm. >2mm: 2cm margin. SLNB for >=0.8mm or ulcerated. Breslow is strongest prognostic factor

Clark level less important. Stage I (thin, node-neg): 95% 5yr survival. Stage IV: immunotherapy transformed prognosis. SLNB positive: adjuvant immunotherapy considered.

**Q:** What are the indications for Mohs surgery?

**A:** BCC/SCC in critical areas (face, ears, hands, genitals), recurrent tumors, ill-defined borders, aggressive subtypes (morpheaform BCC, perineural SCC), immunosuppressed

99% cure BCC primary, 95% recurrent. 100% margin examination in real-time. Tissue-sparing for cosmetic sites. Standard excision with margins appropriate for trunk/extremity low-risk tumors.

**Q:** How are actinic keratoses managed?

**A:** Individual: cryotherapy. Field: 5-FU topical 4wk, imiquimod 4-16wk, PDT. Sun protection to prevent recurrence. SCC transformation <1%/yr per lesion but cumulative risk significant

Field cancerization: entire sun-damaged area at risk. SCC arising in AK: hard, thickened, non-healing - biopsy. Multiple AKs warrant field therapy. Diclofenac gel is gentler alternative.

**Q:** What melanoma follow-up is recommended after treatment?

**A:** Stage IA-IIA: exam q6-12mo x5yr then annually. IIB+: q3-6mo x3yr, then q6-12mo to 5yr, then annually. Imaging for IIB+. Lifelong skin surveillance. Self-skin exam education

80% recurrence within 3yr. Adjuvant pembrolizumab for IIB-IIIC. Family: 2-3x risk increase, consider genetics if multiple melanomas or CDKN2A pattern.

**Q:** What is dermoscopy and how does it aid diagnosis?

**A:** 10x magnification with polarized light. BCC: arborizing vessels, blue-gray nests. Melanoma: irregular network, blue-white veil, irregular dots. Improves melanoma sensitivity 70%->90%+

Two-step: 1) melanocytic? 2) benign or suspicious? Reduces unnecessary biopsies 42%. Requires training. Structured approaches: pattern analysis, ABCD dermoscopy score.

**Q:** What systemic treatments exist for advanced melanoma?

**A:** Immunotherapy: anti-PD1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) +/- anti-CTLA4 (ipilimumab). Targeted: BRAFi (dabrafenib) + MEKi (trametinib) for BRAF V600E. Combination immunotherapy ~60% response

5yr survival metastatic now ~50% with immunotherapy (was <10%). BRAF mutation in ~50%. Immune-related AE: colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies - prompt recognition essential.

**Q:** How is BCC managed by risk level?

**A:** Low-risk nodular/superficial: excision (4mm margin) or C&E. High-risk (morpheaform, recurrent, facial): Mohs. Superficial: imiquimod/5-FU/PDT options. Advanced: vismodegib (hedgehog inhibitor)

BCC rarely metastasizes (<0.5%) but locally destructive. Gorlin syndrome: multiple BCCs, PTCH1 mutation. Vismodegib for unresectable. 5yr recurrence: standard 5-8%, Mohs 1%.

**Q:** What melanoma screening/prevention is recommended for transplant recipients?

**A:** Annual dermatology from 1yr post-transplant. Monthly self-exam. SCC 65-250x, BCC 10x, melanoma 2-5x risk. Aggressive sun protection. Consider acitretin for SCC chemoprevention

Skin cancer most common malignancy in transplant. SCC>BCC (reverse of general population). HPV contributes. Consider mTOR inhibitor (sirolimus) switch to reduce skin cancer risk.

**Q:** What is the approach to a suspected SCC?

**A:** Biopsy any non-healing, keratotic nodule/plaque on sun-damaged skin. Staging for high-risk: >2cm, >2mm depth, perineural invasion, immunosuppressed. Excision with 4-6mm margins or Mohs for high-risk

SCC metastatic potential 2-5% (higher in immunosuppressed, lip, ear). High-risk SCC: consider adjuvant radiation. Cemiplimab (anti-PD1) for advanced/unresectable SCC. Differentiate from keratoacanthoma (rapid growth, keratin plug).

**Q:** Describe the clinical features that distinguish basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma.

**A:** Basal cell carcinoma (BCC): pearly/translucent papule or nodule with telangiectasias, rolled borders, central ulceration (rodent ulcer); most common skin cancer, locally invasive, rarely metastasizes; sun-exposed areas (nose most common). Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC): keratinized, scaly, firm papule/nodule, may ulcerate; can arise from actinic keratosis; has metastatic potential (2–5%). Melanoma: asymmetric, irregular borders, color variation, diameter >6mm, evolving (ABCDE criteria); highest mortality of skin cancers; types include superficial spreading (most common), nodular (worst prognosis), lentigo maligna, acral lentiginous.

Canada has among the highest rates of skin cancer globally. The Canadian Dermatology Association's sun awareness program promotes prevention. BCC and SCC are treated primarily with surgical excision (Mohs micrographic surgery for high-risk facial BCC). Melanoma staging uses the Breslow depth (most important prognostic factor) and sentinel lymph node biopsy for tumors >1 mm thick.

**Q:** What is the Breslow depth, and how does it guide melanoma management and prognosis?

**A:** Breslow depth is the thickness of the melanoma measured from the granular layer of the epidermis to the deepest point of tumor invasion, in millimeters. It is the single most important prognostic factor. <1 mm: excellent prognosis (>95% 5-year survival), wide local excision with 1 cm margins. 1–2 mm: wide local excision with 1–2 cm margins + sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB). >2 mm: wide local excision with 2 cm margins + SLNB. Positive SLNB may warrant completion lymph node dissection and adjuvant therapy (immunotherapy: nivolumab, pembrolizumab).

Canadian melanoma guidelines align with NCCN and recommend SLNB for melanomas >0.8 mm or with ulceration. Adjuvant checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy (anti-PD-1) has significantly improved survival in stage III melanoma and is funded by most Canadian provincial drug programs. Regular skin surveillance is recommended for patients with a history of melanoma.

**Q:** An elderly patient on the lower leg has a non-healing, well-demarcated, erythematous scaly plaque that has been present for months. Biopsy shows full-thickness epidermal dysplasia. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Squamous cell carcinoma in situ (Bowen disease). Full-thickness epidermal dysplasia without dermal invasion. Treatment options: surgical excision, topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, photodynamic therapy, or cryotherapy. If left untreated, 3–5% progress to invasive SCC.

Bowen disease is distinguished from actinic keratosis (partial-thickness dysplasia) by having full-thickness atypia. Risk factors include chronic sun exposure, arsenic exposure, and HPV. The Canadian Dermatology Association recommends excisional biopsy for lesions suspicious for invasive SCC, especially if nodularity or induration develops within a Bowen disease lesion.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for actinic keratosis and what is the risk of progression to squamous cell carcinoma?

**A:** Risk factors: chronic UV exposure, fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II), age >50, male sex, immunosuppression (organ transplant recipients have 65× increased SCC risk). Actinic keratoses are premalignant: individual lesion risk of progression to SCC is 0.025–16% per year, but the field often contains multiple lesions. Treatment: cryotherapy, topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, photodynamic therapy, or diclofenac gel.

Actinic keratoses represent a field cancerization effect where the surrounding skin also has subclinical dysplasia. The Canadian Dermatology Association recommends field-directed therapy (5-FU, imiquimod, PDT) rather than lesion-directed therapy alone when multiple AKs are present. Organ transplant recipients require regular dermatologic surveillance due to dramatically increased skin cancer risk.

**Q:** What are the features of melanoma that warrant urgent referral, and what does the 'ugly duckling sign' refer to?

**A:** Urgent referral criteria (ABCDE): Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation (multiple shades of brown, black, red, white, blue), Diameter >6 mm, Evolution (any changing lesion). The ugly duckling sign: a nevus that looks different from all the patient's other nevi — it stands out as the outlier. This sign has high sensitivity for melanoma detection, especially in patients with many nevi.

The Canadian Dermatology Association promotes the ugly duckling sign as a complement to ABCDE criteria. Dermoscopy performed by trained clinicians improves diagnostic accuracy by 10–30%. Any lesion with rapid change, new pigmentation, bleeding, or ulceration warrants excisional biopsy with 2 mm margins for histologic assessment. Shave biopsies should be avoided if melanoma is suspected to preserve Breslow depth measurement.

**Q:** What is the Leser-Trelat sign and what malignancy should it raise concern for?

**A:** Leser-Trelat sign is the sudden appearance of multiple seborrheic keratoses, which can be a paraneoplastic phenomenon most commonly associated with gastric adenocarcinoma.

Although seborrheic keratoses are individually benign, rapid eruptive appearance of many lesions should prompt investigation for internal malignancy, especially GI tract tumours.

**Q:** What is the ABCDE rule for evaluating pigmented lesions, and when should excisional biopsy be considered?

**A:** ABCDE: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variation, Diameter >6 mm, Evolution (change). Excisional biopsy should be considered for rapid change, asymmetry, varied colours, irregular borders, persistent pain, or persistent bleeding.

The ABCDE criteria help identify melanoma risk in pigmented lesions. Any new or changing pigmented lesion warrants evaluation; excisional biopsy with narrow margins is preferred to ensure complete histological assessment.

**Q:** What are congenital nevomelanocytic nevi (CNN) and what is their malignant transformation risk?

**A:** CNN (congenital hairy nevi): sharply demarcated pigmented papule/plaque with coarse hairs. Malignant transformation rate is 1–5% and correlates with lesion size. Giant CNN can cause neurocutaneous melanosis.

CNN are classified by size: small (<1.5 cm), medium, large (>20 cm), and giant (>40 cm). Giant lesions carry the highest melanoma risk. MRI should be considered if neurological involvement is suspected.

**Q:** What is an atypical (dysplastic) nevus and what is its clinical significance?

**A:** Atypical nevus: variegated macule/papule with irregular borders, distinct melanocytes in the basal layer. Five or more atypical nevi increases melanoma risk. Familial atypical mole and melanoma syndrome (FAMM) involves numerous dysplastic nevi.

Atypical nevi require baseline photography, close surveillance, and whole-body skin examinations. Excisional biopsy is indicated if a lesion is changing or highly atypical. Family history of melanoma markedly increases risk.

**Q:** What is xeroderma pigmentosum and what is its management?

**A:** Xeroderma pigmentosum: rare autosomal recessive disorder of DNA repair (nucleotide excision repair gene mutations), causing extreme UV sensitivity, blistering, xerosis, skin colour changes, high skin cancer risk, and possible neurological involvement. Management: strict sun avoidance, anticancer drugs (isotretinoin, fluorouracil) in adults.

Xeroderma pigmentosum patients have markedly elevated rates of skin malignancies (BCC, SCC, melanoma). UV damage is irreversible, making prevention the cornerstone of management.

**Q:** What is the classification of Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) vs Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) based on body surface area?

**A:** SJS: <10% BSA epidermal detachment; SJS/TEN overlap: 10-30% BSA; TEN: >30% BSA. Prognosis: 5% mortality in SJS, 30% in TEN.

SJS/TEN are characterized by full-thickness epidermal loss, positive Nikolsky sign, mucosal erosions, and flaccid bullae. The SCORTEN score (age ≥40, malignancy, BSA ≥10%, tachycardia >120, urea >10 mmol, glucose >14, bicarbonate <20) predicts mortality.

**Q:** What is the management of Stevens-Johnson Syndrome/Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis?

**A:** Discontinue offending drug, admit to intermediate/intensive care or burn unit, supportive care (IV fluids, electrolytes, nutrition, pain control, wound care, sterile handling, monitor for infection). Consider IVIg, cyclosporine, corticosteroids, or etanercept.

Evidence suggests cyclosporine is most associated with survival benefit (OR 0.1; 95% CI 0.0-0.4). Glucocorticoids also showed benefit in systematic review. IVIg had no demonstrated benefit.

**Q:** What is the SCORTEN score and how does it predict mortality in TEN?

**A:** SCORTEN assigns one point each for: age ≥40, malignancy, BSA detached ≥10%, tachycardia >120 bpm, urea >10 mmol/L, glucose >14 mmol/L, bicarbonate <20 mmol/L. Score at admission predicts survival: 94% (score 0-1), 87% (score 2), 53% (score 3), 25% (score 4), 17% (score ≥5).

SCORTEN is validated for predicting mortality in TEN and guides clinical management decisions including appropriate level of care. It can be used to determine whether patients should be treated in a burn unit.

**Q:** What are actinic keratoses (AK), and what is their risk of malignant transformation?

**A:** AK are ill-defined, scaly, erythematous papules/plaques on sun-damaged skin with a gritty 'sandpaper' texture. They result from UV (UVB) radiation damage and p53 mutation in keratinocytes. Risk of transformation to SCC: <1 in 1000 per year, but higher if persistent, immunosuppressed, or prior skin cancer.

AK are pre-malignant lesions found on sun-exposed areas (face, helices, scalp, dorsal hands/forearms). Risk factors for progression to SCC include immunosuppression, history of skin cancer, and persistence of the lesion.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for actinic keratoses?

**A:** Destructive: cryotherapy, shave excision with curettage ± electrodesiccation. Topical: 5-fluorouracil cream (2–4 weeks), imiquimod 5% (twice weekly for 16 weeks), diclofenac sodium 3% (BID for 90 days), tirbanibulin 1% (once daily for 5 days). Other: photodynamic therapy, chemical peels (TCA, phenol), laser resurfacing, excision.

Cochrane review found cryotherapy, diclofenac, 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, ingenol mebutate, photodynamic therapy, and TCA peels all effective. Photodynamic therapy and imiquimod had better cosmetic outcomes.

**Q:** What is leukoplakia and what are its risk factors and significance?

**A:** A morphologic term for homogeneous or speckled white plaques with sharply demarcated borders on the oropharynx (floor of mouth, soft palate, ventral/lateral tongue). It is precancerous and can progress to oral SCC. Strongly associated with tobacco use and alcohol consumption.

Leukoplakia has 1–5% prevalence in adults >30 years; M>F, fair-skinned; most common oral mucosal pre-malignant lesion. Low-risk lesions require elimination of carcinogens and follow-up; moderate/dysplastic lesions require excision or cryotherapy. Biopsy is mandatory.

**Q:** What is the most common type of skin cancer in humans, and what are its subtypes?

**A:** Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) — most common malignancy in humans (75% of malignant skin tumours in >40 year-olds). Subtypes: (1) Nodular BCC (most common): pearly, telangiectatic rolled border with central ulceration. (2) Pigmented BCC: pigment in translucent lesion, mimics melanoma. (3) Superficial BCC: least aggressive. (4) Morpheaform/sclerosing BCC: flesh/yellow shiny plaque with indistinct borders, most aggressive.

BCC arises from malignant proliferation of basal keratinocytes. It is locally aggressive but rarely metastatic. Over 80% occur on sun-exposed sites, with a latency of 20–50 years between UV damage and BCC onset. Risk factors include male sex, skin phototypes I–II, and arsenic exposure.

**Q:** When is Mohs surgery indicated and what are its advantages?

**A:** Mohs surgery is indicated for BCC and SCC on the face or in areas difficult to reconstruct; can also be considered for lentigo maligna and melanoma in situ. Advantages: highest cure rate, best cosmetic results, preserves healthy skin tissue, provides immediate margin control.

Mohs surgery is expensive and resource-intensive. For BCC <2 cm treated early, the overall cure rate is 95%. Traditional excision, electrodesiccation/curettage, and cryotherapy are other options for appropriate lesions.

**Q:** What systemic agents are used for metastatic basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma?

**A:** Vismodegib: for metastatic BCC and Gorlin Syndrome (multiple BCCs); side effects include muscle spasms, hair loss, abnormal taste, weight loss, nausea, amenorrhea. Cemiplimab: for metastatic SCC and metastatic BCC refractory to vismodegib; side effects include musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, nausea.

Vismodegib is a hedgehog pathway inhibitor. Gorlin Syndrome (basal cell nevus syndrome) is a genetic predisposition to multiple BCCs. Cemiplimab is a PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor.

**Q:** What is Bowen's disease and how does it differ from invasive SCC?

**A:** Bowen's disease is squamous cell carcinoma in situ — a sharply demarcated erythematous patch/thin plaque with scale/crusting, typically 1–3 cm. It is confined to the epidermis. It evolves to invasive SCC in 10–20% of cutaneous lesions and >20% of mucosal lesions.

Bowen's disease is treated similarly to superficial BCC — options include topical imiquimod, cryotherapy, 5-fluorouracil, and electrodesiccation/curettage. Progression to invasive SCC involves breach of the basement membrane.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and prognosis of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the skin?

**A:** Hyperkeratotic, indurated pink/red papule/plaque/nodule with surface scale/crust ± ulceration on sun-exposed sites (face, ears, scalp, forearms, dorsum of hands). More rapid enlargement than BCC. Rate of metastasis: 2–5%. Higher risk if diameter >2 cm, depth >2 mm, recurrent, involving bone/muscle/nerve, on scalp/ears/nose/lips, immunosuppressed, or arising from scar/chronic ulcer.

SCC is the second most common cutaneous neoplasm in less-pigmented skin types and the most common in immunocompromised patients (e.g., organ transplant recipients). Risk factors include UV radiation, HPV 16/18, arsenic, and chronic inflammation (discoid lupus, hidradenitis suppurativa).

**Q:** What are the clinical features of keratoacanthoma and how is it managed?

**A:** Rapidly growing, firm, dome-shaped, erythematous or skin-coloured volcano-like nodule with a central keratin-filled crater on sun-exposed skin; may spontaneously regress. Considered a low-grade variant of SCC; treat as SCC until proven otherwise. Management: surgical excision or saucerization (shave biopsy) with electrodesiccation of the base, or intralesional methotrexate/5-fluorouracil injection.

Keratoacanthoma is an epithelial neoplasm with atypical keratinocytes, most common in adults >50 years. Etiology includes HPV, UV radiation, and chemical carcinogens (tar, mineral oil). It must be differentiated from nodular BCC, MCC, and SCC.

**Q:** What is the ABCDE mnemonic for recognizing malignant melanoma?

**A:** A — Asymmetry. B — Border (irregular and/or indistinct). C — Colour (varied). D — Diameter (increasing or >6 mm). E — Evolution (change in colour, size, or shape). Sensitivity 92%, specificity 100% for distinguishing melanoma from benign moles.

Approximately 2/3 of melanomas arise de novo without a pre-existing nevus. Dermoscopic features and the ABCDE criteria aid clinical diagnosis. Excisional biopsy is preferred for histologic confirmation.

**Q:** What are the clinical subtypes of malignant melanoma listed from most to least common?

**A:** 1) Superficial spreading melanoma (60–70%): lateral spread in epidermis then dermis; irregular indurated enlarging plaque. 2) Nodular melanoma (15–30%): vertical growth, blue-black nodule, rapidly fatal; EFG = elevated, firm, growing. 3) Lentigo maligna / lentigo maligna melanoma (5–15%): elderly, flat brown macule expanding radially. 4) Acral lentiginous melanoma (5–10%): palmar, plantar, subungual; most common in Type 6 skin. 5) Amelanotic melanoma (2–8%): pink/red, no pigment, often delayed diagnosis.

Superficial spreading melanoma is most likely associated with a pre-existing nevus. Nodular melanoma may present as amelanotic (pink). Acral lentiginous melanoma is the most common subtype in patients with darker skin types and has poor prognosis on mucous membranes.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for malignant melanoma?

**A:** Sun exposure (sunburns), tanning beds, fair skin/blue eyes/red or fair hair, freckling, immunosuppression (e.g., renal transplantation), dysplastic nevi, >50 common nevi, large congenital nevi (>20 cm), familial dysplastic nevus syndrome, positive personal/family history of melanoma, increasing age.

In Canada, melanoma occurs in approximately 1 in 75 individuals. Most common sites are the back in men and calves in women. Worse prognosis is associated with male sex, scalp/hand/foot location, late lesion, and absence of pre-existing nevus.

**Q:** How is malignant melanoma managed surgically?

**A:** Excisional biopsy is preferred (margin determined by Breslow depth); otherwise incisional biopsy. Full depth of dermis must be removed, extending beyond edges of lesion after histologic diagnosis. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is assessed at time of wide excision. Sentinel lymph node dissection is controversial.

Sentinel lymph node biopsy is offered to patients with stage IB or higher melanoma (>1 mm Breslow thickness OR 0.8–1 mm with ulceration OR any thickness with ulceration). Lesions that appear to regress may be deeper than anticipated.

**Q:** What is the 'EFG' mnemonic used for in dermatology and what does it represent?

**A:** EFG stands for Elevated, Firm, Growing — a mnemonic for nodular melanoma, which is the subtype that grows primarily vertically with little lateral spread. Nodular melanoma is uniformly ulcerated, blue-black, and rapidly fatal, but may be amelanotic (pink).

Nodular melanoma accounts for 15–30% of all melanomas. Because it lacks the classic ABCDE features initially, the EFG mnemonic helps identify this rapidly growing variant before extensive invasion occurs.

**Q:** What are the indications, dosing schedule, and key adverse effects of imiquimod 5% cream (Aldara®)?

**A:** Imiquimod 5% cream is applied 3x/week overnight (6–10 h then wash off) for up to 16 weeks. Indications include genital warts, cutaneous warts, actinic keratosis (AK), and superficial basal cell carcinoma (BCC). Side effects include local erythema, ulceration, edema, and flu-like symptoms.

Imiquimod is an immune response modifier (toll-like receptor 7 agonist) that induces local cytokine production; it works best on mucosal warts. Patients should avoid natural/artificial UV exposure during treatment.

**Q:** What did the BRIM-3 trial demonstrate about vemurafenib in metastatic melanoma, and what mutation must be present?

**A:** BRIM-3 (NEJM 2011) showed that vemurafenib, a BRAF kinase inhibitor, improved overall survival (84% vs. 64% at 6 months) and progression-free survival compared to dacarbazine in patients with untreated metastatic melanoma with BRAF V600E mutation. Vemurafenib reduced risk of death by 63%. Common adverse events: arthralgia, rash, alopecia, keratoacanthoma, photosensitivity.

BRAF V600E mutation must be confirmed before vemurafenib is used. This was a landmark trial establishing targeted therapy as superior to chemotherapy in BRAF-mutated metastatic melanoma. Keratoacanthomas and cutaneous SCCs can arise during vemurafenib therapy.

**Q:** What was demonstrated in the landmark ipilimumab trial (NCT00094653) for metastatic melanoma?

**A:** In this NEJM 2010 trial of 676 HLA-A*0201-positive patients with unresectable stage III/IV melanoma, ipilimumab ± gp100 vaccine showed median survival of 10–10.1 months vs. 6.4 months with gp100 alone. Grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events occurred in 10–15% of ipilimumab-treated patients.

Ipilimumab is a CTLA-4 blocking antibody that enhances anti-tumour immune responses. This was the first agent to demonstrate improved overall survival in metastatic melanoma and established checkpoint inhibition as a therapeutic strategy.

**Q:** What did the RELATIVITY-047 trial establish about dual checkpoint blockade in untreated advanced melanoma?

**A:** RELATIVITY-047 (NEJM 2022) showed that relatlimab (LAG-3 inhibitor) plus nivolumab (PD-1 inhibitor) achieved median progression-free survival of 10.1 months vs. 4.6 months with nivolumab alone (HR 0.75, p=0.006) in previously untreated stage III/IV melanoma.

LAG-3 is an immune checkpoint that, when combined with PD-1 blockade, produces additive immunostimulatory effects. This trial established dual checkpoint inhibition as a new standard of care for advanced melanoma, superior to single-agent PD-1 blockade.

**Q:** In the NCT03698019 trial, what did neoadjuvant-adjuvant pembrolizumab show for resectable advanced melanoma?

**A:** NCT03698019 (NEJM 2023) in patients with resectable stage IIIB–IVC melanoma (n=313) showed event-free survival of 72% (neoadjuvant-adjuvant pembrolizumab) vs. 49% (adjuvant-only pembrolizumab) at follow-up (p=0.004). Neoadjuvant + adjuvant PD-1 blockade was superior to adjuvant therapy alone.

Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) given before and after surgery for resectable advanced melanoma produces greater event-free survival than post-surgical therapy alone, likely by priming the immune response to micrometastatic disease present at surgery.

**Q:** What is the AJCC staging and approximate 5-year survival for melanoma with Breslow thickness <1.0 mm (no ulceration)?

**A:** Stage I (T1a-T2a); approximately 90% 5-year survival.

Breslow thickness is the most important prognostic factor in melanoma. Thin (<1 mm) non-ulcerated melanomas have an excellent prognosis with 5-year survival ~90%.

**Q:** What is the approximate 5-year survival for Stage IV melanoma (any metastases, T4 >4.0 mm)?

**A:** Approximately 10% 5-year survival.

Stage IV melanoma with distant metastases carries a very poor prognosis (~10% 5-year survival). Systemic therapies including immunotherapy (anti-PD1, anti-CTLA-4) and BRAF inhibitors have improved outcomes in recent years.

**Q:** Which targeted therapy is used for unresectable or metastatic melanoma with a BRAF V600E mutation?

**A:** BRAF inhibitors such as vemurafenib (small molecule targeted therapy).

BRAF V600E mutations occur in ~50% of melanomas. Targeted BRAF inhibitors provide significant tumour response but are limited to patients with confirmed BRAF mutations.

**Q:** What immunotherapy agents are used for unresectable/metastatic melanoma?

**A:** Anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab), anti-PD1 agents (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), and high-dose IFN.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1 pathways have revolutionized treatment of advanced melanoma, improving durable response rates and overall survival.

**Q:** What virus causes Kaposi sarcoma, and what are the 4 clinical types?

**A:** Human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8). The 4 types are: classical (Mediterranean older adults), endemic (sub-Saharan Africa), iatrogenic (immunosuppression/renal transplant), and AIDS-related.

Kaposi sarcoma is an angioproliferative neoplasm requiring HHV-8 infection. AIDS-related KS is the most aggressive form and is an AIDS-defining illness.

**Q:** Describe the typical skin lesions of Kaposi sarcoma.

**A:** Purplish, reddish-blue, or dark brown/black macules, plaques, and nodules; typically on distal extremities; can ulcerate and bleed; also affects GI tract and lymphatics causing lymphedema.

KS lesions are vascular in origin. Diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy and HHV-8 PCR. Treatment varies by extent — superficial lesions may be treated locally; systemic disease requires radiation or chemotherapy.

**Q:** What are the causes and distinguishing features of Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) vs. Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)?

**A:** Both caused by drugs (phenytoin, sulfas, penicillins, NSAIDs), bone marrow transplantation, or blood transfusions. SJS: <10% BSA involvement. SJS/TEN overlap: 10-30% BSA. TEN: >30% BSA. Both show diffuse erythema, necrosis, severe mucous membrane involvement, and entire epidermis desquamation. TEN mortality >50%.

SJS and TEN are severe mucocutaneous reactions predominantly drug-induced (onset usually 1-3 weeks after starting causative drug). Key management: stop precipitating medication, fluid resuscitation, wound care. Early transfer to burn centre improves outcomes. Corticosteroids are controversial (may increase infection risk).

## General

**Q:** What two cell types undergo age-related decline in skin regenerative abilities?

**A:** Keratinocytes and fibroblasts

Both cell types lose self-renewing capacities, have altered proliferative activity, and undergo functional decline with aging.

**Q:** What is senile purpura and how does it present?

**A:** Spontaneous non-blanchable red-to-purple patches that resolve over 1-3 weeks, leaving residual brown-yellow discoloration from hemosiderin deposition

Senile purpura (solar/actinic purpura) results from age-related loss of skin integrity and structural support.

**Q:** What is the normal timeline for keratinocyte progression from stratum basale to stratum corneum?

**A:** Approximately 4 weeks

Keratinocytes undergo continuous renewal and migration from the basal layer to the surface.

**Q:** What is the keratinocyte to melanocyte ratio in the stratum basale?

**A:** 10:1

Despite this ratio, melanocyte numbers are equal among all races, explaining why differences in skin color result from melanosome activity rather than cell number.

**Q:** What components make up the pilosebaceous unit?

**A:** Hair, hair follicle, sebaceous gland, and arrector pili muscle

The pilosebaceous unit is a functional complex involved in hair maintenance and sebum production.

**Q:** What is the protective function of sebum?

**A:** Covers skin surface to provide a protective barrier with antifungal properties

Sebum is secreted onto the skin surface via hair follicles, creating a lipid barrier.

**Q:** Where are apocrine sweat glands concentrated in humans?

**A:** Axillae and perineum

Apocrine glands are considered vestigial in humans but are functional in other species for pheromone production.

**Q:** What is the primary function of eccrine sweat glands?

**A:** Temperature regulation via secretion of sweat to cool the skin surface

Eccrine glands are found throughout the body (except lips, nail beds, glans penis) and are essential for thermoregulation.

**Q:** How does melanin provide UV radiation protection?

**A:** Melanin is transferred from melanocytes to keratinocytes in melanosomes, where it absorbs UV radiation

The melanin-keratinocyte unit represents the skin's primary photoprotective mechanism.

**Q:** Where are Merkel cells located and what is their function?

**A:** Stratum basale; involved in touch sensation

Merkel cells are specialized sensory cells that form close associations with nerve endings.

**Q:** What is the difference between a papule and a plaque?

**A:** Papule is <1cm diameter; plaque is ≥1cm diameter (raised superficial lesion)

Size is the distinguishing feature using SCALDA criteria for lesion classification.

**Q:** What is lichenification?

**A:** Thickening of the skin with accentuation of normal skin markings

Lichenification results from chronic rubbing or scratching and is characteristic of atopic dermatitis.

**Q:** What is the difference between a vesicle and a bulla?

**A:** Vesicle is <1cm diameter; bulla is ≥1cm diameter (elevated fluid-filled lesion)

Both contain fluid within the epidermis or subepidermally, distinguished by size.

**Q:** What is the key feature of a wheal?

**A:** Transient (<24 hours), blanchable papule or plaque with halo and central clearing, formed by dermal edema

Wheals are characteristic of urticaria and represent rapid-onset, short-lived edema.

**Q:** What is the difference between open and closed comedones?

**A:** Open comedone (blackhead) has an dilated open pore; closed comedone (whitehead) has a sealed pore

Both represent collections of sebum and keratin but differ in whether the follicle opening is patent.

**Q:** How does skin contribute to thermal regulation in cold environments?

**A:** Peripheral vasoconstriction, hair, and subcutaneous adipose tissue provide insulation

These mechanisms reduce heat loss to maintain core body temperature.

**Q:** What are the basophilic granules in stratum granulosum?

**A:** Keratohyalin granules, which contain proteins involved in keratinization

These granules mark the transition zone where cells begin the process of cornification.

**Q:** What is male pattern baldness also known as?

**A:** Androgenic Alopecia

This androgen-dependent condition is characterized by progressive hair loss in a characteristic distribution pattern.

**Q:** How long does it take for cells to progress from stratum basale to stratum corneum?

**A:** Approximately 4 weeks

This continuous turnover maintains the epidermal barrier and replaces damaged cells.

**Q:** What do melanocytes produce and how do they transfer their product to keratinocytes?

**A:** Melanosomes containing melanin, transferred via dendritic processes to surrounding keratinocytes

This transfer determines skin and hair pigmentation and provides UV radiation protection.

**Q:** What is the pilosebaceous unit?

**A:** Hair + hair follicle + sebaceous gland + arrector pili muscle

This functional unit is involved in hair growth, sebum production, and arrector pili muscle contraction.

**Q:** Where are sebaceous glands notably ABSENT?

**Q:** What is the key histological difference between Bullous Pemphigoid and Pemphigus Vulgaris?

**A:** Bullous Pemphigoid has subepidermal (tense) bullae, while Pemphigus Vulgaris has intraepidermal (flaccid) bullae

This distinction reflects the different target antigens: BP180/BP230 are at the dermal-epidermal junction (hemidesmosomes), while desmogleins are within the epidermis (intercellular adhesion)

The flaccid nature of pemphigus bullae reflects intraepidermal splitting, while the tense bullae of pemphigoid reflect subepidermal separation

**A:** Epidermal detachment with shear stress; positive in Pemphigus Vulgaris, Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome, and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis

Nikolsky sign reflects loss of intercellular adhesion in the epidermis, indicating intraepidermal blistering mechanisms

**A:** Onset 1-6 weeks after first drug exposure; symptoms persist for weeks after drug withdrawal

**A:** Discontinue offending drug plus prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/d; consider cyclosporine if steroids are inadequate

**Q:** What complication can occur if DRESS is not treated promptly?

**A:** Progression to generalized exfoliative dermatitis/erythroderma

**Q:** A 3-month-old infant presents with a hot, firm, red-blue plaque on the face that appeared shortly after birth and has been growing. What is the most likely diagnosis, and when should treatment be considered?

**A:** Hemangioma of infancy. Treatment is considered if there is functional impairment (visual compromise, airway obstruction, high-output cardiac failure) or cosmesis. 50% resolve spontaneously by age 5. First-line treatment includes topical timolol or systemic propranolol.

Infantile hemangiomas are benign vascular tumours that proliferate after birth then regress. Early specialist referral is needed for high-risk locations. Propranolol has become standard first-line systemic therapy.

**Q:** A 45-year-old man with chronic liver disease has multiple small red lesions on his upper body that blanch when the central arteriole is compressed with a paperclip. What are these lesions?

Spider angiomas blanch when pressure is applied to the central arteriole. While they can occur in healthy individuals, multiple spider angiomas suggest a hyperestrogenic state, most commonly chronic liver disease.

**Q:** What is the backbone of topical acne therapy, and what combination approach is most effective for mild-to-moderate acne?

**A:** Topical retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene) are the backbone of topical acne therapy. A combination of topical retinoids with topical erythromycin or clindamycin is more effective than either agent alone. Benzoyl peroxide should be included to prevent antibiotic resistance.

Retinoids target the microcomedone, the precursor to all acne lesions, by normalizing follicular keratinization. Adding benzoyl peroxide prevents C. acnes resistance that occurs with antibiotic monotherapy.

**Q:** A 17-year-old male with severe nodulocystic acne is being started on isotretinoin. What baseline investigations are required, and what is the most critical counselling point?

**A:** Baseline investigations: fasting lipid profile (risk of hypertriglyceridemia), LFTs, and pregnancy test (f-hCG). The most critical counselling point is teratogenicity -- isotretinoin is associated with spontaneous abortion and major birth defects (facial dysmorphism, cognitive impairment). Two forms of contraception are required for female patients.

Isotretinoin is highly teratogenic and requires pregnancy exclusion before initiation. Hypertriglyceridemia and hepatotoxicity are monitored adverse effects. Most other side effects are temporary and resolve on discontinuation.

**Q:** How can rosacea be differentiated from acne vulgaris on clinical examination?

**A:** Rosacea is differentiated from acne by: (1) absence of comedones, (2) predilection for the central face (forehead, nose, cheeks, chin), and (3) presence of flushing and telangiectasia. Rosacea also features dome-shaped inflammatory papules and pustules with remissions and exacerbations triggered by heat, cold, alcohol, and spicy food.

The key distinguishing feature is the absence of comedones in rosacea. Acne has comedones as a hallmark. Rosacea predominantly affects fair-skinned individuals aged 30-50 with F>M prevalence.

**Q:** A 6-month-old infant presents with erythematous, pruritic plaques on the face and extensor surfaces. There is a family history of asthma. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what is the underlying genetic association?

**A:** Atopic dermatitis (eczema). It is associated with a genetic mutation in the filaggrin gene (FLG), causing impaired epidermal barrier function and prolonged skin dryness. It is part of the atopic triad with asthma and allergic rhinitis.

Atopic dermatitis affects 10-20% of children under 5 in developed countries. The filaggrin gene mutation leads to barrier dysfunction, allowing allergen penetration. Distribution varies by age: face/extensors in infants, flexural areas in older children and adults.

**Q:** What are the advantages of topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) over topical corticosteroids in the management of atopic dermatitis?

**A:** Topical calcineurin inhibitors do not cause skin atrophy, making them safe for sensitive areas such as the face and neck. They can be used as steroid-sparing agents for long-term use and applied 2-3 times per week to recurrent sites for relapse prevention. The main side effect is a burning sensation that decreases with continued use.

Long-term topical corticosteroid use causes atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, and purpura. Calcineurin inhibitors avoid these effects by selectively inhibiting T-cell activation without affecting dermal collagen synthesis.

**Q:** How do you distinguish irritant contact dermatitis from allergic contact dermatitis?

**A:** Irritant contact dermatitis is a non-immune toxic injury causing erythema, dryness, and burning, occurring in anyone given sufficient irritant exposure. Allergic contact dermatitis is a Type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction causing papulovesicular eruptions with pruritus, requiring prior sensitization that persists indefinitely. Patch testing identifies the specific allergen in allergic contact dermatitis.

Irritant contact dermatitis is more common and affects the hands most frequently. Allergic contact dermatitis requires sensitization and can be confirmed with patch testing. Common allergens include nickel, fragrance, and tattoo dyes.

**Q:** A patient with chronic venous insufficiency presents with erythematous, scaly, pruritic plaques on the medial ankles with brown hemosiderin deposition. What is the most important therapy?

**A:** Compression stockings are the most important therapy for stasis dermatitis (if no peripheral arterial disease). Additional management includes leg elevation, daily moisturizer after shower, and mid-to-high potency topical corticosteroids for inflammation.

Stasis dermatitis results from chronic venous insufficiency causing venous stasis and surrounding soft tissue inflammation. Complications include ulceration (commonly at the medial malleolus) and secondary bacterial infections. Peripheral arterial disease must be ruled out before compression.

**Q:** What are the '6 Ps' of lichen planus, and what is the pathognomonic finding on oral examination?

**A:** The 6 Ps are: Purple, Polygonal, Planar (flat-topped), Pruritic, Papules, and Peripheral (wrists). Wickham's striae -- a lacy, whitish reticular network on oral mucosa -- is the pathognomonic finding.

Lichen planus is an immune-mediated condition affecting skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and nails. Oral involvement occurs in 60% of cases. Erosive oral lichen planus carries an increased risk of SCC. It is rarely associated with hepatitis C.

**Q:** A 25-year-old presents with an oval, scaly plaque on the trunk followed 2 weeks later by multiple smaller oval plaques arranged along skin tension lines in a 'Christmas tree' pattern. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Pityriasis rosea. The initial lesion is the 'herald patch,' and the subsequent eruption follows Langer lines (skin tension lines). It is self-limiting, resolving spontaneously in 6-12 weeks.

Pityriasis rosea is an acute, self-limiting eruption suspected to be caused by HHV-7 or HHV-6 reactivation. No treatment is required. Symptomatic relief with TCS can be provided if pruritic. The 'Christmas tree' pattern on the back is characteristic.

**Q:** What is the Auspitz sign, and in which dermatological condition is it found?

**A:** The Auspitz sign is pinpoint bleeding observed when the silvery-white scale of plaque psoriasis is removed. It is characteristic of plaque psoriasis.

The Auspitz sign results from the thinned epidermis overlying dilated and tortuous capillaries in the dermal papillae. Psoriasis is characterized by well-circumscribed erythematous plaques with silvery scales on extensor surfaces, scalp, and trunk.

**Q:** A 30-year-old patient with psoriasis affecting >10% BSA (PASI >10) has failed topical therapy. What are the next steps in management?

**A:** For moderate-to-severe psoriasis (BSA >10%, PASI >10): phototherapy (if accessible), systemic therapy (methotrexate, cyclosporine, acitretin, apremilast), or biologic therapy (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17, anti-IL-23 agents). TCS plus topical vitamin D3 analogue can be used as adjunct therapy.

Severity guides treatment: mild psoriasis is managed topically, while moderate-to-severe disease requires systemic or biologic therapy. Biologics such as secukinumab (anti-IL-17A) and guselkumab (anti-IL-23) show high efficacy rates.

**Q:** What is guttate psoriasis, and what antecedent infection is commonly associated with it?

**A:** Guttate psoriasis presents as discrete, scattered salmon-pink small scaling papules diffusely on the trunk and legs, sparing palms and soles. It is commonly preceded by streptococcal pharyngitis (GAS). Treatment includes broadband UVB phototherapy and antibiotics if GAS is confirmed on throat culture.

Guttate psoriasis is triggered by molecular mimicry between streptococcal antigens and keratinocyte proteins. It typically affects younger patients and may resolve after treatment of the streptococcal infection, though some patients progress to chronic plaque psoriasis.

**Q:** What is erythrodermic psoriasis, and why is it a medical emergency?

**A:** Erythrodermic psoriasis is generalized erythema affecting >90% of BSA with fine desquamative scale. It is potentially life-threatening due to risk of dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and hypothermia. It requires immediate hospitalization with IV fluids, electrolyte monitoring, and treatment with cyclosporine, acitretin, methotrexate, or biologics.

The massive skin inflammation leads to loss of thermoregulation and barrier function. Aggravating factors include lithium, beta-blockers, NSAIDs, antimalarials, and sudden corticosteroid withdrawal. It is one of the few true dermatologic emergencies.

**Q:** How do you differentiate bullous pemphigoid from pemphigus vulgaris based on clinical features and pathophysiology?

**A:** Bullous pemphigoid: tense subepidermal bullae, pruritic, IgG against basement membrane (BP180/BP230), heals without scarring, age 60-80. Pemphigus vulgaris: flaccid intraepidermal bullae, non-pruritic, IgG against desmoglein-1 and -3, positive Nikolsky sign, oral involvement in 90%, age 15-50, potentially fatal without treatment.

The mnemonic is: pemphigus Vulgaris = superficial/intraepidermal/flaccid; pemphigoiD = deeper/dermal-epidermal junction/tense. The level of blister formation determines the tenseness of the bulla.

**Q:** A patient with intensely pruritic grouped papulovesicles on the extensor elbows, knees, and buttocks is found to have IgA deposits on skin biopsy. What is the diagnosis and what associated GI condition must be investigated?

**A:** Dermatitis herpetiformis. 90% of patients have gluten-sensitive enteropathy (celiac disease), though only 20% have intestinal symptoms. Treatment includes dapsone and a lifelong gluten-free diet, which also reduces the risk of intestinal lymphoma.

Dermatitis herpetiformis is caused by transglutaminase IgA deposits in the skin. Patients carry HLA-DQ2 or DQ8. The gluten-free diet treats both the skin and intestinal manifestations and reduces lymphoma risk in untreated celiac disease.

**Q:** What are the four criteria used to classify drug reactions by Naranjo et al.?

**A:** (1) Temporal relationship between drug exposure and reaction, (2) recognized response to the suspected drug, (3) improvement after drug withdrawal, (4) recurrence on re-challenge. A definite drug reaction requires all 4 criteria; probable requires criteria 1-3; possible requires only criterion 1.

The Naranjo criteria provide a systematic approach to establishing causality in adverse drug reactions. Re-challenge is rarely performed deliberately due to safety concerns but may occur inadvertently.

**Q:** A patient presents with a morbilliform rash, fever, cervical lymphadenopathy, and hepatitis 3 weeks after starting carbamazepine. What is the diagnosis, and what is the mortality rate if untreated?

**A:** Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS) / Drug-Induced Hypersensitivity Syndrome (DIHS). Mortality is 10% if severe, undiagnosed, and untreated. Common causative agents include anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine), sulfonamides, and allopurinol.

DRESS typically presents 1-6 weeks after first drug exposure and persists weeks after drug withdrawal. It starts on the face and spreads caudally without mucosal involvement. Treatment is drug discontinuation plus prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day.

**Q:** What distinguishes Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) from toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and what is the mortality difference?

**A:** SJS: <10% BSA with epidermal detachment; 5% mortality. SJS/TEN overlap: 10-30% BSA. TEN: >30% BSA; 30% mortality. Both present with prodromal rash, positive Nikolsky sign, full-thickness epidermal loss, and mucous membrane erosions. Common causative drugs include allopurinol, anti-epileptics, sulfonamides, and NSAIDs.

SJS and TEN represent a spectrum of the same disease, differentiated by BSA involvement. The SCORTEN score predicts mortality. HLA-B*1502 (carbamazepine in East Asians) and HLA-B*5801 (allopurinol) are pharmacogenomic risk factors. Treatment includes ICU admission, supportive care, and IVIg or cyclosporine.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1)?

**A:** Diagnosis requires 2 or more of: (1) >=6 cafe-au-lait macules (>1.5 cm in adults, >0.5 cm prepubertal), (2) axillary or inguinal freckling, (3) >=2 Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas), (4) optic glioma, (5) >=2 neurofibromas or 1 plexiform neurofibroma, (6) distinctive bony lesion (sphenoid wing dysplasia), (7) heterozygous pathogenic NF1 variant.

NF1 is autosomal dominant with 1:3000 incidence and high spontaneous mutation rate. It is caused by loss-of-function of neurofibromin. Associated conditions include pheochromocytoma, scoliosis, and renal artery stenosis. A child of an affected parent needs only 1 criterion.

**Q:** What autoimmune conditions should be investigated in a patient diagnosed with vitiligo?

**A:** Thyroid disease (most common), pernicious anemia, Addison's disease, and Type 1 diabetes mellitus. Wood's lamp can detect lesions by illuminating UV light onto skin to reveal porcelain white (amelanotic) discolouration.

Vitiligo is an autoimmune destruction of melanocytes with 1% incidence. It displays the Koebner phenomenon and affects extensor and periorificial areas. Treatment options include topical calcineurin inhibitors, TCS, phototherapy (NB-UVB), and topical ruxolitinib (JAK inhibitor).

**Q:** A child presents with honey-crusted lesions on the face. What is the diagnosis, and what is the first-line treatment for localized disease?

**A:** Impetigo, caused by GAS, S. aureus, or both. For localized disease: topical antibiotics such as mupirocin 2% or fusidic acid 2% TID, continuing for 7-10 days after resolution. For extensive disease: systemic antibiotics (dicloxacillin or cephalexin for 7-10 days).

Impetigo is an acute superficial bacterial infection common in preschool children. The golden-yellow 'honey-crusted' appearance is characteristic. Differential diagnosis includes infected eczema, HSV, and VZV.

**Q:** How do erysipelas and cellulitis differ in terms of clinical features and depth of skin involvement?

**A:** Erysipelas involves the upper dermis: confluent erythematous plaque with sharp, raised edges, well-demarcated, very painful, caused primarily by GAS, spreads via lymphatics. Cellulitis involves the lower dermis/subcutaneous fat: unilateral erythematous flat lesion, poorly demarcated, not uniformly raised, caused by GAS and S. aureus.

Both present with erythema and pain but differ in depth and border definition. Erysipelas classically has sharply demarcated raised borders. Both are treated with beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillin/cephalexin) as first line.

**Q:** What is the treatment for tinea capitis, and why are oral agents required?

**A:** Oral terbinafine (Lamisil) for 4 weeks is the first-line treatment. Oral agents are required because the dermatophyte resides within the hair root, which topical agents cannot penetrate. Adjunctive antifungal shampoos (selenium sulfide, ketoconazole) may prevent spread.

Tinea capitis presents with round, scaly patches of alopecia with broken hairs. A kerion (boggy, purulent nodule) may form. It primarily affects children and is very contagious. Terbinafine is a CYP2D6 inhibitor with potential liver toxicity.

**Q:** A patient presents with intense nocturnal pruritus and linear burrows in the finger web spaces, axillae, and groin. What is the diagnosis and treatment?

**A:** Scabies (Sarcoptes scabiei). Treatment: apply permethrin 5% cream from neck down to soles, leave on 8-14 hours, repeat in 7 days. Also treat family/close contacts, wash linens in hot water. Ivermectin is an alternative. Pruritus may persist 2-3 weeks after effective treatment due to prolonged hypersensitivity.

Scabies is transmitted primarily through prolonged skin-to-skin contact with 1-month incubation. The mite burrows into the stratum corneum. Post-treatment pruritus is common and can be managed with mid-potency TCS and antihistamines.

**Q:** What is the Hutchinson sign in herpes zoster, and what does it signify?

**A:** Hutchinson's sign is herpes zoster (shingles) involving the tip of the nose, signifying ocular involvement via the nasociliary branch of the ophthalmic division (V1) of the trigeminal nerve. This requires urgent ophthalmologic evaluation.

The nasociliary branch supplies the nose tip and the eye. When zoster involves V1, corneal involvement (herpes zoster ophthalmicus) can lead to vision-threatening complications. Antiviral therapy must be initiated within 72 hours.

**Q:** What are the stages of syphilis and their characteristic skin findings?

**A:** Primary: single painless indurated chancre at inoculation site, heals in 4-6 weeks. Secondary (2-6 months later): maculopapular rash (non-pruritic, includes palms/soles), condylomata lata (genital wart-like papules), mucous patches. Tertiary (3-7 years later): gumma (granulomatous non-tender nodule).

Primary syphilis may have a negative VDRL; FTA-ABS is more sensitive early on. Dark field microscopy can detect spirochetes in chancre fluid. Treatment is penicillin G 2.4 million units IM -- single dose for primary/secondary, weekly for 3 weeks for tertiary.

**Q:** A 70-year-old man presents with a rough, gritty, erythematous papule with white scale on his sun-exposed forehead. What is the diagnosis, and what is the risk of malignant transformation?

**A:** Actinic keratosis (solar keratosis). The risk of transformation to squamous cell carcinoma is <1 in 1000 per year, but higher if persistent or in immunosuppressed patients. Treatment options include cryotherapy, topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, photodynamic therapy, or excision.

Actinic keratoses result from cumulative UVB damage to keratinocytes with UV-induced p53 gene mutations. They are easier to appreciate on palpation (gritty, sandpaper texture) than inspection. They are pre-malignant and should be treated to prevent SCC progression.

**Q:** What is the most common malignancy in humans, and what is the most common subtype?

**A:** Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common malignancy in humans, accounting for 75% of all malignant skin tumours in those over 40. The most common subtype is nodular BCC, presenting as a skin-coloured papule/nodule with a rolled, translucent ('pearly') telangiectatic border and depressed/ulcerated centre.

BCC arises from malignant proliferation of basal keratinocytes, primarily due to UVB exposure with a 20-50 year latency. It is locally aggressive but rarely metastasizes. Mohs surgery offers the highest cure rate for facial lesions. 95% cure rate if treated when <2 cm.

**Q:** What are the ABCDE criteria for evaluating a suspicious pigmented lesion for melanoma?

**A:** A = Asymmetry, B = Border irregularity, C = Colour variation (varied), D = Diameter (increasing or >6 mm), E = Evolution (change in colour, size, or shape). This has 92% sensitivity for melanoma.

About 2/3 of melanomas arise de novo without a pre-existing nevus. The most common subtype is superficial spreading melanoma (60-70%). Most common site is back in males and calves in females. Breslow thickness is the most important prognostic factor.

**Q:** A patient has a melanoma with Breslow thickness of 2.5 mm. What is the approximate AJCC stage and 5-year survival?

**A:** Breslow thickness 2.01-4.0 mm corresponds to Stage III (if node involvement) with approximately 45% 5-year survival, or Stage II (T3) with approximately 70% 5-year survival depending on ulceration and nodal status.

Breslow thickness is the single most important prognostic factor for melanoma. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is offered for melanomas >=1 mm (or 0.8 mm with ulceration). Treatment options for advanced melanoma include anti-PD1 agents (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab), and BRAF inhibitors for BRAF V600E mutations.

**Q:** What is mycosis fungoides, and what is the more aggressive variant called?

**A:** Mycosis fungoides is a cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) characterized by scaling, erythematous patches/plaques/tumours on the trunk and buttocks, caused by clonal proliferation of skin-homing CD4 T-cells. Sezary syndrome is the aggressive widespread variant with erythroderma, lymphadenopathy, and circulating Sezary cells.

Mycosis fungoides typically affects patients >50 years (M:F = 2:1) and has excellent prognosis in early disease. Hypopigmented MF is the most common subtype in children with darker skin. Sezary syndrome has high mortality and requires systemic therapy including extracorporeal photopheresis.

**Q:** A woman presents with recurrent painful inflammatory nodules with sinus tracts and scarring in the axillae and inguinal folds. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS). It is a chronic scarring inflammatory condition resulting from follicular occlusion in intertriginous zones rich in apocrine glands. Affects 1-4% of the population, F>M, onset in 2nd-3rd decade. Associated with smoking and obesity.

HS is part of the follicular occlusion tetrad (with cicatricial alopecia, acne conglobata, and pilonidal disease). Treatment ranges from topical antibiotics for mild disease to biologics (adalimumab, infliximab) for refractory moderate-to-severe disease. Behavioral modifications include smoking cessation and weight management.

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis for erythema nodosum, and what investigation should be ordered first?

**A:** Causes include idiopathic (40%), drugs (sulfonamides, OCPs), infections (GAS, TB, histoplasmosis, Yersinia), inflammation (sarcoidosis, Crohn's > UC), and malignancy (leukemia, Hodgkin's). A chest X-ray should be ordered first to rule out sarcoidosis and chest infection.

Erythema nodosum is a panniculitis presenting as tender red nodules on the shins. It is most common in women aged 15-30 (F:M = 3:1). Lesions resolve spontaneously in 6 weeks. The mnemonic 'No DOSUMM' helps: No cause, Drugs, Other infections, Sarcoidosis, UC, Malignancy, Many infections.

**Q:** What skin findings are associated with dermatomyositis?

**A:** Heliotrope rash (periorbital violaceous erythema with edema), Gottron's papules (violaceous flat-topped papules with atrophy over the knuckles/MCP/PIP joints), periungual erythema/telangiectasia, and calcinosis cutis. Dermatomyositis is associated with underlying malignancy (breast, lung, ovary).

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy with characteristic cutaneous findings. It is important to screen for underlying malignancy, especially in adult-onset cases. The skin findings may precede or accompany proximal muscle weakness.

**Q:** What is acanthosis nigricans, and what is its clinical significance?

**A:** Acanthosis nigricans is thickened, velvety hyperpigmentation of flexural skin, most commonly perinuchal. It is a cutaneous marker of tissue insulin resistance, associated with diabetes mellitus, obesity, and other endocrine disorders. It can also be associated with malignancy (especially GI adenocarcinoma).

The insulin resistance drives epidermal hyperproliferation via IGF-1 receptor activation. When it appears suddenly in a non-obese patient, malignancy should be suspected (paraneoplastic acanthosis nigricans). Treatment targets the underlying cause.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for hemangioma of infancy causing functional impairment?

**A:** Topical timolol or systemic propranolol. Systemic corticosteroids, laser treatment, or surgery are alternatives.

Hemangiomas of infancy are benign vascular tumours that appear shortly after birth and regress spontaneously in ~50% by age 5. Treatment is reserved for the ~10% causing functional impairment such as visual compromise or airway obstruction.

**Q:** What condition is associated with spider angiomas, and how do you confirm the diagnosis on physical exam?

**A:** Spider angiomas are associated with hyperestrogenic states (liver disease, pregnancy, OCPs). Applying a paperclip tip to the centre blanches the lesion.

Spider angiomas have a central arteriole with slender radiating branches. While they can be associated with liver disease or pregnancy, they are more often idiopathic. Blanching with central pressure confirms the diagnosis.

**Q:** A patient presents with localized lipid deposits manifesting as papules, plaques, or nodules in the skin. What investigation should be ordered?

**A:** Fasting lipid panel (to assess for hyperlipidemia) and biopsy (shave, punch, or excisional).

Xanthomas can be associated with hyperlipidemia (macrophage uptake of oxidized LDL) or with monoclonal gammopathy/multiple myeloma. A fasting lipid panel is essential except for verruciform xanthomas, which are not associated with hyperlipidemia.

**Q:** What is the classification system for acne vulgaris severity, and what is the backbone of topical acne therapy?

**A:** Type I: comedonal, sparse, no scarring. Type II: comedonal, papular, moderate. Type III: comedonal, papular, pustular with scarring. Type IV: nodulocystic, risk of severe scarring. Topical retinoids are the backbone of therapy.

All acne regimens should include a retinoid unless the patient cannot tolerate it. Benzoyl peroxide helps prevent Cutibacterium acnes resistance. The pathophysiology involves follicular hyperkeratinization, excess sebum production, and C. acnes colonization.

**Q:** What are the absolute contraindications for isotretinoin, and what monitoring is required before starting treatment?

**A:** Pregnancy is the absolute contraindication (teratogenic — requires 2 forms of contraception). Baseline monitoring includes lipid profile (risk of hypertriglyceridemia), LFTs, and β-hCG.

Isotretinoin is associated with spontaneous abortion and major birth defects including facial dysmorphism and cognitive impairment. Most adverse effects are temporary. Patients may experience transient acne exacerbation before improvement.

**Q:** How is rosacea differentiated from acne vulgaris?

**A:** Rosacea lacks comedones, has a predilection for the central face, and presents with symptoms of flushing. It features dome-shaped inflammatory papules, pustules, non-transient erythema, and telangiectasia.

Rosacea affects ages 30-50, F>M, and is most prevalent in fair-skinned people. Exacerbating factors include heat, cold, wind, sun, stress, hot liquids, alcohol, and spices. Phyma (rhinophyma) can develop in longstanding cases.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of contact dermatitis and how do irritant and allergic types differ?

**A:** Irritant: toxic injury to skin via non-immune mechanism (erythema, dryness, burning). Allergic: Type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction (erythema with papulovesicular eruption, pruritus). Irritant is more common; allergic persists indefinitely once sensitized.

Hands are the most common site for irritant contact dermatitis. Patch testing is used to identify specific allergens in allergic contact dermatitis. Common allergens include nickel, fragrance, and tattoo dyes.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis and what genetic mutation is associated?

**A:** T-cell driven inflammatory process with epidermal barrier dysfunction. Associated with filaggrin gene (FLG) mutation causing impaired skin barrier function and prolonged dryness.

Atopic dermatitis affects 10-20% of children under 5 in developed countries and is associated with personal/family history of atopy (asthma, allergic rhinitis). Distribution varies by age: face/extensors in infants, flexural in children, and hands/face in adults.

**Q:** What are the advantages of topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) over topical corticosteroids in atopic dermatitis?

**A:** No skin atrophy; safe for sensitive areas such as the face and neck. Can be used as steroid-sparing agents for long-term use.

Tacrolimus ointment (0.03% and 0.1%) and pimecrolimus cream (1%) can be used for acute treatment and long-term maintenance. The main side effect is burning sensation, which decreases with continued use. Health Canada recently removed prior black box warnings.

**Q:** What is the most important therapy for stasis dermatitis, and what is the classic location?

**A:** Compression stockings (if no peripheral arterial disease). Classic location is the medial ankle with erythematous, scaly, pruritic plaques on lower legs.

Stasis dermatitis results from chronic venous insufficiency leading to venous stasis and surrounding soft tissue inflammation. Late stages show brown hemosiderin deposition, woody fibrosis, and lipodermatosclerosis. Ulceration commonly occurs at the medial malleolus.

**A:** Purple, Pruritic, Polygonal, Planar (flat-topped), Peripheral (wrists), Papules.

**A:** Pityriasis rosea. No treatment required — clears spontaneously in 6-12 weeks. TCS if pruritic.

**Q:** What is the Auspitz sign and what condition is it associated with?

**A:** Pinpoint bleeding observed when scale is removed from a psoriatic plaque. It is associated with plaque psoriasis.

Plaque psoriasis is characterized by well-circumscribed erythematous papules/plaques with silvery-white scales on extensor surfaces, scalp, trunk, and nails. The shortened keratinocyte cell cycle correlates with Th1- and Th17-mediated inflammatory response.

**Q:** What percentage of psoriasis patients develop psoriatic arthritis, and what features increase risk?

**A:** 20-30% of patients with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis. Nail or scalp involvement increases risk.

Psoriatic arthritis is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy. Psoriasis has polygenic inheritance with 8% risk if one parent is affected and 41% if both parents are affected. Risk factors include smoking, obesity, alcohol, drugs, infections, and physical trauma.

**Q:** Differentiate bullous pemphigoid from pemphigus vulgaris by lesion type, depth, and antibody target.

**A:** Bullous pemphigoid: tense subepidermal bullae, IgG against BP180/BP230 (hemidesmosomes), age 60-80. Pemphigus vulgaris: flaccid intraepidermal bullae, IgG against desmoglein-1 and -3, age 15-50. Mnemonic: 'PemphigoiD = Deeper; Vulgaris = superficial flaccid.'

Bullous pemphigoid heals without scarring and is rarely fatal. Pemphigus vulgaris may be fatal without immunosuppressive treatment. Nikolsky's sign (epidermal detachment with shear stress) is positive in pemphigus vulgaris.

**Q:** What is the BSA classification for SJS vs SJS/TEN overlap vs TEN?

**A:** SJS: <10% BSA epidermal detachment. SJS/TEN overlap: 10-30%. TEN: >30%. SJS mortality is ~5%; TEN mortality is ~30%.

Common causative agents include allopurinol, anti-epileptics, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, and cephalosporins. HLA-B1502 (East Asians, carbamazepine) and HLA-B5801 (allopurinol) are risk factors. Management includes drug discontinuation, ICU/burn unit care, and IVIg or cyclosporine.

**Q:** What are the SCORTEN criteria used for in TEN, and what scores predict mortality?

**A:** SCORTEN predicts TEN mortality. One point each for: age ≥40, malignancy, initial BSA detached ≥10%, tachycardia ≥120, serum urea >10, glucose >14, bicarbonate <20. Score 0-1: 94% survival; 2: 87%; 3: 53%; 4: 25%; ≥5: 17%.

SCORTEN is calculated at admission and determines appropriate clinical setting. Higher scores indicate need for ICU/burn unit care. Treatment includes IV fluids, electrolyte replacement, wound care, and potentially IVIg, cyclosporine, or corticosteroids.

**A:** Thyroid disease, pernicious anemia, Addison's disease, and type 1 diabetes mellitus.

Vitiligo is an acquired autoimmune destruction of melanocytes with 1% incidence. It presents with sharply demarcated white patches on extensor surfaces and periorificial areas. Wood's lamp illuminates amelanotic patches. Treatment includes topical calcineurin inhibitors, TCS, and phototherapy.

Primary syphilis is treated with penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose. VDRL is negative in primary, positive in secondary. FTA-ABS has greater sensitivity for early disease. Dark field examination can detect spirochetes in chancre fluid.

**Q:** What is the ABCDE mnemonic for melanoma and what are the clinical subtypes from most to least common?

**A:** A=Asymmetry, B=Border irregular, C=Colour varied, D=Diameter >6mm, E=Evolution. Subtypes: superficial spreading (60-70%), nodular (15-30%), lentigo maligna (5-15%), acral lentiginous (5-10%).

About 2/3 of melanomas arise de novo. Nodular melanoma is rapidly fatal with primarily vertical growth. Acral lentiginous is the most common subtype in patients with Type 6 skin. Breslow depth determines excision margins and staging.

**Q:** What is the most common malignancy in humans, and what is the typical cure rate when treated early?

**A:** Basal cell carcinoma (BCC). 95% cure rate if the lesion is <2cm or treated early.

BCC is a malignant proliferation of basal keratinocytes with primarily tangential growth and rare metastasis. >80% occur on sun-exposed sites due to UVB exposure. Subtypes include nodular (most common), pigmented, superficial, and morpheaform.

**Q:** What is the most common cutaneous malignancy in immunocompromised patients such as organ transplant recipients?

**A:** Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), with increased mortality compared to immunocompetent patients.

SCC is a malignant neoplasm of keratinocytes with primarily vertical growth. Rate of metastasis from primary SCC is 2-5%. Higher risk factors for metastasis include diameter >2cm, depth >2mm, recurrence, and immunosuppression.

**Q:** What is hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) and what is the follicular occlusion tetrad?

**A:** HS is a chronic scarring inflammatory condition of intertriginous areas due to follicular occlusion. The follicular occlusion tetrad includes: HS, cicatricial alopecia, acne conglobata, and pilonidal disease.

HS affects 1-4% of the population, F>M, onset between puberty and age 40. Axillae are the most common site. It is associated with smoking and excess weight. Moderate-to-severe disease may require biologic therapy such as adalimumab.

**Q:** What are the dermatologic manifestations of dermatomyositis?

**A:** Heliotrope rash (periorbital violaceous erythema with edema), Gottron's papules (violaceous flat-topped papules over knuckles with atrophy), periungual erythema, telangiectasia, calcinosis cutis.

Dermatomyositis is an autoimmune condition that can be a paraneoplastic syndrome associated with breast, lung, or ovarian cancer. The heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules are pathognomonic. Skin manifestations are part of the systemic disease involving proximal muscle weakness.

**Q:** What skin finding is a cutaneous marker of insulin resistance?

**A:** Acanthosis nigricans — thickened, velvety, hyperpigmented skin most commonly in the peri-nuchal (posterior neck) area and flexural regions.

Acanthosis nigricans is associated with diabetes mellitus, obesity, other endocrine disorders, and malignancy. It can also be seen in polycystic ovary syndrome. When associated with malignancy, it may appear more extensive and involve unusual locations.

**Q:** What is the erythema nodosum DDx mnemonic (NOSUMM)?

**A:** No cause identified (40% idiopathic), OCPs/pregnancy, Sarcoidosis, Ulcerative colitis/Crohn's, Malignancy (leukemia, Hodgkin's), Many infections (GAS, TB, histoplasmosis, Yersinia).

Erythema nodosum presents as round, red, tender, poorly demarcated nodules on the extensor lower legs (shins). It is an acute/chronic inflammation of subcutaneous fat (panniculitis). Affects ages 15-30, F:M 3:1. Resolves spontaneously in 6 weeks.
