# General Medicine

## General

**Q:** How does the duration of action compare between naloxone and naltrexone?

**A:** Naloxone is short-acting (t½ = 1 hour); naltrexone is longer-acting (t½ = 10 hours)

This significant difference in half-life affects clinical use—naloxone for acute overdose reversal, naltrexone for maintenance therapy.

**Q:** What is the clinical definition of chronic pain?

**A:** Pain that persists or recurs for longer than 3 months, or pain of duration/intensity beyond normal tissue healing that adversely affects functioning

Chronic pain differs from acute pain in duration and often involves CNS changes like central sensitization.

**Q:** What is the definition and primary predictor of chronic post-surgical pain?

**A:** Pain developing after surgery persisting ≥2 months; primary predictor is history of chronic pain; other risk factors include female gender, surgical approach, poor social supports, catastrophizing

Understanding risk factors allows identification of high-risk patients who may benefit from preventive analgesia strategies.

**Q:** What were the key findings of the Cochrane review comparing PCA vs. non-patient controlled opioid analgesia?

**A:** PCA had lower VAS pain scores over first 48 hours, higher patient satisfaction, higher opioid consumption, and higher incidence of pruritus—but not other adverse events

The review (49 studies, 1725 PCA vs 1687 control patients) found moderate-to-low quality evidence supporting PCA efficacy.

**Q:** What are the nociceptive pathways involved in labour pain at different stages?

**A:** Early labour: cervical dilation and effacement stimulates visceral nerve fibers entering spinal cord at T10-L1; Late labour/delivery: distention of lower vagina and perineum causes somatic nociceptive impulses via pudendal nerve at S2-S4

This dual innervation explains why different analgesic techniques may be more effective at different stages of labour.

**Q:** What does each component of the PQRSTU approach represent on an ECG?

**A:** P wave = atrial contraction; P-R interval = conduction from SA to AV node; QRS complex = ventricular depolarization; ST segment = completion of ventricular depolarization; T wave = ventricular repolarization; U wave = repolarization of Purkinje fibers/myocardium

The PQRSTU approach organizes ECG interpretation by anatomical correlates of cardiac electrical activity.

**Q:** What are the normal characteristics of a P wave in leads II and V1?

**A:** Lead II: rounded, <120 msec duration, <2.5 mm height; Lead V1: biphasic with positive then negative phase

Normal P wave characteristics help identify atrial abnormalities and arrhythmias.

**Q:** What ECG findings characterize atrial flutter?

**A:** Sawtooth P waves with continuous atrial activity at 300 bpm. Visualized best by flipping ECG upside-down and checking inferior leads (II, III, aVF)

Atrial flutter creates a characteristic sawtooth pattern due to the atrial macroreentry circuit.

**Q:** What ECG finding indicates right atrial enlargement (RAE) and what is it called?

**A:** Tall P wave (>2.5 mm) in lead II or V1; called P pulmonale

RAE causes increased P wave amplitude due to delayed right atrial depolarization, often from pulmonary disease.

**Q:** What ECG findings indicate left atrial enlargement (LAE)?

**A:** Biphasic P wave with negative deflection >1 mm deep or >1 mm wide in V1; wide (>100 msec) notched P wave in lead II

LAE produces characteristic P wave changes reflecting prolonged left atrial conduction time.

**Q:** What is the normal P-R interval on ECG and what does it represent?

**A:** Normal P-R interval is 120-200 msec, representing time from sinus node activation to start of ventricular depolarization (atrial conduction, AV node, bundle of His)

The P-R interval reflects conduction through the atria, AV node, and His-Purkinje system.

**Q:** What is dromotropy in the context of ECG interpretation?

**A:** Dromotropy refers to conduction velocity through the AV node. Positive dromotropy = faster conduction (e.g., sympathetic stimulation); Negative dromotropy = slower conduction (e.g., vagal stimulation)

Understanding dromotropy helps explain P-R interval changes with autonomic nervous system activity.

**Q:** What ECG finding suggests pre-excitation syndrome and what causes it?

**A:** Short P-R interval (<120 msec) with a delta wave (upsloping of the first part of the QRS complex); caused by accessory pathways (e.g., Bundle of Kent in WPW)

Pre-excitation allows electrical impulses to bypass the AV node, shortening the P-R interval.

**Q:** What defines a pathological Q wave indicating myocardial necrosis?

**A:** Q wave >40 msec duration OR >1/3 of total QRS amplitude

Pathological Q waves indicate prior or current myocardial infarction with tissue necrosis.

**Q:** What is the normal position of the ST segment on ECG?

**A:** The ST segment should be at the same level as the baseline (T-P segment)

Deviation of the ST segment from baseline is a key finding in ischemia and infarction.

**Q:** In which leads is the T wave typically negative under normal conditions?

**A:** Lead aVR and V1 normally have negative T waves; all other leads should be positive

Normal T wave polarity follows repolarization patterns, with aVR being the main exception.

**Q:** What is T wave alternans and what is its clinical significance?

**A:** Beat-to-beat variations in T wave morphology, often due to PVC overlap (R on T phenomenon). Can precipitate VT or VFib

T wave alternans is an electrical instability marker associated with sudden cardiac death risk.

**Q:** What are normal QTc values and how is QTc calculated?

**A:** Normal QTc: males 360-450 msec, females 360-460 msec. QTc = QT / √(RR) using Bazett's formula (NOTE: inaccurate at rapid rates >100 bpm)

QTc corrects the QT interval for heart rate, allowing comparison across different rates.

**Q:** What is the proposed origin of the U wave on ECG?

**A:** Repolarization of Purkinje fibers OR delayed/prolonged repolarization of the myocardium

The exact origin of U waves remains debated but may represent late repolarization events.

**Q:** Where do you measure ST changes relative to the J point?

**A:** ST changes are measured starting at 60 msec from the J point (junction between QRS complex and ST segment)

Standardized measurement from the J point ensures consistent interpretation of ST changes.

**Q:** What ECG findings suggest low atrial rhythm?

**A:** Short P-R interval (<120 msec) with inverted P waves in leads II, III, and aVF

Low atrial rhythm originates near the AV node, causing retrograde atrial activation.

**Q:** How does volume overload (e.g., aortic insufficiency) lead to cardiac remodeling?

**A:** Volume overload causes ventricular dilatation (eccentric remodeling)

Increased preload stretches the myocardium, leading to chamber enlargement while maintaining cardiac output initially.

**Q:** What is pulsus alternans and what does it indicate?

**A:** Alternating strong and weak pulses with regular rhythm; indicates severe left ventricular dysfunction

Pulsus alternans reflects beat-to-beat variation in stroke volume due to impaired myocardial contractility in advanced heart failure.

**Q:** What is Kussmaul's sign and what does it indicate?

**A:** JVP rises with inspiration instead of falling; indicates right ventricular failure or constrictive pericarditis

Normally JVP falls with inspiration due to increased venous return to the right heart; failure of this response suggests RV dysfunction.

**Q:** What were the key findings of the LEADER trial regarding liraglutide in cardiovascular outcomes?

**A:** Liraglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in T2DM patients regardless of HF history (HR:0.89 with HF; HR:0.83 without HF; P=0.63); no increased HF hospitalization risk

The LEADER trial demonstrated cardiovascular benefit of liraglutide in high-risk diabetic patients, with consistent effects regardless of baseline heart failure status.

**Q:** What is pulsus alternans?

**A:** Alternating strong and weak pulses with regular rhythm, indicating severe left ventricular dysfunction

Pulsus alternans reflects beat-to-beat variation in stroke volume due to impaired ventricular contractility and is a sign of advanced systolic heart failure.

**Q:** What is the median survival after cardiac transplantation and how does it differ for 1-year survivors?

**A:** Median survival is 12.5 years overall, extending to 14.8 years among 1-year survivors

Long-term survival data shows most deaths occur early; those surviving the first year have significantly better long-term outcomes.

**Q:** What is the gold standard for detecting cardiac transplant rejection?

**A:** Endomyocardial biopsy

Endomyocardial biopsy allows direct histological examination of myocardial tissue to identify rejection episodes.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of LATE death following cardiac transplantation?

**A:** Allograft vasculopathy - approximately 50% develop graft vasculopathy within 10 years

Progressive coronary artery disease in the donor heart develops due to chronic immune-mediated injury and is a major long-term concern.

**Q:** What malignancy is most commonly associated with cardiac transplant immunosuppression?

**A:** Cutaneous neoplasms (most common), followed by non-Hodgkin lymphoma and lung cancer; overall 15% develop malignancy

Chronic immunosuppression increases risk of skin cancers and lymphoproliferative disorders.

**Q:** What are the three main indications for ventricular assist devices (VAD)?

**A:** Bridge to transplantation, bridge to decision (for transplant evaluation), or long-term permanent therapy ('destination therapy')

VADs provide mechanical circulatory support across different clinical scenarios in advanced heart failure.

**Q:** How does an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) work mechanically?

**A:** IABP is catheter-based inserted via femoral artery to descending aorta. The balloon inflates during diastole to increase ascending aorta and coronary artery blood flow, thereby decreasing afterload and myocardial O2 demand

Counterpulsation is the fundamental mechanism - diastolic inflation augments coronary perfusion while reducing systolic afterload.

**Q:** Which Canadian guidelines address fitness to drive after LVAD implantation?

**A:** Canadian Cardiovascular Society Focused Position Statement Update on Assessment of the Cardiac Patient for Fitness to Drive (Can J Cardiol 2012;28:137-140) - patients with continuous-flow LVAD who are stable 2 months post-implantation qualify for private passenger vehicle driving

LVAD patients can typically resume private driving after demonstrating clinical stability, though commercial driving requires additional assessment.

**Q:** What are the selection criteria recommendations from the Canadian Cardiovascular Society/Canadian Cardiac Transplant Network 2020 position statement?

**A:** Cardiac transplantation recommended for HF patients <70 years; frailty assessment with validated tool recommended for all candidates; LVAD implantation recommended for eligible patients with pulmonary HTN on right heart catheterization; NOT recommended for non-adherent patients, those with alcohol/illicit drug use, mental illness affecting compliance

The 2020 statement provides updated Canadian-specific guidance emphasizing frailty assessment and strict adherence requirements.

**Q:** What are the components of a typical VAD circuit?

**A:** A pump (implantable), an outflow graft, and a driveline to connect to an external power source and controller

VAD systems require both implanted and external components to deliver mechanical circulatory support.

**Q:** When is postoperative mechanical VAD support indicated?

**A:** When unable to separate from cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) despite inotropic and IABP support

Post-cardiotomy shock requiring escalating mechanical support represents an important bridge-to-recovery indication for VAD therapy.

**Q:** What is the typical configuration of VAD support for left, right, or biventricular failure?

**A:** LVAD for left ventricular support, RVAD for right ventricular support, or BiVAD for biventricular support

Different configurations address failure of specific cardiac chambers, with biventricular support reserved for the most severe cases.

**Q:** What is the median survival after cardiac transplantation and how does it change for 1-year survivors?

**A:** Median survival is 12.5 years, extending to 14.8 years among 1-year survivors

Long-term survival data from cardiac transplant recipients shows that surviving the first year significantly improves prognosis, with median survival approaching 15 years.

**Q:** What is the gold standard test to detect cardiac transplant rejection?

**A:** Endomyocardial biopsy

Serial endomyocardial biopsies allow direct histological assessment of rejection, typically graded using the ISHLT classification system.

**Q:** When is the risk of acute rejection greatest after cardiac transplantation?

**A:** During the first 3 months after transplant

The initial post-transplant period has the highest rejection risk before immunosuppression achieves adequate therapeutic levels and tolerance develops.

**Q:** What percentage of cardiac transplant recipients develop graft vasculopathy within 10 years?

**A:** Approximately 50%

Allograft vasculopathy is a form of accelerated atherosclerosis that affects coronary arteries in the transplanted heart, representing chronic rejection.

**Q:** What malignancy is most common in cardiac transplant recipients and why?

**A:** Cutaneous neoplasms (most common), followed by non-Hodgkin lymphoma and lung cancer, due to immunosuppressive medication

Chronic immunosuppression impairs immune surveillance against malignant cells, particularly affecting skin and lymphoid tissue.

**Q:** Why does a transplanted heart not respond to anticholinergics like atropine?

**A:** The donor heart is completely denervated and does not receive parasympathetic vagal stimulation or intrinsic postganglionic sympathetic stimulation

Denervation occurs during organ harvest and reinnervation does not reliably occur, so the heart rate is controlled solely by circulating catecholamines.

**Q:** How does an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) decrease myocardial oxygen demand?

**A:** The balloon inflates during diastole to increase ascending aorta and coronary artery pressure, then deflates during systole to decrease afterload

Counterpulsation reduces left ventricular workload by decreasing systolic pressure while enhancing coronary perfusion during diastole.

**Q:** What percentage of patients were discharged with the device in-place comparing continuous-flow vs pulsatile-flow LVAD?

**A:** 86% of continuous-flow patients and 6% of pulsatile-flow patients

The dramatic difference in discharge rates reflects the superior durability and lower complication profile of continuous-flow devices.

**Q:** What is the second most common cause of late death following cardiac transplantation?

**A:** Malignancy

Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder and solid tumors develop due to impaired immune surveillance from chronic immunosuppression.

**Q:** What percentage of cardiac transplant recipients develop malignancy due to immunosuppressive medication?

**A:** 15%

Regular skin examinations and cancer screening are essential components of long-term post-transplant care.

**Q:** What is the recommended age cutoff for cardiac transplant consideration and what frailty assessment is recommended?

**A:** Consideration for patients <70 years; all patients being considered should have frailty assessed with a validated tool

Frailty assessment helps identify patients who may not tolerate the physiological stress of transplant surgery and immunosuppression.

**Q:** What is the median survival after cardiac transplantation and how does it extend for 1-year survivors?

**A:** Median survival is 12.5 years, extending to 14.8 years among 1-year survivors

Long-term outcomes after cardiac transplantation are favorable for patients who survive the first year, with significantly improved survival rates compared to those who don't reach the 1-year mark.

**Q:** What are the three functions of ventricular assist devices (VADs)?

**A:** Bridge to transplantation, bridge to decision (for transplant evaluation), or destination therapy (long-term permanent support)

VADs provide mechanical circulatory support for different clinical scenarios, from temporary support while awaiting transplant to permanent support in patients ineligible for transplantation.

**Q:** How does an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) work to decrease afterload?

**A:** Inflation of the balloon occurs during diastole to increase ascending aorta and coronary artery blood flow, which decreases afterload, reduces myocardial O2 demand, and increases coronary perfusion

Counterpulsation with IABP is the most common temporary mechanical circulatory support, providing hemodynamic benefits through diastolic augmentation.

**Q:** Can LVAD-supported patients with stable NYHA class achieve fitness to drive?

**A:** Stable patients 2 months post-LVAD implantation may qualify for private driving; commercial driving is generally not recommended

Canadian Cardiovascular Society guidelines address driving restrictions for LVAD patients, balancing patient independence with road safety concerns.

**Q:** What is the recommended evaluation for frailty in patients being considered for cardiac transplantation or LVAD?

**A:** Assessment of frailty with a validated tool is recommended for all patients being considered for cardiac transplantation

Frailty assessment helps identify patients at higher risk for poor outcomes and guides shared decision-making regarding transplant or mechanical circulatory support.

**Q:** What are the nephrotoxic effects of common post-transplant immunosuppressive medications?

**A:** Immunosuppressives such as prednisone, cyclosporine, and tacrolimus may have nephrotoxic effects

Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine and tacrolimus) are cornerstone immunosuppressants but carry significant nephrotoxic risk, potentially causing chronic kidney disease.

**Q:** What is the classification for cardiac conduction system anatomy?

**A:** SA node, AV node, bundle of His, bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers

Anderson et al. described the anatomy of the cardiac conduction system including the sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node, and distal conduction pathways.

**A:** Transcatheter valve-in-valve approach is an alternative to surgical re-replacement

Massas et al. compared transcatheter versus surgical valve replacement for failed pulmonary homografts in the Ross population.

**Q:** What diagnostic modalities are covered under the 2011 ACCF/AHA Appropriate Use Criteria for Echocardiography?

**A:** Transthoracic, transesophageal, and stress echocardiography

The 2011 Appropriate Use Criteria document provides guidance on when echocardiography should be used based on clinical scenarios.

**Q:** Define drug efficacy and how it is measured.

**A:** The relative ability of a drug to produce a maximal response; measured by Emax (the maximal response a drug can elicit).

Efficacy represents the ceiling effect of a drug—the maximum biological or biochemical effect achievable under ideal conditions.

**Q:** Define drug potency and how it is measured.

**A:** The concentration of drug needed to produce 50% of Emax; measured by EC50 (or ED50 if dose is used). A drug with a lower EC50 is more potent.

Potency is distinct from efficacy—a drug may be more potent but less efficacious depending on where their dose-response curves plateau.

**Q:** What is the difference between affinity and efficacy for agonists?

**A:** Affinity is the ability of the agonist to bind to the receptor; efficacy is the ability to elicit a response once bound.

A drug like salbutamol has greater affinity for β2-receptors than β1-receptors, allowing preferential binding, but its efficacy depends on the downstream signal it produces.

**Q:** How does competitive (reversible) antagonism differ from non-competitive antagonism?

**A:** Competitive antagonism: antagonist binds to the active site, increasing agonist concentration can overcome the blockade (e.g., naloxone vs. morphine). Non-competitive antagonism: antagonist binds to an allosteric site, producing effects that reduce agonist binding; cannot be overcome by increasing agonist concentration.

Reversible competitive antagonists bind non-covalently, allowing competitive displacement, while non-competitive antagonists cause insurmountable blockade through allosteric mechanisms.

**Q:** What clinical scenario would prompt use of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)?

**A:** TDM is most useful when steady-state concentration has been reached, for drugs with narrow therapeutic indices, and particularly for drugs with non-linear kinetics (e.g., phenytoin, theophylline) where drug-specific nomograms are required.

TDM helps optimize dosing for drugs where the relationship between dose and concentration is complex or where small concentration changes cause significant clinical effects.

**Q:** A drug with EC50 of 2mg is compared to a drug with EC50 of 5mg. Which is more potent and why?

**A:** The drug with EC50 of 2mg is more potent because it requires a lower concentration to produce 50% of its maximal effect.

Lower EC50 indicates higher potency—a drug reaches its half-maximal effect at a lower concentration, though this does not necessarily mean greater efficacy (Emax).

**Q:** What is the difference between efficacy and potency?

**A:** Efficacy is the ability of a drug to produce a maximal biological response (measured by Emax). Potency is the concentration of drug needed to produce 50% of Emax (measured by EC50/ED50). A drug with lower EC50 is more potent.

Drug A with EC50 lower than Drug B means A is more potent. However, potency and efficacy are independent properties — a drug can be highly potent but have lower efficacy than a less potent drug.

**Q:** What is competitive antagonism and how does it differ from non-competitive antagonism?

**A:** Competitive antagonists bind to the active site on a receptor, preventing agonist binding without activating the receptor. In reversible competitive antagonism, increased agonist concentration can overcome the antagonist (e.g., naloxone vs. morphine). Non-competitive antagonists bind to an allosteric site distinct from the active site and reduce agonist binding through allosteric effects.

Reversible competitive antagonism produces parallel rightward shifts of the dose-response curve that can be overcome with increasing agonist concentration, while non-competitive antagonism produces a decrease in maximal effect.

**Q:** What is the graded dose-response relationship?

**A:** The graded dose-response relationship relates the dose (or concentration) of a drug to the intensity of the effect it produces in an individual, typically plotted as log(dose) vs. response on a sigmoidal curve.

From this curve, key parameters are derived including ED50 (dose producing 50% of maximal effect), EC50 (concentration producing 50% of Emax), and Emax (maximal achievable effect).

**Q:** Which drugs commonly exhibit zero-order kinetics in the therapeutic range and are targets for therapeutic drug monitoring?

**A:** Phenytoin, theophylline, and aspirin (at high doses) exhibit zero-order kinetics and are common targets for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). Drug-specific nomograms exist to guide dosing.

The complexity of dosing drugs with non-linear kinetics — where small dose changes can produce disproportionate changes in concentration — has led to the creation of specialized clinical nomograms for drugs like phenytoin and theophylline.

**Q:** What is the difference between drug efficacy and drug effectiveness?

**A:** Drug efficacy is the ability of a drug to produce a maximal response under ideal/controlled conditions. Drug effectiveness is a better measure of how the drug works in real-world clinical situations, accounting for factors like patient adherence, variable absorption, and disease state.

High efficacy in clinical trials does not always translate to high effectiveness in practice. Effectiveness incorporates practical real-world factors that may limit the drug's performance compared to its theoretical maximum.

**Q:** What drugs exhibit zero-order (non-linear) kinetics at therapeutic concentrations?

**A:** Alcohol, phenytoin, and aspirin (more commonly associated with overdose)

In zero-order kinetics, a constant amount of drug is eliminated per unit time regardless of concentration, and the half-life concept does not apply.

**Q:** Why does phenytoin follow non-linear kinetics?

**A:** Saturation of various ADME processes creates non-linear kinetics, with first-order at lower concentrations and zero-order at higher concentrations after saturation

This complexity has led to drug-specific nomograms for dosing, and phenytoin is a common target for therapeutic drug monitoring.

**Q:** How many half-lives are required to achieve steady-state levels?

**A:** Approximately 4-5 half-lives

This is a fundamental principle for predicting when therapeutic drug concentrations will be reached.

**Q:** What is EC50 and what does it measure?

**A:** EC50 is the concentration of a drug needed to produce 50% of its maximal effect (Emax); it measures drug potency

A drug with a lower EC50 is more potent, as it achieves 50% of maximal effect at a lower concentration.

**Q:** What is competitive antagonism and how can it be overcome?

**A:** Antagonist binds directly to the active site on the receptor (zero efficacy) and prevents agonist binding; reversible competitive antagonists can be overcome by increasing agonist concentration

Example: naloxone is a reversible competitive antagonist to morphine/heroin at opioid receptors.

**Q:** What is the difference between reversible and irreversible competitive antagonists?

**A:** Reversible antagonists bind non-covalently and can be displaced by increased agonist; irreversible antagonists form covalent bonds and cannot be displaced

Irreversible antagonists permanently block receptors, so no amount of agonist can overcome the blockade.

**Q:** What is non-competitive antagonism?

**A:** Antagonist (negative allosteric modulator) binds to an alternate site distinct from the active site, producing allosteric effects that reduce agonist binding

Example: phenoxybenzamine forms a covalent bond with adrenergic receptors, preventing adrenaline and norepinephrine from binding.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of naloxone?

**A:** Naloxone is a reversible competitive antagonist at opioid receptors (μ, κ, δ), displacing morphine and heroin from binding sites

Used clinically to reverse opioid overdose, naloxone's competitive nature means higher doses may be needed with potent opioids.

**Q:** Why are drugs with non-linear kinetics often targeted for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)?

**A:** The complexity of dosing drugs with non-linear kinetics requires drug-specific nomograms; small dose increases can cause disproportionate changes in plasma concentration

Examples include phenytoin and theophylline, where linear dosing assumptions lead to toxicity.

**Q:** What is the difference between ED50 and EC50?

**A:** ED50 uses dose to measure potency (dose required for 50% of maximal effect); EC50 uses concentration (concentration required for 50% of Emax)

Both measure potency but in different units—ED50 in dose (mg/kg) and EC50 in concentration (M or μg/mL).

**Q:** Which drugs follow zero-order (non-linear) kinetics in the therapeutic range?

**A:** Alcohol, phenytoin, and aspirin follow zero-order kinetics in the therapeutic range. Zero-order kinetics is more commonly associated with overdose.

In zero-order kinetics, a constant amount of drug is eliminated per unit time regardless of concentration. The concept of half-life does not apply.

**Q:** What is the key difference between reversible and irreversible competitive antagonists?

**A:** Reversible competitive antagonists bind non-covalently to the receptor active site, so increasing concentrations of agonist can overcome the antagonist (e.g., naloxone vs. morphine). Irreversible competitive antagonists form a covalent bond with the receptor and cannot be displaced.

The reversibility of antagonism has important clinical implications - reversible antagonists can be overcome by dose escalation, while irreversible antagonists permanently block the receptor.

**Q:** What is therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) and when is it most useful?

**A:** TDM is most useful when steady-state concentration has been reached. It is particularly important for drugs with non-linear kinetics (e.g., phenytoin, theophylline) where drug-specific nomograms are used to aid clinicians in dosing.

Drugs with narrow therapeutic indices or non-linear kinetics are common targets for TDM because small dose changes can lead to disproportionate changes in plasma concentration.

**Q:** What are the three main classification levels used in clinical practice guidelines?

These classification levels help clinicians understand the strength of recommendation and evidence quality supporting each guideline statement.

Pericardial disease guidelines address diagnostic criteria and treatment algorithms for both inflammatory and effusive conditions.

**Q:** What is Nikolsky's sign and what condition is it associated with?

**Q:** What are the 4 criteria for diagnosing a drug reaction (Naranjo scale)?

**A:** 1) Temporal relationship between drug exposure and reaction, 2) Recognized response to suspected drug, 3) Improvement after drug withdrawal, 4) Recurrence of reaction on re-challenge. All 4 = definite; #1-3 = probable; only #1 = possible.

The Naranjo algorithm provides a standardized method to assess causality in adverse drug reactions, with re-challenge being the most definitive but rarely performed.

**Q:** What is the mortality rate of untreated severe DRESS syndrome?

**Q:** What neurological association exists with Bullous Pemphigoid?

**A:** One or more neurologic disorders are present in 22-46% of cases of bullous pemphigoid.

The shared basement membrane antigens between skin and brain may explain this association, suggesting a possible common autoimmune pathogenesis.

**Q:** What is the difference between Pemphigus Vulgaris and Pemphigus Foliaceus?

**A:** Pemphigus Foliaceus is more superficial, caused by antibodies against desmoglein-1 only, appears as crusted patches/erosions rather than flaccid bullae, and can be managed with topical steroids in localized disease.

The more superficial split in pemphigus foliaceus means blistering is less clinically apparent, and the disease tends to be less severe than pemphigus vulgaris.

**Q:** What is the age range and gender distribution for Bullous Pemphigoid?

**A:** Mean age of onset: 60-80 years. Female equals male (F=M).

Bullous pemphigoid predominantly affects elderly patients, reflecting the age-related changes in immune regulation.

**Q:** What is the prognosis of Bullous Pemphigoid and Pemphigus Vulgaris?

**A:** Pemphigoid: heals without scarring, usually chronic, rarely fatal. Pemphigus Vulgaris: heals with hyperpigmentation but no scarring, may be fatal unless treated with immunosuppression.

The fundamentally different depths of blistering and immune mechanisms result in markedly different outcomes between these two conditions.

**Q:** What common sites are affected in Bullous Pemphigoid?

**A:** Flexor aspect of forearms, axillae, medial thighs, groin, abdomen, and mouth (in approximately 33% of cases).

The distribution reflects areas of friction and skin movement, though any body surface may be involved in extensive disease.

**Q:** What investigations are done for Pemphigus Vulgaris?

**A:** Immunofluorescence showing intraepidermal IgG and C3 deposition. Circulating serum anti-desmoglein IgG antibodies (usually anti-desmoglein-3 first, then anti-desmoglein-1).

These serological and histological tests confirm the diagnosis and help differentiate from other blistering disorders.

The timing helps distinguish urticaria types and may indicate whether the mechanism is immediate hypersensitivity or serum sickness-like.

Uncomplicated pyelonephritis can often be treated with oral fluoroquinolones; IV therapy is indicated for patients requiring hospitalization.

**Q:** What are options for recurrent/cystitis prophylactic treatment?

**A:** Continuous: TMP-SMX 40 mg/200 mg PO daily or 3x/week for 6-12 months, or nitrofurantoin 50-100 mg PO daily for 6-12 months. Post-coital: TMP-SMX 40-80 mg/200-400 mg or nitrofurantoin 50-100 mg within 2 hours of coitus

Prophylaxis is considered after 2-3 documented episodes of recurrent cystitis.

**Q:** What is retrograde pyelogram used for in hydronephrosis?

**A:** To delineate anatomy and allow for stent insertion to decompress if obstruction is present

Retrograde pyelography is both diagnostic and therapeutic, allowing visualization and simultaneous stent placement.

**Q:** What is hypospadias?

**A:** A condition where the urethral meatus opens on the ventral side of the penis, proximal to the normal location in the glans

Depending on severity, hypospadias may result in difficulty directing urinary stream, intercourse problems, or depositing sperm in the vagina.

**Q:** When should hypospadias be surgically corrected?

**A:** Early surgical correction before 2 years of age; neonatal circumcision should be deferred as the foreskin may be used in repair

Optimal repair timing is before 2 years old. The foreskin is often utilized in the correction, so circumcision should be deferred.

**Q:** What is the exstrophy-epispadias complex?

**A:** A spectrum of defects from failure of cloacal membrane closure: bladder exstrophy (exposed bladder), cloacal exstrophy (exposed bladder and bowel with imperforate anus), and epispadias (urethra on dorsal penis)

The severity depends on timing of cloacal membrane rupture. Associated with spina bifida in >50% of cloacal exstrophy cases.

**Q:** What is the treatment for Wilms' tumour?

**A:** Unilateral: radical nephrectomy or nephron-sparing surgery + radiation + chemotherapy. Bilateral: nephron-sparing surgery following neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Always investigate contralateral kidney and renal vein (for tumour thrombus) before treatment.

**Q:** What is the prognosis for Wilms' tumour?

**A:** 5-year survival: 90%; 5-year recurrence rate: 15%

Wilms' tumour has excellent prognosis with modern multimodality treatment.

**Q:** What is the treatment for cryptorchidism?

**A:** Orchiopexy (surgical correction); hormonal therapy has not proven benefit over standard surgical treatment

Surgical correction is the standard treatment. Timing is important for preserving fertility and reducing malignancy risk.

**Q:** What are the complications of untreated bilateral cryptorchidism?

**A:** Approximately 100% infertility due to Leydig and germ cell loss

Paternity rates are 33-65% for formerly bilateral cryptorchid, 90% for formerly unilateral cryptorchid, and 93% for normal men.

**Q:** What is 46,XY DSD?

**A:** Disorder in phenotypic males (46,XY) due to defect in testicular synthesis of androgens OR androgen resistance in target tissues

Clinical features include micropenis, undescended testes, hypospadias, and incomplete development of male secondary sexual characteristics.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of 46,XX DSD?

**A:** Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), specifically 21-hydroxylase deficiency, leading to excess androgens

Undiagnosed/untreated CAH can cause life-threatening electrolyte abnormalities (salt-wasting CAH) in newborns.

**Q:** What is ovotesticular DSD?

**A:** A form of DSD where both ovarian and testicular tissue are present in the same individual

Ovotesticular DSD is one of the classifications of disorders of sexual differentiation.

**Q:** In a newborn with bilateral non-palpable testicles, what DSD should be considered and what evaluation is required?

**A:** Consider 46,XX DSD with salt-wasting CAH; requires proper evaluation including karyotype and gonadal palpation before any shunt surgery

Bilateral non-palpable testicles in a newborn should raise suspicion for DSD and prompt comprehensive evaluation before surgical intervention.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for bilateral Wilms' tumour?

**A:** Nephron-sparing surgery following neoadjuvant chemotherapy to preserve renal function

Bilateral disease requires conservative approach to preserve as much renal tissue as possible.

**Q:** Does surgical correction of cryptorchidism reduce the risk of testicular torsion?

**A:** Yes, surgical correction reduces the increased risk of testicular torsion

Undescended testes have increased risk of torsion, which can be reduced by surgical correction (orchiopexy).
