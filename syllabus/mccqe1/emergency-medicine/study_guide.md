# EmergencyMedicine

## Resuscitation & Cardiac Arrest

**Q:** A 55-year-old collapses in the ED. He is pulseless with VF on monitor. What are the immediate steps?

**A:** Immediately: start CPR (30:2), defibrillate (200J biphasic) as soon as defibrillator available, call for help; continue CPR 2 min, then rhythm check — ACLS algorithm

AHA/ACLS guidelines (adopted by Canadian emergency medicine): high-quality CPR is the most important intervention. Minimize interruptions (<10 seconds). Defibrillation within 2 minutes for shockable rhythms (VF/pVT). After shock: immediately resume CPR for 2 min before rhythm check.

**Q:** What is the ACLS algorithm for VF/pulseless VT?

**A:** Defibrillate → CPR 2 min → rhythm check → if shockable: defibrillate → CPR 2 min → IV/IO epinephrine 1mg q3-5 min → after 3rd shock: amiodarone 300mg IV → continue cycle

ACLS 2020: epinephrine should be given as soon as IV/IO access is established (after 1st shock if available, or during CPR cycles). Amiodarone 300 mg after 3rd shock (second dose 150 mg), or lidocaine as alternative. Avoid prolonged interruptions.

**Q:** What is the ACLS algorithm for PEA (pulseless electrical activity) and asystole?

**A:** CPR + IV epinephrine 1mg q3-5 min (no shock — non-shockable); search for and treat reversible causes (5Hs + 5Ts)

5Hs: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ions (acidosis), Hypo/hyperkalemia, Hypothermia; 5Ts: Tamponade, Tension pneumothorax, Thrombosis (PE or MI), Toxins, Trauma — identification and treatment of reversible causes is the key to ROSC in PEA/asystole.

**Q:** What are the components of high-quality CPR?

**A:** Rate 100-120/min, depth 5-6 cm (adults), full chest recoil between compressions, minimize interruptions (<10 sec), avoid hyperventilation (10 breaths/min with advanced airway), rotate compressors every 2 min to prevent fatigue

ACLS 2020 guidelines: compression fraction >60% (time chest compressed during CPR). Avoid pausing CPR for rhythm check beyond 10 seconds. Switch compressors every 2 minutes. Continuous waveform capnography: ETCO2 <10 mmHg indicates poor CPR quality.

**Q:** What is ETCO2 monitoring and how is it used during cardiac arrest?

**A:** End-tidal CO2 (ETCO2): measures exhaled CO2; during CPR reflects cardiac output; ETCO2 <10 mmHg = poor CPR quality; ETCO2 sudden spike to >40 mmHg = ROSC; persistent ETCO2 <10 mmHg after 20 min = poor prognosis

ACLS 2020: continuous ETCO2 monitoring recommended during CPR. Used to assess CPR quality, confirm ET tube placement, and identify ROSC. ETCO2 can guide the decision to terminate resuscitation (controversial — other factors must be considered).

**Q:** A patient achieves ROSC after cardiac arrest. What is the immediate post-resuscitation care?

**A:** Post-cardiac arrest care: targeted temperature management (TTM) 33-36°C for 24h if unconscious, hemodynamic optimization (MAP ≥65 mmHg), avoid hypoxia (SpO2 94-98%) and hypercapnia (PaCO2 35-45), ECG for STEMI, brain CT, urgent coronary angiography if cardiac cause suspected

TTM/hypothermia reduces neurological injury. ACLS 2020: TTM is recommended for unconscious post-arrest patients with shockable rhythm; reasonable for non-shockable. Avoid fever (>37.5°C) in all post-arrest patients. Emergent coronary angiography for ST elevation or suspected cardiac cause.

**Q:** What is the pediatric BLS sequence for a child (1-8 years)?

**A:** Single rescuer: C-A-B; 30:2 compressions-to-breaths (2-hand or 2-finger for infant), defibrillate (2 J/kg initial dose) for shockable rhythms — two rescuers: 15:2 ratio

Pediatric cardiac arrest is usually respiratory in origin (not cardiac). Airway management and ventilation are more critical than in adults. Epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg IV/IO (max 1mg). Shockable rhythms (VF/pVT) are less common in children. Amiodarone 5 mg/kg IV/IO.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to airway management in cardiac arrest?

**A:** Bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation preferred initially; advanced airway (ET tube or supraglottic) only when provider can place without interrupting CPR; after advanced airway: 10 breaths/min asynchronous with compressions

ACLS 2020: BVM ventilation by experienced rescuers may be as effective as ETI with less interruption. Video laryngoscopy preferred for ETI. Confirm ETI with ETCO2 and clinical assessment. Supraglottic airways (LMA, King LT) are alternatives to ETI.

**Q:** When should cardiac resuscitation efforts be terminated?

**A:** Termination of resuscitation (TOR) in pre-hospital VF: unwitnessed + no bystander CPR + no ROSC after 3 cycles of ACLS and transport time >30 min — Bystanderr CPR rule: transport if ROSC, shockable rhythm, or bystander CPR initiated

Pre-hospital TOR rules (Canadian): BLS TOR rule: arrest not witnessed by EMS, no bystander CPR, no AED use before EMS, no ROSC after 3 ACLS cycles — consider field termination (high specificity for no survival). Exceptions: hypothermia, drowning, pregnancy, children.

**Q:** What are the shockable vs. non-shockable rhythms in cardiac arrest?

**A:** Shockable: Ventricular Fibrillation (VF) and Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT) — defibrillate; Non-shockable: Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA) and Asystole — NO defibrillation, focus on CPR and reversible causes

Defibrillation only works for VF/pVT by synchronously depolarizing myocardium. Attempting to defibrillate asystole or PEA is futile and wastes time. VF should be detected and defibrillated within 2 minutes — each minute of VF without defibrillation reduces survival by 10%.

**Q:** What is therapeutic hypothermia (targeted temperature management) and who qualifies?

**A:** TTM 33-36°C for 24 hours: all adult patients who remain comatose after ROSC from cardiac arrest; evidence strongest for VF/pVT rhythms; also reasonable for non-shockable rhythms

TTM works by reducing cerebral metabolic rate (6-10% per degree decrease), reducing excitotoxicity and free radical production. Complications: bradycardia (expected), coagulopathy, electrolyte shifts, infection. Active fever prevention (normothermia 37°C) for all post-arrest patients not undergoing TTM.

**Q:** What is the management of bradycardia with hemodynamic instability?

**A:** Atropine 0.5 mg IV q3-5 min (max 3 mg total); if no response: transcutaneous pacing immediately, then dopamine 2-10 mcg/kg/min or epinephrine 2-10 mcg/min IV infusion — identify and treat underlying cause

ACLS bradycardia algorithm: atropine first if hemodynamically unstable. Atropine may be ineffective for high-degree AV block (infranodal) or post-cardiac transplant — use transcutaneous pacing immediately for these. Transvenous pacing for definitive management while awaiting permanent pacemaker.

**Q:** What is the management of unstable tachycardia?

**A:** Synchronized cardioversion (not defibrillation): narrow complex SVT 50-100J, AF 120-200J, VT with pulse 100J — sedate if patient is conscious; do NOT delay for rhythm interpretation if hemodynamically unstable

ACLS: unstable tachycardia = hypotension, altered consciousness, chest pain, or acute pulmonary edema from arrhythmia. Synchronized cardioversion delivers shock during QRS (avoids R-on-T phenomenon). For conscious patients: procedural sedation (ketamine, etomidate, midazolam).

**Q:** What is return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and how is it confirmed?

**A:** ROSC: resumption of palpable pulse after cardiac arrest; confirmed by: palpable pulse, ETCO2 spike >40 mmHg, arterial waveform, SpO2 tracing

Clinical signs of ROSC: patient movement, spontaneous breathing, return of pulse. ETCO2 provides real-time confirmation during CPR. After ROSC: post-cardiac arrest bundle (TTM, hemodynamic optimization, 12-lead ECG, coronary angiography if appropriate, neurological assessment).

**Q:** What is the advanced airway approach in pediatric resuscitation?

**A:** BVM ventilation with 2-person technique preferred; ETI: uncuffed ET tube (children <8y, size = age/4 + 4), or cuffed tube (age/4 + 3.5) confirmed by ETCO2 and CXR

Pediatric ETI: confirm tube size with broselow tape or formula. Atropine no longer routinely recommended for pediatric RSI. Ketamine + succinylcholine or rocuronium for RSI. Supraglottic airways acceptable if ETI not possible.

**Q:** What is the sodium bicarbonate indication in cardiac arrest?

**A:** Sodium bicarbonate (1 mEq/kg IV) is indicated for: pre-existing hyperkalemia, sodium channel blocker toxicity (TCA overdose), prolonged arrest with severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1) — NOT routine in cardiac arrest

ACLS: routine bicarbonate administration does not improve outcomes in cardiac arrest and may worsen intracellular acidosis. Hyperkalemia-induced arrest: bicarbonate shifts K+ into cells. TCA overdose with wide QRS: 1-2 mEq/kg bicarbonate for QRS >100ms or hemodynamic instability.

**Q:** A 25-year-old is found drowning in an icy lake. He is in cardiac arrest with temperature 28°C. What is different about his resuscitation?

**A:** Hypothermic cardiac arrest: CPR, warm IV fluids, active external/internal rewarming; do NOT terminate resuscitation until patient is warm (≥32-35°C) — "No one is dead until they are warm and dead"

ACLS hypothermia guidelines: hypothermic hearts may be in refractory VF until rewarmed — warm first, then attempt defibrillation. ECMO (extracorporeal rewarming) is most effective for severe hypothermia with cardiac arrest. Target rewarming 1-2°C per hour with active techniques.

**Q:** What is pericardial tamponade and how is it relieved in cardiac arrest?

**A:** Tamponade: Beck triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds); FAST exam shows pericardial fluid; causes PEA arrest; treat with pericardiocentesis (subxiphoid approach) or surgical thoracotomy in cardiac arrest

Tamponade is a reversible cause of PEA arrest (5Ts). Pericardiocentesis: needle inserted at 45° angle between xiphoid and left costal margin, directed toward left shoulder, aspirate blood. Emergency thoracotomy for traumatic tamponade provides direct access to heart.

**Q:** What is the role of extracorporeal CPR (ECPR) in cardiac arrest?

**A:** ECPR (VA-ECMO during CPR): bridge for refractory cardiac arrest in selected patients (witnessed arrest, <60-65 years, treatable cause, no ROSC after ≥20 min of conventional ACLS)

ECPR provides perfusion while definitive treatment is applied (PCI for MI, warmed for hypothermia, LVAD bridge for cardiomyopathy). SAVE-J and ELSO data show improved survival in selected patients. Limited by availability of ECMO expertise and equipment. CANUCKS/ARREST trials ongoing.

**Q:** What are the determinants of successful defibrillation?

**A:** Time to defibrillation (most important), biphasic vs. monophasic waveform (biphasic more effective, lower energy), paddle/pad placement (correct position and contact), patient factors (metabolic, drug state)

ACLS: for every minute of VF without defibrillation, survival decreases by 10%. Public access defibrillation (AED) programs in public spaces significantly reduce out-of-hospital cardiac arrest mortality. Biphasic defibrillators (120-200J) are more effective than monophasic (360J) at lower energies.

**Q:** What is the ACLS algorithm for pulseless VT/VF?

**A:** CPR -> Shock 200J biphasic -> CPR 2min -> Epinephrine 1mg q3-5min -> Shock -> Amiodarone 300mg then 150mg -> repeat

H's and T's for reversible causes. ROSC: targeted temperature management 32-36C for 24h. Epinephrine for all pulseless rhythms.

**Q:** What is rapid sequence intubation (RSI) protocol?

**A:** Preoxygenate -> Induction (propofol/ketamine/etomidate) -> Paralysis (succinylcholine 1-2mg/kg or rocuronium 1.2mg/kg) -> Intubate -> Confirm (ETCO2, bilateral breath sounds, CXR)

Ketamine preferred if hypotensive/asthmatic. Etomidate: adrenal suppression risk. Succinylcholine contraindicated in hyperkalemia, burns>48h, denervation injuries.

**Q:** In adult ACLS, what is the recommended dose of epinephrine during cardiac arrest?

**A:** Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3-5 minutes

Per AHA/HSFC 2020 guidelines, epinephrine 1 mg (1:10,000) IV/IO every 3-5 min. For shockable rhythms give after 2nd shock. For non-shockable rhythms give immediately upon IV/IO access.

**Q:** List the Hs and Ts of reversible causes of cardiac arrest.

**A:** Hs: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia. Ts: Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Toxins, Thrombosis (PE/coronary)

Systematic evaluation of reversible causes is critical during each rhythm check. In Canada hypothermia arrests are particularly relevant given climate. Do not declare death until rewarmed to 32C.

**Q:** When and at what dose should amiodarone be given in shock-refractory VF/pVT?

**A:** Amiodarone 300 mg IV after 3rd shock, then 150 mg for recurrent VF/pVT. Alternative: lidocaine 1-1.5 mg/kg

ALPS trial showed amiodarone and lidocaine improved survival to hospital admission vs placebo for witnessed VF. Per HSFC amiodarone is first-line antiarrhythmic for refractory VF.

**Q:** What defibrillation energies are used for adult VF/pVT?

**A:** Biphasic: 120-200 J initial (device-specific), same or higher for subsequent. Monophasic: 360 J all shocks. If unknown use max energy.

Minimize peri-shock pause to less than 10 seconds. Resume compressions immediately post-shock without rhythm check. CPR quality (rate 100-120, depth 5-6cm, full recoil) is the strongest predictor of ROSC.

**Q:** What drugs and doses are used for Rapid Sequence Intubation?

**A:** Induction: Ketamine 1-2 mg/kg or Propofol 1-2 mg/kg. Paralytic: Succinylcholine 1-1.5 mg/kg (onset 45s) or Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg (onset 60s)

Ketamine preferred in hemodynamic instability and asthma. Propofol causes hypotension. Etomidate 0.3 mg/kg is another option but may cause adrenal suppression. Preoxygenation with 100% O2 for 3 min or 8 vital capacity breaths.

**Q:** What are the contraindications to succinylcholine?

**A:** Hyperkalemia, burns/crush >48h, denervation injury, malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, neuromuscular disease, penetrating eye injury, prolonged immobilization

Succinylcholine causes 0.5 mEq/L K+ rise normally. Upregulated extrajunctional AChRs in burns/denervation cause potentially fatal hyperkalemia. Use rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg instead, reversible with sugammadex 16 mg/kg.

**Q:** What is targeted temperature management after cardiac arrest?

**A:** Maintain 32-36C for at least 24h in comatose patients with ROSC. Rewarm at 0.25-0.5C/h. Neuroprognostication deferred to 72h+ using multimodal assessment.

TTM2 trial: no benefit of 33C vs 36C but fever prevention is critical. Active cooling devices preferred. Monitor for shivering (treat with sedation). Avoid hyperthermia during rewarming phase.

**Q:** Describe the ACLS algorithm for symptomatic bradycardia.

**A:** Atropine 1 mg IV q3-5 min (max 3 mg). If ineffective: transcutaneous pacing, dopamine 5-20 mcg/kg/min, or epinephrine 2-10 mcg/min infusion

Atropine ineffective in infranodal block (Mobitz II, complete heart block). Start transcutaneous pacing immediately if high-degree block. Verify capture by palpating femoral pulse. Transvenous pacing is definitive.

**Q:** What is the stepwise management of stable SVT?

**A:** Vagal maneuvers (modified Valsalva) then adenosine 6mg rapid IV push then 12mg then 12mg. Refractory: diltiazem 0.25 mg/kg IV or metoprolol

Modified Valsalva with passive leg raise has higher conversion rate than standard. Adenosine requires rapid push with saline flush at proximal site. Contraindicated in pre-excited AF and severe asthma. Half-life only 6 seconds.

**Q:** How is unstable tachycardia managed?

**A:** Synchronized cardioversion with procedural sedation. Energy: narrow regular 50-100J, narrow irregular 120-200J, wide regular 100J, wide irregular use defibrillation dose

Unstable = hypotension, AMS, ischemic chest pain, acute HF. Do NOT synchronize for VF or polymorphic VT (use defibrillation). Ensure sync markers align with R waves before delivering shock.

**Q:** What is the first-line vasopressor and target in septic shock?

**A:** Norepinephrine targeting MAP 65 mmHg. Second-line: vasopressin 0.03 U/min. Third: epinephrine. Add hydrocortisone 200 mg/d for refractory shock

SSC 2021 guidelines: start vasopressors after 30 mL/kg crystalloid if still hypotensive. Dynamic measures (pulse pressure variation, passive leg raise) guide further fluids. Balanced crystalloids (RL) preferred over NS for large-volume resuscitation.

**Q:** What are the indications and technique for emergency cricothyrotomy?

**A:** Indication: CICO (cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate). Technique: vertical skin incision, horizontal stab through cricothyroid membrane, bougie, 6.0 cuffed ETT. Contraindicated in children <8 (needle cric instead)

Final step in difficult airway algorithm after failed intubation and failed supraglottic airway. Landmark: palpable depression between thyroid and cricoid cartilages in midline. Scalpel-bougie-tube technique is fastest and most reliable.

**Q:** How is tension pneumothorax managed emergently?

**A:** Needle decompression (14G) at 4th/5th ICS anterior axillary line (preferred) or 2nd ICS MCL, followed by chest tube insertion

Clinical diagnosis: do not delay for CXR. Signs: hypotension, JVD, tracheal deviation, absent breath sounds. Finger thoracostomy is an alternative in intubated patients. 4th/5th ICS AAL preferred due to thinner chest wall.

**Q:** What is the massive transfusion protocol?

**A:** 1:1:1 ratio pRBC:FFP:platelets. TXA 1g IV within 3h of injury. Permissive hypotension (SBP 80-90) until surgical control in penetrating trauma

PROPPR and CRASH-2 trials provide evidence. Activate for anticipated >10u pRBC/24h or >4u/1h. Avoid lethal triad (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy). POC testing (TEG/ROTEM) guides component therapy. Warm all products.

**Q:** What are the components and scoring of the Glasgow Coma Scale?

**A:** Eye 1-4, Verbal 1-5, Motor 1-6. Total 3-15. Severe TBI: 3-8 (intubate). Moderate: 9-12. Mild: 13-15. Motor is most prognostic.

Assess after resuscitation. Record intubated verbal as 1T. GCS has inter-rater variability limitations. FOUR score is an alternative for intubated patients. Serial GCS monitoring detects deterioration.

**Q:** What does each eFAST view assess in trauma?

**A:** RUQ: hepatorenal recess (Morrisons). LUQ: splenorenal. Suprapubic: pelvic free fluid. Subxiphoid: pericardial effusion. Bilateral anterior lung: pneumothorax

Sensitivity ~85% for free fluid, improves with serial exams. Positive eFAST + hemodynamic instability = OR. Absent lung sliding on anterior views suggests pneumothorax. M-mode shows barcode sign (PTX) vs seashore sign (normal).

**Q:** What are weight-based pediatric cardiac arrest drug doses?

**A:** Epi: 0.01 mg/kg (0.1 mL/kg of 1:10,000) q3-5min. Defib: 2J/kg first, 4J/kg subsequent. Amiodarone: 5mg/kg (max 300mg). IO preferred if no rapid IV.

Pediatric arrests usually respiratory etiology. Most common rhythm: PEA/asystole. Use Broselow tape for unknown weight. Compressions: infants 4cm depth, children 5cm, rate 100-120. 15:2 compression-ventilation ratio with 2 rescuers.

**Q:** What are the criteria, dosing, and contraindications for IV alteplase in acute ischemic stroke?

**A:** 0.9 mg/kg (max 90mg), 10% bolus then 60min infusion within 4.5h onset. CI: active bleeding, BP >185/110, PLT <100k, INR >1.7, recent surgery

Canadian Stroke Best Practice Guidelines. Extended window (3-4.5h) excludes age >80, DM+prior stroke, NIHSS >25. EVT for large vessel occlusion up to 24h with perfusion imaging. BP must be controlled before and 24h after tPA.

**Q:** Outline the ABCDE primary survey in trauma.

**A:** A: Airway + C-spine protection. B: Breathing (assess/treat pneumothorax). C: Circulation (hemorrhage control, 2 large-bore IVs). D: Disability (GCS, pupils). E: Exposure + environmental control

ATLS systematic approach: address life threats as found. Primary survey <5 min. C-spine immobilized until cleared. Adjuncts: monitoring, Foley, NG/OG, CXR, pelvis XR, eFAST.

**Q:** How does cardiac arrest management differ in pregnancy?

**A:** Standard ACLS + manual left uterine displacement + IV access above diaphragm. If no ROSC by 4 min, perimortem cesarean by 5 min if >=23 weeks gestation

Perimortem C-section relieves aortocaval compression improving maternal resuscitation AND neonatal outcomes. Same defibrillation doses. Remove fetal monitors before shock. Consider amniotic fluid embolism, eclampsia, hemorrhage as causes.

**Q:** A 60-year-old diabetic is found unresponsive. GCS is 6 (E1V2M3). Glucose is 35 mg/dL. What is the immediate management, and what mnemonic covers the differential for altered level of consciousness?

**A:** Immediate IV dextrose (D50W) and thiamine (before glucose if alcoholism/malnutrition suspected to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy). The DIMS mnemonic for altered LOC: Drugs/toxins (opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol), Infections (meningitis, encephalitis, sepsis), Metabolic (hypoglycemia, DKA, hepatic encephalopathy, uremia, electrolytes), Structural (stroke, hemorrhage, trauma, mass).

Hypoglycemia is the most rapidly reversible cause of altered LOC and must be checked immediately in all patients. GCS <8 generally indicates need for airway protection (intubation). The mnemonic DIMS (or AEIOU-TIPS) provides a systematic approach to avoid missing treatable causes of coma.

**Q:** Explain the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) components and the scores indicating severe, moderate, and mild impairment.

**A:** Eye opening (E): 4-spontaneous, 3-to voice, 2-to pain, 1-none. Verbal (V): 5-oriented, 4-confused, 3-inappropriate words, 2-incomprehensible sounds, 1-none. Motor (M): 6-obeys commands, 5-localizes pain, 4-withdraws, 3-abnormal flexion (decorticate), 2-extension (decerebrate), 1-none. Total: 3-15. Severe ≤8, Moderate 9-12, Mild 13-15.

The motor component is the most prognostically significant element of the GCS. A GCS of 8 or less is generally the threshold for endotracheal intubation for airway protection. Decorticate posturing (flexion) indicates damage above the red nucleus, while decerebrate posturing (extension) indicates brainstem damage below the red nucleus.

**Q:** A 22-year-old is brought to the ED unresponsive with pinpoint pupils and a respiratory rate of 6. What is the most likely cause and immediate treatment?

**A:** Opioid overdose. Immediate treatment: naloxone (0.4-2 mg IV/IM/IN, repeat every 2-3 minutes as needed) and airway management with bag-valve-mask ventilation. Monitor for re-sedation as naloxone's half-life (30-90 min) is shorter than most opioids.

The classic triad of opioid toxicity is respiratory depression, altered consciousness, and miosis (pinpoint pupils). Naloxone is a competitive opioid antagonist that reverses all effects. With potent synthetic opioids like fentanyl, higher and repeated doses of naloxone may be required. Observation for at least 4-6 hours post-naloxone is essential.

**Q:** What are the 4 types of shock and their hemodynamic profiles?

**A:** Hypovolemic: low CVP, low CO, high SVR. Cardiogenic: high CVP, low CO, high SVR. Distributive: low CVP, high CO, low SVR. Obstructive: high CVP, low CO, high SVR

Distributive includes septic (most common), anaphylactic, neurogenic. Obstructive: PE, tamponade, tension pneumothorax. Treatment targets the underlying cause + fluid/vasopressor support.

**Q:** What is massive transfusion protocol?

**A:** 1:1:1 ratio of pRBCs:FFP:platelets. Activate when anticipated need >10 units pRBCs in 24h or >4 units in 1h. Give tranexamic acid within 3h of bleeding

CRASH-2 trial: TXA reduces mortality in trauma. Monitor for complications: hypothermia, hypocalcemia (citrate toxicity), hyperkalemia, DIC. Type O negative blood for emergencies before crossmatch.

**Q:** What are transfusion reactions and their management?

**A:** Acute hemolytic: ABO incompatibility, fever/flank pain/hemoglobinuria — stop transfusion, IV fluids. Febrile non-hemolytic: most common, antipyretics. Allergic: urticaria/anaphylaxis. TRALI: non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema within 6h

TACO vs TRALI: TACO has volume overload (elevated BNP, responds to diuretics). TRALI has bilateral infiltrates, hypoxia, no fluid overload. TRALI: supportive care, no diuretics.

**Q:** What are the hemodynamic profiles (CVP, CO, SVR) for the four types of shock: hypovolemic, cardiogenic, distributive, and obstructive?

**A:** Hypovolemic: low CVP, low CO, high SVR. Cardiogenic: high CVP, low CO, high SVR. Distributive: low CVP, high CO, low SVR. Obstructive: high CVP, low CO, high SVR.

Distributive shock (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic) is unique in having low SVR due to inappropriate vasodilation. Cardiogenic and obstructive share similar profiles but differ in etiology — pump failure vs. mechanical obstruction. CVP helps differentiate hypovolemic (low preload) from cardiogenic (high preload).

**Q:** A trauma patient presents with hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds. What is the diagnosis, and what is the immediate management?

**A:** Beck's triad indicates cardiac tamponade (obstructive shock). Immediate management is pericardiocentesis or emergent thoracotomy if unstable.

Cardiac tamponade is a form of obstructive shock where fluid in the pericardial space compresses the heart, impairing diastolic filling. Pulsus paradoxus (>10 mmHg drop in systolic BP on inspiration) is another classic finding. Bedside echocardiography confirms the diagnosis rapidly.

**Q:** In septic shock, what is the first-line vasopressor, and when should vasopressin be added?

**A:** Norepinephrine is first-line. Vasopressin (up to 0.03 U/min) is added as a second agent when norepinephrine alone is insufficient to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg.

Norepinephrine provides potent alpha-1 vasoconstriction with modest beta-1 effects, increasing SVR and MAP. Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors and may be particularly useful in vasodilatory shock refractory to catecholamines. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends against dopamine as first-line except in select bradycardic patients.

**Q:** When is dobutamine indicated in shock management, and what is its mechanism of action?

**A:** Dobutamine is indicated in cardiogenic shock with low cardiac output despite adequate volume resuscitation. It is a beta-1 agonist that increases cardiac contractility and heart rate.

Dobutamine increases myocardial contractility (positive inotropy) and can improve cardiac output in pump failure. It may cause hypotension due to peripheral beta-2 vasodilation, so it is often combined with norepinephrine. It is not appropriate as a sole agent in undifferentiated shock.

**Q:** What is the recommended initial fluid resuscitation in septic shock, and why are balanced crystalloids (Ringer's lactate) preferred over normal saline?

**A:** Give 30 mL/kg of crystalloid within the first 3 hours. Balanced crystalloids are preferred because normal saline's high chloride content (154 mEq/L) can cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and acute kidney injury.

The SMART trial demonstrated lower rates of major adverse kidney events with balanced crystalloids versus NS in critically ill patients. Ringer's lactate has a chloride concentration closer to plasma (109 mEq/L). Colloids (albumin) have not shown survival benefit over crystalloids in most settings.

**Q:** What is the massive transfusion protocol ratio, and when should it be activated?

**A:** 1:1:1 ratio of packed RBCs, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets. Activate when >10 units pRBC anticipated in 24 hours, or >4 units in 1 hour, with ongoing hemorrhage.

The 1:1:1 ratio mimics whole blood and prevents dilutional coagulopathy. The PROPPR trial showed that a 1:1:1 ratio achieved hemostasis more effectively than 1:1:2. Tranexamic acid (TXA) should be given within 3 hours of injury per the CRASH-2 trial.

**Q:** What are the four types of transfusion reactions, their timing, and key distinguishing features?

**A:** Acute hemolytic (minutes, ABO incompatibility, fever/flank pain/hemoglobinuria), febrile non-hemolytic (during/after, cytokine-mediated, fever/chills), allergic/anaphylactic (minutes, urticaria to shock), and TRALI (within 6 hours, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema).

Acute hemolytic reactions are the most dangerous and usually result from ABO mismatch clerical errors. TRALI is the leading cause of transfusion-related mortality and presents with bilateral infiltrates, hypoxia, and no evidence of volume overload. TACO (transfusion-associated circulatory overload) is the main differential for TRALI.

**Q:** What is the difference between type and screen versus crossmatch in blood banking?

**A:** Type and screen determines ABO/Rh type and screens for unexpected antibodies. Crossmatch mixes donor RBCs with patient serum to confirm compatibility before transfusion.

Type and screen is sufficient when transfusion is unlikely but should be available (e.g., elective surgery with low bleeding risk). Crossmatch is required before non-emergency transfusion. In massive hemorrhage, uncrossmatched O-negative (or O-positive for males) blood can be given immediately.

**Q:** What are the initial ventilator settings for a patient with ARDS using lung-protective ventilation?

**A:** Tidal volume 6 mL/kg ideal body weight, plateau pressure ≤30 cmH2O, PEEP titrated per FiO2-PEEP table, respiratory rate 20-35 to maintain pH >7.25, target SpO2 88-95%.

The ARDSNet trial showed a 9% absolute mortality reduction with low tidal volume (6 mL/kg IBW) versus 12 mL/kg. Ideal body weight is based on height, not actual weight, to prevent overdistension. Permissive hypercapnia is acceptable as long as pH remains above 7.20.

**Q:** Compare the ventilator modes: Assist-Control (AC), SIMV, and Pressure Support (PS).

**A:** AC: every breath (triggered or mandatory) delivers full set tidal volume/pressure. SIMV: mandatory breaths at set rate, spontaneous breaths unsupported (or with PS). PS: patient-triggered only, clinician sets pressure, patient controls rate/volume.

AC is commonly used for initial ventilation as it guarantees minute ventilation. PS is a weaning mode where the patient must initiate all breaths. SIMV was historically used for weaning but is falling out of favor as it may increase work of breathing during spontaneous breaths.

**Q:** During a Code Blue, what are the key team roles and the recommended epinephrine dosing schedule?

**A:** Team roles: team leader, airway manager, compressor, medication/IV nurse, recorder, defibrillator operator. Epinephrine 1 mg IV every 3-5 minutes for non-shockable rhythms (PEA/asystole); for shockable rhythms, give after the second shock.

Effective team dynamics with closed-loop communication are critical to resuscitation outcomes. High-quality CPR (rate 100-120/min, depth 5-6 cm, full chest recoil, minimal interruptions) is the single most important intervention. Epinephrine improves short-term ROSC but has uncertain neurological outcome benefit.

**Q:** What is targeted temperature management (TTM) post-cardiac arrest, and what are the current recommendations?

**A:** TTM involves actively controlling body temperature to 32-36°C for at least 24 hours in comatose patients after ROSC. Fever prevention (≤37.5°C) for 72 hours is essential.

The TTM2 trial showed no benefit of 33°C over 36°C with strict fever prevention, shifting practice toward normothermia with aggressive fever avoidance. Hypothermia reduces cerebral metabolic demand and may attenuate reperfusion injury. Shivering must be managed as it increases metabolic demand.

**Q:** What are the reversible causes of cardiac arrest (H's and T's)?

**A:** H's: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia. T's: Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Toxins, Thrombosis (pulmonary/coronary).

Identifying and treating reversible causes is critical during cardiac arrest, especially for PEA and asystole which are non-shockable. Bedside ultrasound can rapidly identify tamponade, PE, and hypovolemia. Hyperkalemia is suggested by peaked T waves and wide QRS on the defibrillator monitor.

**Q:** A patient in septic shock remains hypotensive after 30 mL/kg crystalloid and norepinephrine at 0.5 mcg/kg/min. What additional interventions should be considered?

**A:** Add vasopressin (0.03 U/min), consider stress-dose hydrocortisone (200 mg/day IV), reassess volume status with dynamic measures (passive leg raise, pulse pressure variation), and evaluate for source control.

Corticosteroids are recommended in septic shock refractory to fluids and vasopressors, per the ADRENAL and APROCCHSS trials. Dynamic measures of fluid responsiveness are superior to static measures like CVP. Inadequate source control (e.g., undrained abscess) is a common reason for refractory shock.

**Q:** What are the indications for giving uncrossmatched O-negative blood versus waiting for type-specific or crossmatched blood?

**A:** Give uncrossmatched O-negative blood in life-threatening hemorrhage when there is no time for type and screen (typically <10 minutes). Type-specific blood can be given once ABO/Rh is known (~10-15 min). Fully crossmatched blood requires ~45-60 minutes.

O-negative is the universal donor for RBCs as it lacks A, B, and Rh-D antigens. O-positive can be used for Rh-negative males in emergencies since Rh sensitization is only clinically relevant in pregnancy. Emergency release protocols should be established in all trauma centers.

**Q:** Why is Desflurane not used for inhalational induction?

**A:** Desflurane is an airway irritant that can provoke breath holding, laryngospasm, and salivation

These airway reflexes make Desflurane unsuitable for mask induction, particularly in pediatric patients.

**Q:** What is MAC-intubation?

**A:** The MAC of anesthetic that inhibits movement and coughing during endotracheal intubation; generally 1.3 MAC

This higher concentration is required to suppress airway reflexes during laryngoscopy and intubation.

**Q:** What are the clinical indications for muscle relaxants?

**A:** Facilitate intubation, assist mechanical ventilation, prevent muscle stretch reflex and decrease muscle tone, allow surgical field access

Muscle relaxation provides optimal conditions for airway management and surgical procedures.

**Q:** What is the recommended compression depth for adult CPR according to 2020 AHA guidelines?

**A:** 2-2.4 inches (5-6 cm)

Adult CPR requires compressions of 2-2.4 inches depth, pushing hard and fast with complete chest wall recoil between compressions.

**Q:** What is the compression-to-ventilation ratio for adult CPR?

**A:** 30 compressions to 2 ventilations

Adult CPR uses a 30:2 ratio - 30 chest compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths, with a rate of 100-120 compressions per minute.

**Q:** What is hands-only CPR and when is it preferred?

**A:** Compression-only CPR; preferred if bystander is not trained, does not feel confident, or chooses to use compressions only

Hands-only CPR (continuous chest compressions without rescue breaths) is recommended for untrained bystanders or those unwilling to perform conventional CPR, as it simplifies resuscitation for lay rescuers.

**Q:** What are the two airway maneuvers in CPR and when is each used?

**A:** Head tilt-chin lift (standard), and jaw thrust without head extension (if concern for spinal injury)

The head tilt-chin lift is the standard maneuver to open the airway. The jaw thrust is used when cervical spine injury is suspected, as it opens the airway without extending the neck.

**Q:** What is the compression-to-ventilation ratio for adult CPR according to 2020 AHA guidelines?

**A:** 30:2 (30 compressions to 2 ventilations)

Standard adult CPR uses 30 compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths. This applies to adults and children over 8 years old.

**Q:** What is the recommended compression depth for adult CPR?

**A:** 2-2.4 inches (5-6 cm)

AHA guidelines recommend pushing hard and fast to a depth of at least 2 inches (5 cm) but not exceeding 2.4 inches (6 cm).

**Q:** What is the compression landmark for adult CPR?

**A:** Center of the chest, lower half of the sternum

Compressions should be performed on the lower half of the sternum, between the nipples.

**Q:** What is the compression landmark for infant CPR?

**A:** Just below the nipple line

Infant compressions are performed just below the nipple line using 2 fingers or thumbs.

**Q:** When is hands-only CPR preferred?

**A:** When the bystander is not trained, does not feel confident in conventional CPR, or if trained but chooses compressions only

Hands-only CPR is an acceptable alternative and is preferred in many out-of-hospital cardiac arrest situations.

**Q:** According to 2020 AHA CPR Guidelines, what is the compression-to-ventilation ratio for adult CPR?

**A:** 30:2 (30 compressions to 2 ventilations)

The 2020 AHA guidelines maintain the 30:2 ratio for adult CPR, which allows for adequate chest compressions while providing essential ventilations.

**Q:** What is the recommended compression method for infant CPR (<1 year)?

**A:** 2 fingers or thumbs encircling the chest

For infants, use 2 fingers (or thumbs) placed just below the nipple line, compressing about 1/3 to 1/2 the depth of the chest.

**Q:** When is hands-only CPR preferred over conventional CPR?

**A:** When the bystander is not trained, does not feel confident, or chooses to use compressions only

Hands-only CPR is recommended for untrained bystanders or those who are not confident in providing rescue breaths, as continuous compressions are better than no CPR.

**Q:** What is the airway technique for patients with suspected spinal injury?

**A:** Jaw thrust without head extension (instead of head tilt-chin lift)

Jaw thrust maintains cervical spine alignment while opening the airway in patients with potential spinal injury.

**Q:** What is the maneuver for severe foreign body airway obstruction in an infant?

**A:** Back blows and chest compressions

Infants receive back blows and chest thrusts rather than abdominal thrusts due to risk of organ injury.

**Q:** What is the volume of one breath in adult CPR?

**A:** 1 second per breath, enough to see chest rise

Each breath should be delivered over 1 second, pausing between breaths, and stopped once chest rise is observed.

**Q:** What are the immediate steps in managing status epilepticus?

**A:** Protect airway with positioning, intubate if airway compromised; monitor vitals, ECG, oximetry; check POCT capillary blood glucose; establish IV access; give benzodiazepine (lorazepam 2mg IV or midazolam 5mg IM); fluid resuscitation; give IV dextrose if glucose <3 mmol/L

These steps should be initiated simultaneously within the first minutes of status epilepticus management.

**Q:** What is the treatment for classical heat stroke?

**A:** Cool with water mist and fans or ice water immersion, secure airway, give fluid resuscitation if hypotensive, avoid vasoconstrictors and antipyretics

Rapid cooling is critical; avoid drugs that impair heat dissipation or vasoconstriction

**Q:** What is the approach to cardiac arrest in a hypothermic patient?

**A:** Focus on active core re-warming (preferably ECMO), intubate if needed with warmed humidified O2, check pulse for 1 min, if any pulse do NOT do CPR, if VFib try up to 3 defibrillation attempts if temp <30°C

Bradycardia may be physiologic; medications may be ineffective at low temperatures

**Q:** What is the Parkland formula for burn fluid resuscitation?

**A:** 4mL × weight (kg) × %TBSA for 2nd/3rd degree burns; give half in first 8 hours from burn time, remainder over next 16 hours

This formula guides replacement of insensate fluid losses in major burns

**Q:** When is airway establishment indicated in burn patients?

**A:** Indicated with burns >40% BSA, smoke inhalation injury, or signs of significant upper airway burns

Early airway management is crucial as edema can rapidly compromise breathing

**Q:** What are the indications for airway establishment in burn patients?

**A:** Burns >40% BSA, smoke inhalation injury, or signs of significant upper airway burns

Early airway assessment is critical in major burns. Upper airway burns can cause rapid swelling and airway obstruction.

**Q:** What are the steps in immediate management of status epilepticus?

**A:** 1) Protect airway with positioning, intubate if compromised; 2) Monitor vital signs, ECG, oximetry, POCT glucose; 3) Establish IV access; 4) Give benzodiazepine (lorazepam 2mg IV or midazolam 5mg IM); 5) Fluid resuscitation; 6) IV dextrose if glucose <3 mmol/L

Early seizure termination is crucial in status epilepticus to prevent neuronal damage from prolonged seizure activity.

**Q:** How do you treat heat stroke?

**A:** Cool body temperature with water mist and fans or ice water immersion, secure airway, give fluid resuscitation, avoid β-agonists and antipyretics

Rapid cooling is critical; avoid medications that affect thermoregulation or vasoconstriction.

**Q:** When is airway establishment indicated in burn management?

**A:** Burns >40% BSA or smoke inhalation injury or signs of significant upper airway burns

Early airway intervention is critical before edema compromises the airway.

**Q:** What are the indications for intubation in burn patients?

**A:** Burns >40% BSA, smoke inhalation injury, signs of significant upper airway burns

Early intubation is critical as airway edema can progress rapidly, making later attempts difficult or impossible.

**Q:** What are the key differences in fluid management between DKA and HHS?

**A:** HHS requires more aggressive fluid resuscitation due to more severe dehydration (10-12L deficit vs 6L in DKA)

HHS has a more gradual onset leading to greater fluid depletion. Initial rate may be 1-2L/h NS until euvolemic.

**Q:** Why is the intraosseous (IO) route of drug administration particularly valuable in emergency medicine?

**A:** The IO route provides rapid and reliable vascular access for drug and fluid administration when IV access has failed, with similar pharmacokinetics to IV administration. It can be established quickly and is suitable for emergency situations including cardiac arrest.

The IO route delivers drugs and fluids directly into the bone marrow sinusoids, which drain into the venous system. Onset and pharmacokinetics closely mirror IV administration. Canadian emergency guidelines (based on ACLS/PALS) recommend IO access as an alternative to IV when vascular access cannot be rapidly achieved, particularly in pediatric resuscitation.

**Q:** In the COACT trial, what was shown about immediate coronary angiography vs. delayed angiography in cardiac arrest survivors without STEMI?

**A:** Immediate angiography did not improve 1-year clinical outcomes (survival, MI, revascularization, HF hospitalization) compared to delayed angiography after neurological recovery

COACT (JAMA Cardiol 2020) demonstrated no significant improvement in 1-year outcomes with immediate post-arrest angiography in the absence of STEMI. Current guidelines recommend delayed angiography after neurological stabilization in OHCA without STEMI, reserving immediate angiography for STEMI.

**Q:** What are the 4 steps in the approach to a critically ill patient in the emergency department?

**A:** 1. Rapid Primary Survey (RPS) — ABCDE (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure/Environment); 2. Resuscitation — concurrent with RPS, attend to ABCs; 3. Detailed Secondary Survey; 4. Definitive Care.

In cardiac arrest, the approach shifts to CABs (Compressions, Airway, Breathing). In massive trauma with exsanguination, CAB prioritization treats hypovolemic shock. Changes in hemodynamic or neurological status necessitate returning to primary survey.

**Q:** What are the signs of airway obstruction and indications for endotracheal intubation (the '4 Ps')?

**A:** Signs of airway obstruction: agitation/confusion, 'universal choking sign,' respiratory distress, failure to speak/dysphonia, stridor, cyanosis. The 4 Ps of intubation: Patent airway maintenance, Protects against aspiration (GCS <8), Positive pressure ventilation needed, Pulmonary toilet (suction).

In trauma, C-spine immobilization with in-line stabilization is required during intubation. Orotracheal RSI is preferred; nasotracheal may be used in conscious patients. Surgical airway (cricothyrotomy) is the backup if unable to intubate or ventilate.

**Q:** What drugs can be delivered via endotracheal tube (ETT), and what mnemonic is used?

**A:** NAVEL: Naloxone (Narcan®), Atropine, Salbutamol (Ventolin®), Epinephrine, Lidocaine. These can be administered via ETT when IV/IO access is unavailable.

Endotracheal drug delivery is a last-resort option during resuscitation. Doses administered via ETT are typically 2–2.5 times the IV dose, diluted in 10 mL of normal saline, followed by several positive pressure ventilations to distribute the drug.

**Q:** What are the oxygen delivery methods and their approximate FiO2 ranges?

**A:** Nasal prongs: 25–40% at 1–6 L/min; Simple face mask: 40–60%; Non-rebreather mask: 80–90% at 15 L/min; High-flow nasal cannula: up to 100% at 15–60 L/min. CPAP/BiPAP provide supplemental ventilation beyond face masks.

In the emergency department, oxygen delivery is escalated based on severity of hypoxia and response. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) provides humidified oxygen and positive airway pressure, bridging between conventional oxygen and non-invasive ventilation.

**Q:** What are the four major types of shock and an example cause of each?

**A:** 1. Hypovolemic: hemorrhage, severe burns, dehydration; 2. Cardiogenic: myocardial ischemia, dysrhythmias, cardiomyopathy; 3. Distributive: septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic (spinal cord injury); 4. Obstructive: cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, PE, aortic stenosis.

Shock in trauma patients is hemorrhagic until proven otherwise. Key clinical findings: early shock presents with tachycardia, tachypnea, narrow pulse pressure, cool extremities (except neurogenic/septic); late shock adds hypotension, altered mental status, and oliguria.

**Q:** What are the maintenance fluid rates using the 4-2-1 rule, and what is the shortcut formula for patients ≥20 kg?

**A:** 4-2-1 rule: 0–10 kg = 4 cc/kg/h; 10–20 kg = 2 cc/kg/h; remaining weight = 1 cc/kg/h. Shortcut for ≥20 kg: Fluid rate (cc/h) = 40 + patient's weight in kg. Replace ongoing losses and deficits (assume 10% body weight for deficits).

The 4-2-1 rule (Holliday-Segar method) calculates daily fluid maintenance needs based on metabolic demands proportional to body weight. In resuscitation, initial bolus is given while monitoring HR, BP, and urine output, with early escalation to blood products in hemorrhagic shock.

**Q:** What investigations are ordered during resuscitation in the emergency department?

**A:** CBC, electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose, amylase, INR/PTT, beta-hCG, liver panel, VBG, lactate, troponin, toxicology screen, group and screen. ECG, BP, and O2 monitors are applied continuously. Foley catheter and NG tube are inserted if indicated.

These investigations are obtained concurrently with the primary survey to identify life-threatening abnormalities (e.g., coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis, pregnancy) that guide resuscitation decisions. Lactate elevation indicates tissue hypoperfusion even when BP is maintained.

**Q:** What are 'rescue' airway management techniques and when are they used?

**A:** Rescue techniques include: bougie (used as a guidewire to guide ETT placement), video laryngoscopy (indirect visualization), and laryngeal mask airway (LMA) or Combitube® as supraglottic devices. These are used when direct laryngoscopy fails or is anticipated to be difficult.

A failed airway algorithm progresses from optimized direct laryngoscopy → video laryngoscopy → supraglottic airway device → surgical airway (cricothyrotomy). Provider safety considerations (e.g., COVID-19 requiring full PPE) may preclude certain airway interventions.

**Q:** What are the compression-to-ventilation ratios in adult vs. child/infant CPR (AHA 2020 guidelines)?

**A:** Adults: 30 compressions to 2 ventilations (single or dual rescuer). Children/infants: 30:2 for single rescuer, 15:2 for dual rescuer.

The 15:2 ratio for pediatric two-rescuer CPR reflects the proportionally higher respiratory contribution to pediatric cardiac arrest compared with adults. Hands-only CPR is acceptable for bystanders not confident in ventilation technique.

**Q:** What compression rate and depth are recommended for adult CPR per AHA 2020 guidelines?

**A:** Rate: 100–120/min. Depth: 2–2.4 inches (approximately 5–6 cm). Allow complete chest wall recoil between compressions.

Adequate rate and depth optimise cardiac output during CPR. Incomplete recoil impairs venous return and reduces coronary perfusion pressure. Rate >120/min is associated with inadequate depth.

**Q:** When is immediate defibrillation indicated during cardiac arrest resuscitation?

**A:** Immediately if a shockable rhythm (ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia) is identified. For unwitnessed arrest, perform 5 cycles/2 min of CPR first, then use AED.

Early defibrillation is the single most important determinant of survival in VF/VT arrest. In unwitnessed arrest, a period of CPR first allows restoration of some metabolic substrate before the defibrillation shock.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic METABOLIC for the causes of coma?

**A:** M — Major organ failure; E — Electrolyte/Endocrine; T — Toxins/Temperature; A — Acid disorders; B — Base disorders; O — decreased Oxygen level; L — Lactate; I — Insulin/Infection (sepsis); C — Cardiac/hyperCalcemia.

Toxic-metabolic causes are the majority cause of coma. Structural causes (minority) include hemorrhage, ischemia, and mass lesions. The key clinical distinction: toxic-metabolic coma typically has symmetric findings and preserved upper brainstem reflexes (equal reactive pupils), while structural coma often has asymmetric findings and focal deficits.

**Q:** What are the red flag features for back pain that require urgent investigation?

**A:** Fracture risk (osteoporosis, age >50, mild trauma), infection (fever, IV drug use, recent spinal intervention, immunosuppression), cancer, vascular causes (cardiac risk factors), neurological deficits (saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction), constant pain, paresthesias.

The CORE Back Tool helps screen for common mechanical back pain syndromes. Extraspinal emergencies (aortic dissection, AAA, PE, MI, retroperitoneal bleed, pancreatitis) and spinal emergencies (osteomyelitis, cauda equina syndrome, epidural abscess/hematoma, discitis, spinal fracture, malignancy) must be excluded. Urgent MRI is indicated for neurological findings.

**Q:** What imaging is appropriate for emergency back pain evaluation?

**A:** CT if trauma or fracture risk; urgent MRI if neurological findings; X-ray and MRI if risk of infection or malignancy. Reserve imaging for neurological deficits, metastases, and patients at high-risk of fracture, infection, cancer, or vascular cause.

Routine imaging is not indicated for typical musculoskeletal back pain (moderate, worse with movement or cough, no visceral symptoms). Selective imaging prevents unnecessary radiation and cost while ensuring dangerous pathology is not missed.

**Q:** What are the high-mortality and high-morbidity causes of dyspnea that must be excluded first?

**A:** Respiratory: airway obstruction (foreign body, epiglottitis, abscess, anaphylaxis), pneumo/hemothorax, pulmonary edema/PE/pneumonia/acute asthma/COPD exacerbation. Cardiac: CHF, MI, valvular disease, tamponade, arrhythmia. Metabolic: metabolic acidosis, toxin ingestion. Neuromuscular: myasthenia gravis, diaphragmatic paralysis.

Dyspnea is the subjective sensation of SOB. History and physical identify the cause in ~2/3 of cases. Life-threatening causes must be excluded immediately. Pulse oximetry, CXR, ECG, and ABG/VBG are initial investigations. CT chest is indicated when PE is suspected. NIPPV reduces need for intubation in severe COPD/CHF.

**Q:** What are the indications and dosing for lorazepam in the ED?

**A:** Anxiety: 0.5–2 mg PO/IM/IV q6–8h. Status epilepticus: 4 mg IV, repeat up to q5 min. Alcohol withdrawal: 10–20 mg PO/IV q1h, titrated to signs/symptoms.

Lorazepam is the benzodiazepine of choice for status epilepticus due to its rapid onset and longer duration of action compared to diazepam. In alcohol withdrawal, benzodiazepines prevent progression to delirium tremens and seizures by cross-reacting with GABA-A receptors. Symptom-triggered dosing protocols are as effective as fixed dosing.

**Q:** What are the key properties of fentanyl and midazolam used for procedural sedation?

**A:** Fentanyl: 0.5–1.0 mcg/kg IV; very short-acting narcotic, complication = apnea. Midazolam: 1–5 mg IV as starting dose, titrate PRN; short-acting benzodiazepine, complication = apnea (especially when used with a narcotic). Often used together for procedural sedation.

The combination of opioid + benzodiazepine provides synergistic analgesia and sedation at lower doses of each drug. The primary risk is respiratory depression requiring close monitoring with pulse oximetry and capnography. Reversal agents: naloxone for opioid, flumazenil for benzodiazepine.

**Q:** What is the approach to dizziness in the ED, and what are the four types to distinguish?

**A:** Four types: 1) Vertigo (room spinning — vestibular), 2) Lightheadedness (disconnected from environment — psychogenic/hyperventilation), 3) Presyncope (almost blacking out — cardiovascular), 4) Dysequilibrium (unstable/off-balance — neurological/cerebellar). Always rule out stroke as cause of dizziness.

Distinguishing dizziness type guides workup. Vertigo is further classified as peripheral (BPPV, labyrinthitis, Meniere's) vs. central (stroke, MS, tumor). Central vertigo red flags: direction-changing nystagmus, inability to stand, other neurological signs, new severe headache. HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) distinguishes central from peripheral vertigo.

## Toxicology & Overdose

**Q:** A 22-year-old presents after intentional overdose of unknown pills. GCS 13, miosis, RR 8, oxygen saturation 88%. What is the toxidrome and treatment?

**A:** Opioid toxidrome (miosis, respiratory depression, altered consciousness); administer naloxone 0.4-2 mg IV/IM/intranasal, support airway with BVM, titrate to adequate respiration (not full reversal)

ACEP/CAEP toxicology guidelines: opioid toxidrome is life-threatening primarily through respiratory depression. Naloxone is short-acting — monitor for re-narcotization (especially with long-acting opioids or fentanyl). May need naloxone infusion. Poison Control Centre: 1-800-268-9017 (Ontario) or regional numbers.

**Q:** What are the key toxidromes and their distinguishing features?

**A:** Opioid: miosis, bradypnea, sedation; Sympathomimetic: mydriasis, tachycardia, hyperthermia, diaphoresis; Anticholinergic: mydriasis, dry skin, hyperthermia, urinary retention, ileus, confusion (mad as hatter, hot as hare, dry as bone, blind as bat, red as beet); Cholinergic: SLUDGE/DUMBELS; Serotonin: clonus, hyperreflexia, hyperthermia, agitation

ACLS toxicology: matching clinical features to toxidrome guides antidote selection and management. Anticholinergic toxidrome (antihistamines, TCAs, atropine) treated with physostigmine for severe cases. Cholinergic (organophosphate): atropine + pralidoxime. Serotonin syndrome: cyproheptadine + benzodiazepines.

**Q:** A patient with acetaminophen overdose presents 4 hours after ingestion. Serum acetaminophen level is 250 mg/L. What is the management?

**A:** Plot level on Rumack-Matthew nomogram; if above treatment line: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) IV or oral; if within 4 hours: activated charcoal 1 g/kg (if patient cooperative)

NAC replenishes glutathione, preventing toxic NAPQI from causing hepatotoxicity. Most effective if started within 8 hours. IV NAC: 150 mg/kg over 1h, then 50 mg/kg over 4h, then 100 mg/kg over 16h (21h regimen) — anaphylactoid reactions in 15-20% (managed by slowing infusion, diphenhydramine).

**Q:** A patient on TCAs (tricyclic antidepressants) presents with hypotension, QRS >120 ms, and seizures. What is the first-line antidote?

**A:** Sodium bicarbonate 1-2 mEq/kg IV bolus for QRS >100 ms or hemodynamic instability from TCA toxicity

TCA toxicity causes sodium channel blockade (wide QRS), alpha blockade (hypotension), and antimuscarinic effects. Sodium bicarbonate (target serum pH 7.45-7.55) reverses sodium channel blockade. Avoid physostigmine (can worsen conduction) and flumazenil (lowers seizure threshold). Intralipid for refractory TCA toxicity.

**Q:** What are the features of serotonin syndrome and how does it differ from NMS?

**A:** Serotonin syndrome: ACUTE onset, hyperreflexia, clonus (especially in lower limbs), hyperthermia, agitation, diarrhea — caused by excess serotonergic stimulation; NMS: GRADUAL onset over days, lead-pipe rigidity, no clonus, no diarrhea

Serotonin syndrome causes: SS + MAOI, two serotonergic agents (SSRI + tramadol, SSRI + triptans, SSRI + fentanyl). Treatment: remove precipitating agent, benzodiazepines for agitation/hyperthermia, cyproheptadine (serotonin antagonist) for moderate-severe cases, active cooling.

**Q:** What is the management of digoxin toxicity?

**A:** Digoxin toxicity: nausea, visual changes (yellow-green halos), bradycardia, AV block, hyperkalemia; antidote: digoxin-specific antibody fragments (DigiFab); dose: 10 vials (acute), calculate based on serum level for chronic

Digoxin toxicity triggers: renal failure, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, drug interactions (amiodarone, verapamil, macrolides). Hypokalemia potentiates digoxin toxicity. Avoid calcium gluconate in digoxin toxicity (may worsen cardiac effects). DigiFab reverses toxicity within 30 minutes.

**Q:** How is organophosphate (pesticide) poisoning treated?

**A:** Large doses of atropine (1-4 mg IV q5-10 min until bronchospasm resolves and secretions dry) + pralidoxime (2-PAM) 1-2 g IV (within 24-48h — reactivates acetylcholinesterase); decontaminate skin, protect rescuers

Organophosphate poisoning: cholinergic toxidrome (SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis) + CNS effects (seizures). Pralidoxime works best if given early before enzyme aging. Benzodiazepines for seizures. Atropine (not glycopyrrolate) penetrates CNS for CNS effects.

**Q:** What is the antidote for benzodiazepine overdose and when should it be used?

**A:** Flumazenil (Romazicon) is the antidote but rarely used — can precipitate seizures (in chronic BZD users or mixed TCA/BZD overdose), and has short half-life (re-sedation common); reserved for procedural sedation reversal

CAEP toxicology: flumazenil is generally NOT recommended for benzodiazepine overdose in the ED due to seizure risk, especially in patients with chronic BZD dependence or suspected co-ingestion with TCA or other proconvulsants. Supportive care (airway management) is preferred.

**Q:** A patient has methanol poisoning. What are the clinical features and treatment?

**A:** Methanol: latent period 12-24h, then anion gap metabolic acidosis, visual disturbances (snowfield vision), fundoscopy shows optic disc hyperemia; treat with fomepizole (preferred) or ethanol to block alcohol dehydrogenase; hemodialysis for severe toxicity

Methanol → formaldehyde → formic acid (causes optic nerve toxicity and metabolic acidosis). Fomepizole (4-MP) blocks alcohol dehydrogenase without sedation. Folinic acid enhances formate elimination. Hemodialysis if: pH <7.15, visual symptoms, serum methanol >15 mmol/L.

**Q:** What is the treatment for iron overdose?

**A:** Iron overdose: abdominal pain, vomiting (may have bloody), anion gap metabolic acidosis (Phases I-IV); antidote: deferoxamine IV (chelates free iron) for: symptomatic toxicity, serum iron >90 μmol/L, anion gap acidosis

Iron toxicity phase: I (0-6h: GI symptoms), II (6-24h: apparent improvement), III (12-48h: systemic toxicity — shock, acidosis), IV (2-5 days: hepatic failure), V (chronic: GI scarring). Deferoxamine chelates iron → ferrioxamine → excreted in urine (vin rosé urine color).

**Q:** What is the management of cyanide poisoning?

**A:** Cyanide poisoning (house fires with synthetic materials, nitroprusside toxicity): hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit) 5g IV is antidote — also sodium nitrite + sodium thiosulfate as alternative

Cyanide inhibits cytochrome c oxidase → cellular hypoxia → lactic acidosis. Signs: high anion gap metabolic acidosis, cherry red skin (classic), normal PaO2 despite profound toxicity. Hydroxocobalamin chelates cyanide to form cyanocobalamin. 100% O2 to increase O2 delivery.

**Q:** What is the role of activated charcoal in overdose management?

**A:** Activated charcoal 1 g/kg (max 50g) within 1-2 hours of ingestion for most oral overdoses (exceptions: iron, lithium, alcohols, hydrocarbons, caustics — not adsorbed by charcoal); risk: aspiration if airway not protected

ACEP toxicology: activated charcoal is most effective within 1 hour. After 2 hours, benefit is much less. Contraindications: unprotected airway, caustic ingestion, ileus, bowel obstruction. Multi-dose activated charcoal for salicylates, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, theophylline.

**Q:** A patient has severe metabolic acidosis and high anion gap after ingestion of antifreeze. What is the diagnosis and management?

**A:** Ethylene glycol poisoning; antidote: fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV bolus (preferred) or ethanol; aggressive IV hydration; hemodialysis for: pH <7.3, renal failure, serum ethylene glycol >8 mmol/L

Ethylene glycol → glycolic acid → oxalic acid → calcium oxalate crystals (damage renal tubules). Urine: oxalate crystals (monohydrate and dihydrate), Woods lamp fluorescence if fluorescein in antifreeze. Aggressive treatment to prevent permanent renal failure.

**Q:** What are the features and management of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)?

**A:** LAST: CNS (metallic taste, perioral tingling, seizures, coma) and cardiovascular toxicity (arrhythmias, cardiac arrest); treat with 20% lipid emulsion (Intralipid 1.5 mL/kg IV bolus) + CPR if arrest

Lipid rescue (Intralipid) is the specific antidote for LAST — sequesters lipophilic local anesthetic in lipid sink. Prevention: aspirate before injection, slow injection rate, use lowest effective dose. Bupivacaine is the most cardiotoxic local anesthetic. Max dose epinephrine-containing lidocaine: 7 mg/kg.

**Q:** What is the GHB (gamma-hydroxybutyrate) toxidrome and management?

**A:** GHB: date rape drug; CNS and respiratory depression mimicking opioids, but NOT reversed by naloxone; management: supportive (airway, O2), short-duration (1-4h); agitation: benzodiazepines

GHB has a very steep dose-response curve — small dose increase causes sudden profound coma. Patients may appear deeply unconscious then wake up abruptly. Distinguish from opioids (naloxone ineffective) and alcohol (shorter duration). GHB withdrawal (chronic users): similar to alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal.

**Q:** What are the classic toxidromes?

**A:** Anticholinergic: hot/dry/red/blind/mad. Cholinergic: DUMBELS (diarrhea/urination/miosis/bronchospasm/emesis/lacrimation/salivation). Sympathomimetic: HTN/tachycardia/mydriasis/diaphoresis. Opioid: miosis/respiratory depression/decreased LOC

Serotonin syndrome: clonus, hyperthermia, agitation (vs NMS: rigidity). Antidotes: naloxone(opioid), flumazenil(benzo-caution), N-acetylcysteine(acetaminophen), fomepizole(methanol/EG).

**Q:** What is the acetaminophen overdose protocol?

**A:** Rumack-Matthew nomogram at 4h post-ingestion. NAC if above treatment line. NAC: 150mg/kg over 1h, then 50mg/kg over 4h, then 100mg/kg over 16h (21h protocol)

Toxic dose >150mg/kg. AST/ALT may be normal initially. NAC most effective within 8h but beneficial even late. Check INR, creatinine, lactate. King's College Criteria for transplant referral.

**Q:** Name the 5 classic toxidromes with distinguishing features.

**A:** Sympathomimetic: tachy, HTN, diaphoresis, mydriasis. Anticholinergic: tachy, DRY hot skin, mydriasis, urinary retention. Cholinergic: SLUDGE-BBB. Opioid: miosis, resp depression. Sedative-hypnotic: CNS depression

Key difference anticholinergic vs sympathomimetic: DRY skin vs diaphoresis. Serotonin syndrome overlaps sympathomimetic but has clonus/hyperreflexia. Toxidrome recognition directs empiric antidote therapy.

**Q:** What is the antidote, dosing, and decision criteria for acetaminophen overdose?

**A:** NAC: IV protocol 150/50/100 mg/kg over 21h. Treat if level above Rumack-Matthew treatment line (150 mcg/mL at 4h) or staggered ingestion with any detectable level + ALT elevation

Most effective within 8h but beneficial up to 24h+. Draw 4h level (earlier level uninterpretable). In Canada IV NAC preferred for liver failure. King College Criteria predict need for transplant in fulminant failure.

**Q:** How is salicylate toxicity managed?

**A:** Activated charcoal if <2h. IV NaHCO3 targeting urine pH 7.5-8 and serum pH 7.45-7.55. Hemodialysis for level >100 mg/dL, renal failure, cerebral edema, or clinical deterioration

CRITICAL: do NOT intubate unless absolutely necessary - losing hyperventilatory compensation causes rapid acidemia and cardiovascular collapse. Correct hypokalemia before alkalinization (K+ must be adequate for renal HCO3 retention). Mixed resp alkalosis + met acidosis is classic.

**Q:** What are the signs and management of TCA overdose?

**A:** Wide QRS >100ms, R in aVR >3mm, anticholinergic signs, seizures, hypotension. Treat: NaHCO3 1-2 mEq/kg IV boluses until QRS <100ms. Intralipid for refractory CV toxicity

Sodium channel blockade causes QRS widening. Alkalinization increases protein binding of drug and overcomes sodium channel block. Avoid phenytoin and class IA/IC antiarrhythmics. Seizures: benzodiazepines only.

**Q:** When is flumazenil contraindicated in benzodiazepine overdose?

**A:** Chronic BZD use (seizure risk), co-ingestion with proconvulsant (TCA, cocaine), unknown ingestion, seizure disorder. Half-life shorter than most BZDs causing re-sedation

In practice flumazenil is rarely used in overdose due to seizure risk. Supportive care with airway protection is preferred. If used: 0.2mg IV, repeat q1min to max 3mg. Main role: reversal of procedural sedation with known short-acting BZD.

**Q:** How is toxic alcohol (methanol/ethylene glycol) poisoning managed?

**A:** Fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV loading (blocks alcohol dehydrogenase). Hemodialysis for level >50 mg/dL, visual symptoms (methanol), renal failure (EG), or severe acidosis

Early: elevated osmol gap. Late: anion gap metabolic acidosis as parent metabolized to toxic metabolites (formic acid causes blindness in methanol, oxalic acid causes renal failure in EG). Adjuncts: folate for methanol, thiamine/pyridoxine for EG.

**Q:** Describe the management of organophosphate poisoning.

**A:** Atropine 2-4 mg IV doubled q5min until secretions dry (no maximum dose). Pralidoxime 1-2g IV over 30 min. Full decontamination with PPE

Atropine treats muscarinic effects only - titrate to secretion drying NOT heart rate. Pralidoxime reactivates AChE before permanent aging (give within 24-48h). Nicotinic effects (fasciculations, paralysis) only respond to pralidoxime. PPE essential to prevent secondary exposure.

**Q:** What is the systematic approach to the unknown overdose patient?

**A:** ABCs, check glucose, naloxone 0.04mg IV titrate up, ECG (QRS and QTc), acetaminophen + salicylate levels, osmol gap, VBG/lactate, consider charcoal if <1-2h with protected airway

Wide QRS: consider TCA, class I antiarrhythmics (treat with NaHCO3). Long QTc: multiple drug classes. Elevated osmol gap: toxic alcohols. Thiamine before glucose in malnourished/alcoholic patients.

**Q:** What are the indications, dosing, and limitations of activated charcoal?

**A:** 1 g/kg (max 50g) PO within 1-2h of adsorbable substance. Not adsorbed: iron, lithium, alcohols, heavy metals, potassium (PHAILS). CI: unprotected airway, caustics, hydrocarbons

Multi-dose AC (MDAC q4h) enhances elimination of carbamazepine, dapsone, phenobarbital, quinine, theophylline via GI dialysis. Whole bowel irrigation (PEG-ELS) for sustained-release, iron, body packers.

**Q:** How is beta-blocker overdose managed?

**A:** Glucagon 5-10mg IV bolus then 1-5mg/h (first-line). High-dose insulin: 1 U/kg bolus then 1-10 U/kg/h with dextrose. Intralipid for lipophilic agents. Vasopressors as adjuncts

Glucagon bypasses beta-receptors via own receptor-cAMP pathway. High-dose insulin improves inotropy. Signs: bradycardia, hypotension, hypoglycemia (except with sotalol), bronchospasm, seizures (propranolol). ECMO as rescue for refractory cardiogenic shock.

**Q:** How is calcium channel blocker overdose managed?

**A:** Calcium: CaCl 1-3g or Ca gluconate 3-6g IV. High-dose insulin euglycemia: 1U/kg bolus then 1-10 U/kg/h. Vasopressors. Intralipid. Consider ECMO

Most lethal cardiovascular poisoning. Hyperglycemia at presentation predicts severity and good insulin response. Maintain glucose 8-12 mmol/L with dextrose infusion during high-dose insulin. Methylene blue emerging for refractory vasoplegia.

**Q:** How do serotonin syndrome and neuroleptic malignant syndrome differ in presentation and management?

**A:** SS: rapid onset hours, clonus, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, diarrhea. Rx: cyproheptadine. NMS: onset days, lead-pipe rigidity, hyperthermia, normal/decreased reflexes. Rx: dantrolene, bromocriptine

SS: excess serotonin (SSRI+MAOI, SSRI+tramadol, linezolid combinations). NMS: dopamine blockade (antipsychotics). Both: elevated CK, AMS, autonomic instability. Key differentiator: clonus/hyperreflexia (SS) vs rigidity (NMS).

**Q:** What is the management of acute opioid overdose including dosing and monitoring?

**A:** Naloxone 0.04mg IV initially, titrate q2-3min to RR >12 (not full consciousness). Observe for re-sedation. Infusion: 2/3 of effective bolus dose per hour for long-acting opioids

Community naloxone: intranasal 4mg or IM 0.4mg. Titrate to respiratory effort to avoid precipitating withdrawal. Fentanyl may need multiple doses. Canada Good Samaritan Drug Overdose Act provides legal protection for calling 911.

**Q:** What are the features and management of lithium toxicity?

**A:** Mild (1.5-2.5): hold + IV NS. Moderate (2.5-3.5): consider HD. Severe (>3.5 or symptomatic): emergent HD. Signs: tremor, ataxia, confusion, seizures, arrhythmias. Charcoal NOT effective

Narrow therapeutic index 0.6-1.2 mEq/L. Toxicity potentiated by dehydration, NSAIDs, ACEi, thiazides. Chronic toxicity more dangerous at lower levels than acute. SILENT syndrome: irreversible neurological damage post-toxicity.

**Q:** Describe the stages and management of iron overdose.

**A:** Stage I (0-6h): GI hemorrhage. II (6-24h): apparent improvement. III (12-48h): systemic shock. IV (2-4d): hepatic necrosis. V (2-6wk): GI scarring. Rx: WBI + deferoxamine 15mg/kg/h for level >500

Charcoal does not adsorb iron. Deferoxamine chelates free iron producing vin rose urine. AXR may show radiopaque pills. Stop deferoxamine at 24h (ARDS risk). Serum iron >350 at 4-6h = significant toxicity.

**Q:** Naloxone reverses opioid toxicity by competing with morphine at the mu-opioid receptor. What type of antagonism is this, and can it be overcome?

**A:** Naloxone is a reversible competitive antagonist. Its effect can be overcome by increasing concentrations of the agonist (morphine), or clinically, by repeated naloxone dosing since it has a shorter half-life than many opioids.

Reversible competitive antagonists bind non-covalently to the active receptor site and can be displaced by high agonist concentrations. This contrasts with irreversible antagonists that form covalent bonds and cannot be displaced.

**Q:** What are the universal antidotes given in coma management (mnemonic DONT)?

**A:** D — D50W (50 mL IV) if hypoglycemic on POCT; O — Oxygen if needed; N — Naloxone 0.4 mg IV (up to 10 mg) if opiate overdose suspected; T — Thiamine 100 mg IV if history of EtOH or malnourished.

Thiamine must be given BEFORE or WITH glucose in alcoholic or malnourished patients to prevent precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine-dependent glucose metabolism). Naloxone may require repeated dosing given its short half-life relative to opioids like methadone or fentanyl. CT head is required for all suspected structural coma.

**Q:** What pupil findings are caused by opioid toxicity vs. anticholinergic toxicity in coma?

**A:** Opioids (e.g., heroin, fentanyl): constricted (miotic) pupils — EXCEPT meperidine which causes dilated pupils. Anticholinergic agents (e.g., atropine, tricyclic antidepressants): dilated (mydriatic) pupils.

Pupil examination is a critical part of coma assessment. Cholinergic agents (e.g., organophosphates) also cause constricted pupils. Dilated pupils in coma can be caused by anoxia, anticholinergics, cocaine, methanol, hallucinogens, or serotonin syndrome. Constricted pupils in coma strongly suggest opioid toxicity (exception: meperidine).

**Q:** What is the treatment of choice for toxic alcohol (methanol or ethylene glycol) poisoning, and what is the alternative?

**A:** Fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV bolus is the treatment of choice. Alternatively, 10% ethanol IV bolus and infusion to achieve a blood level of 22 mmol/L (ethanol loading may also be done PO). Urgent hemodialysis may also be required.

Fomepizole and ethanol both inhibit alcohol dehydrogenase, blocking conversion of toxic alcohols to their toxic metabolites. Fomepizole is preferred due to its predictable pharmacokinetics and fewer side effects compared to ethanol. Hemodialysis removes the toxic alcohol and its metabolites directly.

**Q:** How does the anion gap and osmolar gap change as toxic alcohol (methanol/ethylene glycol) is metabolized?

**A:** Initially there is a HIGH osmolar gap with a normal anion gap. As the toxic alcohol is metabolized, the osmolar gap drops and an anion gap metabolic acidosis develops. Co-ingested ethanol is protective by competing for alcohol dehydrogenase.

The parent toxic alcohol contributes to the osmolar gap (osmotically active but not ionized). As it is metabolized to acidic metabolites (formate from methanol, oxalate/glycolate from ethylene glycol), the osmolar gap decreases and the anion gap rises due to accumulation of these organic acids.

**Q:** Which adjunct vitamins are used in methanol versus ethylene glycol poisoning, and why?

**A:** Folic acid for methanol (helps convert formate to CO2/H2O). Pyridoxine and thiamine for ethylene glycol (help divert oxalate metabolism to less toxic metabolites). Both reduce conversion to active toxic metabolites.

Folate-dependent pathway converts formate (methanol's toxic metabolite) to CO2. Pyridoxine and thiamine shift ethylene glycol metabolism away from oxalate production toward less toxic products, reducing risk of acute tubular necrosis and crystalluria.

**Q:** What electrolyte abnormalities are commonly associated with chronic alcohol use?

**A:** Hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, hypocalcemia, hypoglycemia, and hypokalemia.

Chronic alcohol use impairs renal tubular reabsorption of magnesium and phosphate. Poor nutritional intake combined with vomiting and diarrhea contribute to multiple electrolyte deficiencies. Hypoglycemia results from impaired gluconeogenesis and glycogen depletion.

**Q:** What criteria must be met before discharging an intoxicated alcohol patient from the ED?

**A:** Before discharge: stable vital signs, can walk unassisted, and is fully oriented. Also explore need for social services, housing, addictions services, or detox program, and ensure the patient can obtain prescribed medications and complete follow-up.

Alcohol intoxication requires clinical sobriety before safe discharge. Ongoing social determinants of health should be addressed at each ED visit as an opportunity for intervention. Patients with methanol or ethylene glycol ingestion must be admitted.

**Q:** What is the disposition for a patient who overdosed on TCAs (tricyclic antidepressants) with sinus tachycardia but no other ECG abnormalities?

**A:** Sinus tachycardia alone with TCA overdose warrants observation in the ED and consultation with a poison centre. If asymptomatic with no clinical signs of intoxication after proper decontamination and no ECG abnormalities: 6 hours of ED observation is adequate. Prolonged/delayed cardiotoxicity warrants admission to a monitored bed.

TCAs can cause delayed cardiotoxicity (QRS widening, ventricular arrhythmias) due to sodium channel blockade. The 6-hour observation window is based on the time needed for peak toxicity to manifest. QRS >100 ms or right axis deviation are high-risk signs warranting sodium bicarbonate and ICU admission.

**Q:** What are the naloxone dosing guidelines for opioid overdose in the ED, including for cardiorespiratory arrest?

**A:** Administer naloxone IV in incremental doses 0.2–1 mg. In cardiorespiratory arrest following possible opioid overdose: give minimum 2 mg naloxone. If a naloxone infusion is needed (ICU): start at 2/3 of the minimum effective bolus dose per hour. Admit and observe for 24 hours; offer a naloxone kit.

Incremental naloxone dosing avoids precipitating acute opioid withdrawal and agitation in dependent patients. The infusion rule (2/3 of effective bolus dose/hour) maintains adequate reversal. Naloxone kits for take-home harm reduction are standard of care in Canada.

**Q:** What is the role of thiamine in the ED, and when is caution needed?

**A:** Thiamine 100 mg IV/IM initially, then 50–100 mg IM/IV/PO once daily. Used to treat or prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy (in alcohol-dependent patients and malnourished patients). Caution: use in pregnancy.

Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia) results from thiamine deficiency, commonly seen in chronic alcoholics. Thiamine must be given BEFORE glucose in at-risk patients, as glucose administration can precipitate acute Wernicke's by depleting thiamine stores. IV formulation has a small risk of anaphylaxis.

**Q:** What is the dosing and key indication for activated charcoal in the ED?

**A:** Activated charcoal: 30–100 g PO in 250 mL water for poisoning/overdose. Efficacy and safety are case-dependent and are a source of debate; most effective within 1 hour of ingestion.

Activated charcoal adsorbs many drugs in the GI tract, preventing absorption. It is contraindicated for hydrocarbons, caustics, metals (iron, lithium), and alcohols (poorly adsorbed). It should not be used in patients with impaired airway reflexes due to aspiration risk. Multi-dose activated charcoal may enhance elimination of some drugs (theophylline, carbamazepine).

**Q:** What are the indications for admission of oral hypoglycemic agent overdose patients?

**A:** Admit ALL patients for a minimum of 24 hours if hypoglycemic, and 12 hours after the last octreotide dose. Observe asymptomatic patients for at least 8 hours.

Sulfonylureas and other oral hypoglycemic agents cause delayed and prolonged hypoglycemia due to ongoing insulin stimulation. Octreotide (somatostatin analogue) is used to suppress endogenous insulin secretion. The prolonged observation period accounts for the drug's duration of action and risk of recurrent hypoglycemia after initial treatment.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity, what is the timeline of presentation, and how is it treated?

**A:** Mechanism: high doses saturate glucuronidation and sulfation pathways → reactive metabolite NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine) forms → covalently binds hepatocyte membranes. Timeline: 0–24h: N/V (within 4–12h of overdose); 24–48h: asymptomatic but ongoing hepatic necrosis (rising transaminases); >48h: continued necrosis, possible ALF. Treatment: gastric lavage/emesis if <2h, oral activated charcoal, IV or PO N-acetylcysteine (NAC/Mucomyst) — most effective within 8–10h but give regardless of time; determines treatment using Rumack-Matthew nomogram.

NAC works by replenishing glutathione stores, which is the rate-limiting molecule in NAPQI detoxification. No recorded fatal outcomes if NAC is given before transaminase elevation. Depleted glutathione (alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, anticonvulsants) lowers the toxic threshold to 4–6 g.

**Q:** What is the first-line chelation therapy for lead poisoning?

**A:** Dimercaprol (BAL) and EDTA (calcium disodium edetate) are first-line chelating agents.

Dimercaprol and EDTA bind lead and facilitate its urinary excretion. In severe cases (encephalopathy), dimercaprol is given first to mobilize lead from tissues before EDTA is added. Succimer (DMSA) is an oral alternative used in less severe pediatric cases.

## Anaphylaxis & Allergic Reactions

**Q:** A 28-year-old develops generalized urticaria, laryngeal edema, wheezing, and hypotension 10 minutes after a bee sting. Diagnosis and immediate management?

**A:** Anaphylaxis; immediate IM epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg (1:1000 solution) in outer thigh — this is the FIRST priority, not antihistamines or steroids

CAEP/WAO anaphylaxis guidelines: epinephrine is the only life-saving medication. Delay in epinephrine is associated with deaths. Antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts only (no effect on anaphylaxis course). 30-minute observation for uniphasic reactions; 4-6h for those requiring epinephrine. Prescribe epinephrine auto-injector at discharge.

**Q:** What is the Sampson criteria for diagnosing anaphylaxis?

**A:** Anaphylaxis when any one of: (1) Acute onset illness with skin/mucosal involvement + respiratory compromise or hypotension; (2) ≥2 symptoms after allergen exposure (skin, respiratory, GI, hypotension); (3) Hypotension after known allergen exposure

WAO/CAEP criteria identify 95% of anaphylaxis cases. Skin/mucosal involvement absent in ~20% of severe anaphylaxis — do not rely on urticaria for diagnosis. Monitor for biphasic reactions (recurrence 4-8h later without re-exposure, occurring in 5-20%).

**Q:** What is the dosing of IM epinephrine for anaphylaxis and why is the IM route preferred over IV?

**A:** IM: 0.3-0.5 mg of 1:1000 epinephrine in outer thigh; IM preferred over SQ (more reliable absorption); IV epinephrine only for cardiac arrest or severe refractory anaphylaxis (risk of severe arrhythmias at IM doses)

Outer thigh IM injection results in faster absorption and higher peak levels than upper arm IM or SC. IV epinephrine for anaphylaxis requires careful dose reduction and dilution (1:10,000 or 1:100,000 solution) — errors are fatal. Repeat IM epinephrine q5-15 min if needed.

**Q:** What are the triggers for anaphylaxis in Canada and which is the most common trigger in adults?

**A:** Foods (most common overall: peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, eggs, wheat); Medications (most common in adults — especially penicillin, NSAIDs, contrast media); Insect stings (bees, wasps); Latex; Exercise; Idiopathic

CAEP guidelines: food is the most common trigger in children; medications are most common in adults and elderly. Latex allergy is common in healthcare workers. Idiopathic anaphylaxis is a diagnosis of exclusion. Alpha-tryptase levels (1-2h after reaction) confirm mast cell activation.

**Q:** What are the adjunct medications used in anaphylaxis management (after epinephrine)?

**A:** IV H1 antihistamine (diphenhydramine 25-50mg), IV H2 antihistamine (ranitidine 50mg), IV corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 200mg or methylprednisolone 125mg), bronchodilator (salbutamol) for wheeze, IV fluids for hypotension

CAEP guidelines: these adjuncts do NOT replace epinephrine. Antihistamines address urticaria/itching but do NOT prevent or treat respiratory compromise or hypotension. Corticosteroids may prevent biphasic reactions (limited evidence). All patients should receive epinephrine first.

**Q:** What is biphasic anaphylaxis and who is at risk?

**A:** Biphasic anaphylaxis: recurrence of symptoms 1-72 hours (typically 4-8h) after initial resolution without further allergen exposure; occurs in 5-20% of cases; risk factors: severe initial reaction, unknown trigger, delayed epinephrine treatment

CAEP guidelines: biphasic reactions are unpredictable. Observation for 4-6 hours after epinephrine administration. Some guidelines recommend 24-hour observation for severe reactions or those with unknown triggers. Discharge criteria: hemodynamically stable, symptoms resolved, discharge with epinephrine auto-injector.

**Q:** A patient has suspected anaphylaxis to penicillin and requires urgent surgery. Is cephalosporin safe to use?

**A:** Cross-reactivity between penicillin and cephalosporins is ~1-2% (not 10% as previously thought); use with caution based on specific allergy history — desensitization protocol if penicillin is essential for surgical infection prophylaxis

Allergy Canada and CAEP guidelines: 90% of patients with self-reported penicillin allergy tolerate penicillin and cephalosporins. True immediate hypersensitivity mediated by IgE. Cross-reactivity based on R1 side chain similarity — 1st generation cephalosporins have higher cross-reactivity. Skin testing for penicillin allergy is gold standard.

**Q:** What are the discharge instructions for a patient treated for anaphylaxis?

**A:** Prescribe epinephrine auto-injector x2 (EpiPen), allergist referral, medical alert bracelet, written action plan, antihistamines and corticosteroids for mild reactions (NOT in place of epinephrine), return if symptoms recur, food/drug avoidance counseling

CAEP anaphylaxis guidelines: all patients discharged after anaphylaxis require epinephrine auto-injector prescription and training on use. Referral to allergist for allergen identification and immunotherapy (desensitization for bee venom, penicillin). Patients on beta-blockers: epinephrine may be less effective.

**Q:** What medications can interfere with the treatment of anaphylaxis?

**A:** Beta-blockers: blunt epinephrine effect (can cause severe hypertension via unopposed alpha-agonism), may require IV glucagon 1-2 mg; ACE inhibitors: increase bradykinin, worsen angioedema and hypotension

CAEP guidelines: patients on beta-blockers who develop anaphylaxis require higher epinephrine doses and/or IV glucagon (reverses beta-blockade). ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema: hereditary angioedema treatment (icatibant/ecallantide) may be needed. Pre-existing mast cell disease (mastocytosis) worsens anaphylaxis severity.

**Q:** What is hereditary angioedema (HAE) and how is it differentiated from anaphylactic angioedema?

**A:** HAE: C1 inhibitor deficiency causing recurrent angioedema without urticaria, triggered by stress/trauma/estrogens — NOT responsive to epinephrine, antihistamines, or steroids; treat with C1-INH concentrate, icatibant, or ecallantide

HAE Type I (85%): low C1-INH level and function; Type II (15%): normal level, reduced function; Type III: estrogen-sensitive, normal C1-INH. Laboratory: low C4 (consumed even between attacks), low C1-INH level/function. Laryngeal involvement can cause death. Prophylaxis: tranexamic acid, attenuated androgens, C1-INH concentrate.

**Q:** What is epinephrine dosing for anaphylaxis?

**A:** IM epinephrine 0.3-0.5mg (1:1000) in anterolateral thigh. Repeat q5-15min. Children: 0.01mg/kg (max 0.3mg)

NEVER delay epinephrine. IV epi only if refractory + IV access (1:10,000 dilution). Adjuncts: IV fluids, antihistamines, steroids, salbutamol. Observe 4-6h (biphasic reaction risk). Prescribe EpiPen on discharge.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment and correct dosing for anaphylaxis?

**A:** Epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg IM (1:1000) anterolateral thigh. Peds: 0.01 mg/kg max 0.3mg. Repeat q5-15min. NO absolute contraindication to epi in anaphylaxis

Epi IM is the ONLY first-line. IV epi 1-5 mcg/min for refractory shock only. Delay in epi is primary risk factor for death. Per Canadian Allergy and CPS guidelines always give before H1/H2 blockers or steroids.

**Q:** What are the WAO/EAACI diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis?

**A:** (1) Skin/mucosal + respiratory OR hypotension after likely allergen. (2) 2+ organ systems after likely allergen. (3) Hypotension after KNOWN allergen. Any one criterion sufficient

Skin findings absent in ~20%. Tryptase (draw within 1-2h, compare baseline at >24h) confirms mast cell degranulation. Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis - never delay treatment for investigations.

**Q:** What is a biphasic reaction in anaphylaxis and what observation period is recommended?

**A:** Recurrence of symptoms 1-72h after resolution without re-exposure (1-20% incidence). Minimum observation: 4-6h. High-risk (severe, multiple epi doses, asthma): 8-12h or admit

Risk factors for biphasic: severe initial presentation, delayed epinephrine, oral allergen exposure. Methylprednisolone 1-2 mg/kg given to reduce biphasic risk (limited evidence). Discharge with 2 EpiPens and allergist referral.

**Q:** How is refractory anaphylaxis (not responding to IM epinephrine) managed?

**A:** IV epinephrine infusion 1-5 mcg/min, aggressive IVF 1-2L NS, nebulized salbutamol, glucagon 1-5mg IV if on beta-blocker, vasopressin for refractory hypotension

Keep patient SUPINE with legs elevated - upright positioning causes empty ventricle syndrome and cardiac arrest. Beta-blocked patients: glucagon bypasses beta receptors. Consider ECMO for refractory cardiovascular collapse.

**Q:** What mandatory prescriptions and instructions are given at anaphylaxis discharge?

**A:** 2 epinephrine auto-injectors, written action plan, MedicAlert recommendation, allergist referral, trigger avoidance education, return precautions for biphasic reaction

EpiPen Jr 0.15mg (15-30kg), EpiPen 0.3mg (>30kg). 2 devices because second dose may be needed before EMS. Teach: epi FIRST not antihistamines. Provincial formulary coverage varies across Canada.

**Q:** What are the most common anaphylaxis triggers in Canadian children vs adults?

**A:** Children: food (peanut, tree nut, milk, egg, shellfish). Adults: drugs (penicillin, NSAIDs), Hymenoptera stings. Perioperative: NMBAs. Idiopathic ~20%

Health Canada mandates labeling of 11 priority allergens. Prevalence 1-2% lifetime. Food allergy prevalence increasing. Latex allergy declining with non-latex gloves. Cofactors (exercise, alcohol, NSAIDs) lower anaphylaxis threshold.

**Q:** What is the role of antihistamines and corticosteroids in anaphylaxis?

**A:** ADJUNCTS ONLY. H1 (diphenhydramine 25-50mg) + H2 (ranitidine 50mg). Steroids (methylprednisolone 125mg IV) may reduce biphasic risk (4-6h onset). Neither replaces epinephrine

Dangerous common error: treating with antihistamines alone. H1/H2 only help skin symptoms, no effect on bronchospasm or hypotension. Steroids too slow for acute phase. Canadian guidelines emphasize epi first always.

**Q:** How does immune (IgE) anaphylaxis differ from non-immune anaphylaxis?

**A:** Immune: requires prior sensitization, IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation. Non-immune (formerly anaphylactoid): direct mast cell activation (radiocontrast, opioids, vancomycin). First-exposure possible. Treatment IDENTICAL

Anaphylactoid term being phased out. Radiocontrast premedication: prednisone 50mg at 13/7/1h before + diphenhydramine + low-osmolar contrast. Non-immune reactions dose-dependent and often less severe than IgE-mediated.

**Q:** What is food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (FDEIA)?

**A:** Anaphylaxis during exercise 2-6h after specific food (wheat, shellfish most common). Neither food alone nor exercise alone triggers it. Prevention: avoid trigger food 4-6h pre-exercise, carry EpiPen

Cofactors lowering threshold: NSAIDs, alcohol, heat, menstruation, sleep deprivation. Most common subtype of exercise-induced anaphylaxis. Diagnosis by clinical history. Supervised exercise challenge in specialized centers only.

**Q:** What is Kounis syndrome and why is it important in anaphylaxis?

**A:** Allergic acute coronary syndrome: mast cell mediators cause coronary vasospasm (Type I), plaque rupture (Type II), or stent thrombosis (Type III). Check troponin and ECG for ST changes

Occurs during anaphylaxis, especially in patients with pre-existing CAD. Treatment: epinephrine for anaphylaxis + cardiology consult for ACS management. Avoid beta-blockers in anaphylaxis context. Consider in any anaphylaxis patient with chest pain.

**Q:** What is hereditary angioedema (HAE) and what is its treatment?

**A:** C1 inhibitor deficiency causing recurrent non-urticarial swelling of skin/mucosa (eyes, lips, tongue, larynx). 80% have positive family history; 25% die from laryngeal edema. Prophylaxis: danazol or stanozolol. Acute attacks: epinephrine pen as temporizing measure.

HAE is distinguished from allergic angioedema by absence of urticaria, C4 levels low between attacks, and C1-inhibitor deficiency. Triggers include minor trauma, emotional upset, and temperature changes. C1-inhibitor concentrate, icatibant, or fresh frozen plasma are treatment options.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis?

**A:** 1. Acute onset with skin/mucosal involvement PLUS respiratory compromise OR hypotension/end-organ dysfunction. 2. Two or more of the following after exposure to a likely allergen: skin/mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, hypotension, persistent GI symptoms. 3. Hypotension after exposure to a KNOWN allergen.

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction mediated by IgE. Diagnosis is clinical and should be considered even without meeting all criteria in patients with prior anaphylaxis. Life-threatening differentials include asthma and septic shock. Angioedema alone does not meet criteria.

**Q:** What are the most common triggers for anaphylaxis?

**A:** Foods (nuts, shellfish), insect stings, drugs (penicillin, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors), radiographic contrast media, blood products, latex.

Food is the most common trigger in children, while medications are more common in adults. Latex allergy is particularly relevant in healthcare workers and patients with spina bifida. Idiopathic (exercise-induced) anaphylaxis accounts for a significant minority of cases.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis, and what is the correct dose and route?

**A:** Epinephrine (1:1000) 0.3–0.5 mg IM in the anterolateral thigh. Early epinephrine is lifesaving with no absolute contraindications. Pediatric dose: 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg.

Epinephrine reverses vasodilation (alpha-1 effect) and bronchospasm (beta-2 effect), and inhibits further mediator release (beta-2). Intramuscular administration in the anterolateral thigh achieves the fastest peak serum levels. Delay in epinephrine administration is associated with fatal outcomes.

**Q:** How should anaphylaxis be treated in a patient on beta-blockers who does not respond to epinephrine?

**A:** Glucagon, as beta-blockers prevent the chronotropic and inotropic effects of epinephrine. Glucagon acts via a non-adrenergic pathway to increase heart rate and contractility.

Beta-blockers competitively antagonize the beta-adrenergic effects of epinephrine. Glucagon bypasses the beta-receptor and directly stimulates adenylate cyclase, providing an alternative pathway for cardiovascular rescue in refractory anaphylaxis. Larger volumes of IV crystalloid are also typically required.

**Q:** What additional medications are used in severe anaphylaxis beyond epinephrine?

**A:** Antihistamine (diphenhydramine 50 mg IV or cetirizine 10 mg IV), glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone 125–250 mg IV or prednisone 40–60 mg PO), salbutamol via MDI/nebulizer for bronchospasm, large volumes of IV crystalloid, may require intubation for airway edema.

Antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjunct treatments — they do not replace epinephrine and do not act quickly enough to treat the acute reaction. Corticosteroids may help prevent the biphasic reaction (second phase up to 72 hours later). Second-generation H1 antagonists (cetirizine) are preferred as they are less sedating than diphenhydramine.

**Q:** What is the biphasic reaction in anaphylaxis, and how long should patients be observed?

**A:** A second-phase anaphylactic reaction that can occur up to 72 hours after initial resolution. Patients should be observed until symptoms fully resolve and vital signs normalize, especially after severe anaphylaxis or if >1 dose of epinephrine was required.

The biphasic reaction is thought to occur in 1–20% of anaphylaxis cases and can be as severe as the initial reaction. Current evidence does not support routine prolonged observation in all anaphylaxis cases, but local practices and severity guide observation duration. All patients need an epinephrine auto-injector on discharge.

**Q:** What are the indications and dosing for epinephrine in the ED (anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest, bradycardia)?

**A:** Anaphylaxis: 0.3–0.5 mg IM (max 1 mg/dose). Cardiac arrest (ACLS): 1 mg IV q3–5 min. Bradycardia (ACLS): 2–10 mcg/min IV infusion. Hypotension: infusion per protocol.

IM epinephrine in the anterolateral thigh is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis, providing faster absorption than subcutaneous injection. In cardiac arrest, epinephrine provides alpha-1 mediated vasoconstriction improving coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure during CPR. Dosing differences reflect different mechanisms of action needed.

## Emergency Medicine

**Q:** What are the classes of hemorrhagic shock, and at what class does blood pressure decrease?

**A:** Class I: <750 mL (<15% blood volume), normal BP. Class II: 750-1500 mL (15-30%), normal BP. Class III: 1500-2000 mL (30-40%), decreased BP, HR >120. Class IV: >2000 mL (>40%), decreased BP, HR >140. Blood pressure does not decrease until Class III (30-40% loss).

Compensatory mechanisms (vasoconstriction, tachycardia) maintain blood pressure through Classes I and II. Tachycardia is an earlier sign than hypotension. Permissive hypotension with pRBC transfusion is the management approach, with massive transfusion protocol for severe hemorrhage.

**Q:** What is the Glasgow Coma Scale, and what score constitutes a severe head injury?

**A:** GCS = Eyes (1-4) + Verbal (1-5) + Motor (1-6), total 3-15. Mild: 13-15, Moderate: 9-12, Severe: <8. Best motor response is the most prognostically useful component. Change in GCS over time is more relevant than absolute number. If intubated, score is out of 10 with T notation.

The GCS is used for trauma patients with decreased LOC. It is a good indicator of injury severity and neurosurgical prognosis. A deteriorating GCS requires immediate attention. It is less meaningful for metabolic coma.

**Q:** What are the components of the PERC rule for pulmonary embolism, and when should it be applied?

**A:** PERC criteria (score 1 for each): HR >100, O2 sat <95% on room air, prior DVT/PE history, recent trauma or surgery, hemoptysis, exogenous estrogen, clinical signs of DVT, age >=50. A score of 0/8 in a patient with low clinical suspicion effectively rules out PE without further investigation.

The PERC rule should only be applied when the clinician already has low gestalt or low Wells score for PE. It avoids unnecessary D-dimer testing in very low-risk patients. It should not be used in patients on beta-blockers or pregnant women, as these were excluded from the original study.

**Q:** What is the diagnostic triad of diabetic ketoacidosis, and what are the key initial management steps?

**A:** Triad: hyperglycemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis due to severe insulin deficiency. Initial management: (1) Rehydration with NS bolus then high-rate infusion, (2) Potassium replacement (KCl 20 mEq/L if K+ <5.5 and patient voiding), (3) Insulin infusion. Correct pseudohyponatremia (add 3 Na+ per 10 glucose above 5.5 mmol/L).

DKA is precipitated by infection, insulin omission, MI, or SGLT2 inhibitors (euglycemic DKA). Potassium must be replaced before or with insulin to avoid fatal hypokalemia. Cerebral edema is a risk in pediatric patients with overhydration.

**Q:** A patient presents 48 hours after their last alcoholic drink with confusion, agitation, hallucinations, tachycardia, and diaphoresis. What is this condition and how is it managed?

**A:** Delirium tremens (DT), occurring 3-5 days after last drink in ~5% of untreated withdrawal patients. Management: diazepam 10-20 mg IV/PO (or lorazepam if liver dysfunction) titrated using CIWA protocol until score <10. Add phenobarbital if no improvement. Give thiamine 200 mg IM/IV. Admit for monitoring. High mortality rate if untreated.

Alcohol withdrawal progresses from mild (6-8h: tremor, anxiety), to hallucinations (12-48h), to seizures (8h-2d), to DT (3-5d). The goal is early treatment with benzodiazepines to prevent seizures and DT. Thiamine must be given before dextrose to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy.

**Q:** What is the approach to a patient presenting with an acute monoarticular joint that is warm, swollen, and erythematous with fever?

**A:** Suspect septic arthritis until proven otherwise. Investigations: CBC, ESR, CRP, blood cultures, joint X-ray, bedside U/S to identify effusion, joint aspiration for culture, WBC, Gram stain, and crystals. Management: empiric IV antibiotics plus orthopedic consultation for joint decompression and drainage.

Septic arthritis is a surgical emergency requiring urgent drainage. Crystal arthropathies (gout, pseudogout) are the main differential and can be distinguished by crystal analysis of joint aspirate. Do not use allopurinol during acute gout flares as it may worsen the attack.

**Q:** An unconscious trauma patient has a unilateral dilated, non-reactive pupil. What are the most important differential diagnoses?

**A:** Focal mass lesion causing uncal herniation (most critical), epidural hematoma (especially with 'talk and die' presentation), subdural hematoma, or traumatic mydriasis from direct iris sphincter muscle damage. The patient needs immediate CT head and neurosurgical consultation.

Uncal herniation compresses CN III causing ipsilateral pupil dilation. An epidural hematoma classically presents with a lucid interval followed by rapid deterioration. Any patient with deteriorating GCS needs immediate attention and imaging.

**Q:** What is the Canadian CT Head Rule and when does it apply?

**A:** Applies to minor head injury (GCS 13-15) patients. CT required if any high-risk criteria (GCS <15 at 2h, suspected skull fracture, signs of basal skull fracture, ≥2 episodes vomiting, age ≥65) or medium-risk criteria (amnesia before impact >30 min, dangerous mechanism).

The rule does not apply to non-trauma cases, GCS <13, age <16, or patients on Coumadin/anticoagulants. High-risk criteria predict need for neurological intervention. Medium-risk criteria predict brain injury on CT.

**Q:** What are the signs of basal skull fracture?

**A:** Battle's sign (bruised mastoid process), hemotympanum, raccoon eyes (periorbital bruising), CSF rhinorrhea/otorrhea.

Basal skull fractures typically occur through the floor of the anterior cranial fossa. They can be diagnosed radiologically or clinically. NG tube insertion is contraindicated in the presence of basal skull fracture due to risk of intracranial placement.

**Q:** What is Beck's triad and what condition does it indicate?

**A:** Hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds. It indicates cardiac tamponade.

Cardiac tamponade results from pericardial fluid accumulation impairing ventricular function. Additional signs include tachycardia, tachypnea, Kussmaul's sign (increased JVP with inspiration), and pulsus paradoxus (>10mmHg BP drop with inspiration). FAST/echocardiogram confirms diagnosis.

**Q:** What is the management of tension pneumothorax?

**A:** Needle thoracostomy (large bore needle at 2nd ICS mid-clavicular line) or finger thoracostomy. Definitive management is a chest tube. This is a clinical diagnosis — do not wait for imaging.

Tension pneumothorax is caused by a one-way valve mechanism accumulating air in the pleural space. Signs include respiratory distress, tachycardia, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation away from the pneumothorax, hyperresonance, and absent breath sounds.

**Q:** What is neurogenic shock and how does it differ from hypovolemic shock?

**A:** Neurogenic shock: loss of vasomotor/SNS tone causing hypotension, bradycardia, and poikilothermia (warm, dry skin). Occurs with SCI at T6 or above. Hypovolemic shock: tachycardia, cool/clammy skin, peripheral vasoconstriction.

Neurogenic shock occurs within 30 minutes of SCI and lasts up to 6 weeks. Treatment includes fluids, atropine for bradycardia, and vasopressors. It differs from spinal shock (absence of all reflexes below injury level).

**Q:** In blunt abdominal trauma, what is the most commonly injured solid organ?

**A:** Spleen (most common), followed by liver (second most common).

Blunt trauma causes solid organ injury while penetrating trauma usually causes hollow organ or liver injury. FAST exam identifies free fluid but cannot identify specific organ injuries. CT is the most specific test but should be used with caution in hemodynamically unstable patients.

**Q:** What is the Ottawa Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH) Rule?

**A:** Investigate if ≥1 high-risk variable: age ≥40, neck pain/stiffness, witnessed LOC, onset during exertion, thunderclap headache (instantly peaking), limited neck flexion. Has 100% sensitivity for identifying SAH.

SAH presents with sudden onset 'worst headache of life' with maximum intensity within minutes. New generation CT is 100% sensitive within 6 hours of onset. LP for xanthochromia is needed if CT is normal after 6 hours.

**Q:** What are the HEART score components for chest pain risk stratification?

**A:** History (0-2 pts), ECG (0-2 pts), Age (0-2 pts), Risk factors (0-2 pts), Troponin (0-2 pts). Low risk 0-3: 0.6-1.7% MACE; intermediate 4-6: 16.6%; high 7-10: 50.1%.

The HEART score helps risk-stratify patients with chest pain in the emergency department. It incorporates clinical history, ECG findings, patient age, cardiovascular risk factors, and initial troponin levels to guide disposition decisions.

**Q:** What is Waddell's triad in pediatric pedestrian-automobile injuries?

**A:** 1) Tibia-fibula or femur fracture, 2) Truncal or abdominal injury, 3) Craniofacial injury.

Children struck by vehicles tend to have a characteristic pattern of injuries because of their smaller stature. Adults tend to be struck in lower legs, impacted against the car causing truncal injuries, and thrown to the ground causing head injury.

**Q:** What is the approach to febrile infants <28 days old?

**A:** Admit. Full septic workup (CBC, CRP/procalcitonin, blood C&S, urinalysis, urine C&S, LP based on risk stratification, ± stool C&S, ± CXR). Treat empirically with broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (ampicillin + ceftazidime or cefepime or gentamicin).

Febrile infants <28 days are at high risk for serious bacterial infection and require aggressive workup and empiric treatment. Add acyclovir if HSV is suspected and vancomycin when indicated. For ages 28-90 days, risk stratification tools (PECARN, Step-by-Step) guide management.

**Q:** What are the 4 D's of epiglottitis and what should you NOT do?

**A:** 4 D's: Drooling, Dyspnea, Dysphagia, Dysphonia (+ tripod sitting). Do NOT examine the oropharynx or agitate the patient. Call anesthesia/ENT immediately for intubation.

Epiglottitis is now rare with Hib vaccination. Caused by H. influenzae type B, it presents with high fever and rapid onset. Thumb sign on lateral neck x-ray is characteristic. After securing the airway, treat with IV fluids, antibiotics, and blood cultures.

**Q:** What is the treatment of fomepizole and when is it used?

**A:** Fomepizole 15mg/kg IV bolus is the treatment of choice for toxic alcohol ingestion (methanol and ethylene glycol). Alternative: 10% ethanol IV to achieve blood level of 22mmol/L.

Toxic alcohols initially produce a high osmolar gap that drops as the alcohol is metabolized, producing an anion gap metabolic acidosis. The goal of treatment is to block this metabolic pathway. Urgent hemodialysis may be required. Ethanol co-ingestion is protective.

**Q:** A patient with chronic alcohol use presents with AST/ALT ratio >2. What diagnosis does this suggest?

**A:** Alcoholic hepatitis. An AST/ALT ratio >2 suggests alcohol as the cause of hepatitis.

Other GI abnormalities in chronic alcohol use include gastritis (common cause of GI bleed), pancreatitis (elevated lipase), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (in cirrhosis, diagnosed by paracentesis), and variceal bleeding (treated with octreotide).

**Q:** What are the CXR features suggestive of aortic tear (mnemonic ABC WHITE)?

**A:** Abnormal aortic contour, Blurred aortic knob, Calcium sign (>6mm separation), Widened mediastinum (most consistent), Hemothorax, Indistinct aortic knuckle, Tracheal deviation to right, Esophageal (depressed left mainstem bronchus).

90% of aortic tears occur at the subclavian (near ligamentum arteriosum). Most patients die at scene. Diagnosis requires CT angiogram. Management: reduce BP and HR, classify as Type A (ascending aorta — urgent surgery) vs Type B (not ascending — medical management).

**Q:** What is the Canadian C-Spine Rule for clearing the cervical spine?

**A:** Can clear if: alert (GCS 15), stable, oriented, no paresthesias, no intoxication, no midline tenderness, no focal deficits, no painful distracting injuries, AND able to rotate neck 45° each direction without midline tenderness.

CT of the spine is the screening modality of choice (97-100% sensitivity). MRI is preferred for soft tissue injuries. The rule applies only to alert, stable trauma patients where C-spine injury is a concern. If any criteria are not met, C-spine imaging is required.

**Q:** What are the typical vs atypical features of febrile seizures?

**A:** Typical: <5 min duration, generalized, only 1 in 24 hours. Atypical: >5 min, focal features, >1 in 24 hours.

Febrile seizures occur in children age 6 months to 6 years with fever. They have a normal neurological exam afterward and no evidence of intracranial infection. There is often a positive family history. Management: treat fever and look for source of fever.

**Q:** What are the Wells' criteria used for and what investigation follows a positive result?

**A:** Wells' criteria assess clinical probability of pulmonary embolism. High/moderate probability: CT pulmonary angiogram. Low probability: D-dimer first; if negative and low pretest probability, PE is effectively ruled out.

PE presents with pleuritic chest pain (75%) and dyspnea with risk factors for VTE. Classic findings include tachycardia and hypoxemia. Management includes anticoagulation and consideration of thrombolysis for massive PE (hypotension/cardiovascular collapse).

**Q:** What is the OPEN mnemonic for managing open fractures?

**A:** Actually the mnemonic is STAND: Splint, Tetanus prophylaxis, Antibiotics (1st gen cephalosporin + aminoglycoside), Neurovascular status (before and after), Dressings (to cover wound).

Open fractures have communication between fracture site and external skin surface, increasing risk of osteomyelitis. Standard of care is definitive surgical management within 6 hours. Remove gross debris, irrigate, and cover with sterile dressing.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for tension pneumothorax?

**A:** Immediate needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line) followed by chest tube insertion.

Tension pneumothorax presents with tracheal deviation contralateral to the affected side, hypotension/shock, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds, and hyperresonance. It is a clinical diagnosis - do not wait for imaging.

## Toxicology

**Q:** A 35-year-old with acetaminophen overdose presents 6 hours after ingestion with nausea and vomiting. What is the mechanism of hepatotoxicity and the treatment?

**A:** Mechanism: high-dose acetaminophen saturates glucuronidation/sulfation pathways, leading to formation of toxic metabolite NAPQI which covalently binds hepatocyte membranes. Treatment: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) - most effective within 8-10 hours but should be given regardless of timing.

Acetaminophen toxicity requires 10-15 g in healthy individuals or 4-6 g in those with depleted glutathione (alcoholism, malnutrition). NAC promotes hepatic glutathione regeneration. No fatal outcomes recorded when NAC given before transaminase elevation.

**Q:** What is the acetaminophen overdose treatment protocol?

**A:** N-acetylcysteine (NAC) PO or IV, most effective within 8-10 hours of ingestion but should be given regardless of timing. Also: gastric lavage if <2 hours, activated charcoal. Use Rumack-Matthew nomogram for decision.

Toxic dose: 10-15g in healthy adults, 4-6g with depleted glutathione (alcohol use, malnutrition). NAC promotes hepatic glutathione regeneration. No fatal outcomes recorded if NAC given before transaminase rise. Presentation: N/V (0-24h) → asymptomatic (24-48h) → hepatic necrosis (>48h).

## Emergency Environmental

**Q:** What are the key differences between heat exhaustion and heat stroke in terms of body temperature and neurological findings?

**A:** Heat exhaustion: temp <40.5°C (often near normal), NO coma or seizures, dehydration signs (tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension). Heat stroke: temp usually >40.5°C, altered mental status, seizures, delirium, or coma.

Heat exhaustion results from volume loss due to heat stress; it may progress to heat stroke if untreated. Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency from failure of compensatory heat-shedding mechanisms. When diagnosis is uncertain, treat as heat stroke.

**Q:** What is the primary cooling method for heat stroke, and what monitoring is required to avoid complications?

**A:** Cool body temperature with water mist (spray bottle) and standing fans. Ice water immersion is also effective. Monitor body temperature closely using a rectal thermometer to avoid hypothermic overshoot.

Rapid cooling is the cornerstone of heat stroke management. Rectal thermometry is essential because surface cooling can mask ongoing hyperthermia or cause overshoot hypothermia. Airway should be secured due to seizure/aspiration risk.

**Q:** Which medications should be AVOIDED in the treatment of heat stroke and why?

**A:** Avoid beta-agonists (e.g., epinephrine), peripheral vasoconstrictors, and antipyretics (e.g., ASA). These worsen heat dissipation or are ineffective for the underlying pathophysiology.

Heat stroke hyperthermia is due to failure of thermoregulation, not prostaglandin-mediated fever, so antipyretics like ASA are ineffective. Vasoconstrictors impair cutaneous heat loss. Beta-agonists increase heat production.

**Q:** What distinguishes classical from exertional heat stroke regarding patient demographics and skin findings?

**A:** Classical: older, sedentary/immobile patients; occurs in high ambient temperature; skin is DRY and hot. Exertional: younger, more active patients; high endogenous heat production (exercise); skin is often DIAPHORETIC.

Exertional heat stroke may also be complicated by DIC, acute renal failure, rhabdomyolysis, and marked lactic acidosis — complications less common in classical heat stroke. Both present with temp >40.5°C and altered mental status.

**Q:** Define hypothermia and list its three broad etiological categories.

**A:** Hypothermia = core temperature <35°C. Causes: (1) Increased heat loss (e.g., environmental exposure); (2) Decreased heat production (e.g., endocrine disease); (3) Impaired regulation (e.g., CNS failure).

Treatment is based on re-warming and supporting cardiorespiratory function. Predisposing risk factors in Canada include ethanol use, homelessness, psychiatric disease, and older age.

**Q:** What are the temperature thresholds and key clinical findings for mild, moderate, and severe hypothermia?

**A:** Mild (32–34.9°C): tachypnea, tachycardia, ataxia, dysarthria, shivering. Moderate (28–31.9°C): loss of shivering, dysrhythmias, Osborne (J) waves on ECG, decreased LOC, combative behaviour, muscle rigidity, dilated pupils. Severe (<28°C): unresponsive, hypotension, acidemia, VFib/AFib/asystole, flaccidity, apnea.

The J (Osborne) wave on ECG is a classic finding of moderate hypothermia. As temperature drops below 30°C, cardiac irritability increases dramatically and defibrillation is less effective.

**Q:** What is the 'afterdrop' phenomenon in hypothermia management, and how is it avoided?

**A:** Afterdrop is the paradoxical decrease in core temperature when cold blood from extremities returns to the core after peripheral vasodilation from external warming. It is avoided by using active core re-warming in conjunction with active external re-warming (not external re-warming alone).

Active external re-warming (warming blankets) alone can worsen the afterdrop by vasodilating periphery before the core is warm. Active core re-warming (warmed O2, IV fluids, ECMO) prevents this phenomenon.

**Q:** What is the preferred method of active core re-warming for severe hypothermia or hypothermic cardiac arrest?

**A:** Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is the most effective and fastest method of active core re-warming. Transfer to an ECMO-capable centre as soon as possible.

ECMO avoids the afterdrop phenomenon and can simultaneously support cardiac and respiratory function. Alternative core re-warming methods include warmed humidified oxygen, IV fluids, and peritoneal/bladder/pleural irrigation with warm fluids.

**Q:** In hypothermic cardiac arrest, what is the maximum number of defibrillation attempts if core temperature is below 30°C?

**A:** Up to a maximum of 3 shocks if in VFib with core temperature <30°C. Focus remains on active core re-warming.

At temperatures <30°C, the myocardium is refractory to defibrillation and antidysrhythmics. Re-warming is the definitive treatment. Medications (vasopressors, antidysrhythmics) may not be effective at low temperatures.

**Q:** What are the grades of frostbite and what structures are involved at each grade?

**A:** Grade 1: no initial lesion (superficial). Grade 2: initial lesion on distal phalanx. Grade 3: initial lesion on intermediate and proximal phalanx. Grade 4: initial lesion on carpal/tarsal bones.

Frostbite grading is based on the extent of the initial lesion assessed at day 0 after rapid rewarming. Higher grades indicate deeper tissue injury with greater risk of amputation.

**Q:** What is the recommended rewarming protocol for frostbite and what key principle must be followed after thawing?

**A:** Immerse in 37–39°C agitated water for 10–30 min with adequate analgesia. Never allow a thawed area to re-chill or refreeze. Consider tPA and/or iloprost for severe cases with specialist advice.

Refreezing a thawed area causes far more tissue damage than the initial freeze. Rewarming is painful and requires opioid analgesia. Tetanus prophylaxis and consideration of antibiotics (high infection risk) are also required.

**Q:** What are the disposition criteria for non-fatal drowning patients in the emergency department?

**A:** Asymptomatic: discharge if no respiratory impairment, normal vitals, normal mentation. Symptomatic but never hypoxemic: discharge after 6–8h observation if resolving symptoms, normal vitals, normal mentation. CNS symptoms or hypoxemia: admit. Severe hypoxemia, decreased LOC: ICU.

Delayed pulmonary complications can occur in drowning; hence observation even for initially 'well-appearing' symptomatic patients. CXR is only indicated for abnormal lung exam or abnormal O2 saturation.

**Q:** In non-fatal drowning, when should prophylactic antibiotics be given and for what organisms?

**A:** Prophylactic antibiotics are NOT necessary for most drowning cases. They should be given if the water was contaminated or if hot tub drowning occurred (cover Pseudomonas).

Most drowning-related pneumonia is treated when it manifests. Contaminated water (sewage, hot tubs) increases risk of Pseudomonas and other gram-negative organisms, warranting prophylaxis.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for non-fatal drowning in the Canadian context?

**A:** Inability to swim effectively, missing/inadequate pool fencing, improper/absent personal flotation device use, risk-taking behaviour, alcohol and substance use, prescription medications causing drowsiness, inadequate supervision, acute medical issues (e.g., seizures), occupational/recreational water activities.

Drowning is most common in children <4 years and teenagers. Prevention strategies targeting pool fencing, supervision, and PFD use are key public health measures in Canada.

## Emergency Burns

**Q:** What is the Parkland formula for burn fluid resuscitation and how is it administered?

**A:** Ringer's lactate 4 cc/kg/% BSA burned. Give half in the first 8 hours, half in the next 16 hours. Target urine output 40–50 cc/h (0.5 cc/kg/h). BSA percentages differ in pediatric patients.

The Parkland formula guides fluid replacement for 2nd and 3rd degree burns. Urine output is the best measure of resuscitation adequacy. Diuretics should be avoided as they confound assessment.

**Q:** What are the four burn depths and their key clinical characteristics?

**A:** Superficial (1st°): epidermis only, painful, e.g. sunburn. Superficial partial (2nd°): epidermis + superficial dermis, blisters, very painful. Deep partial (2nd°): involves hair follicles/sebaceous glands, white-yellow dermis, absent sensation. Full thickness (3rd°): all dermal layers, pale/insensate/charred. Deep 4th°: involves fat, muscle, bone.

Depth determines management and need for grafting. Sensation is a key differentiator: superficial burns are painful, deep partial and full thickness burns have absent/reduced sensation. Rule of Nines does not include 1st degree burns.

**Q:** What are the indications for escharotomy in burn patients?

**A:** Pain, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia, pulselessness, decreased/absent oximetry signals, increased compartment pressures >30 mmHg (limb eschar), increased intraocular pressures >30 mmHg (peri-orbital eschar). Chest eschar: increased airway pressures, decreased tidal volumes, decreased preload, tachycardia.

Circumferential burns of limbs or chest create inelastic eschar that can cause compartment syndrome or restrict breathing. Escharotomy (incision through eschar) releases the constriction. Fasciotomy may also be required.

**Q:** What burn characteristics require hospital admission?

**A:** 2nd degree burns >10% BSA or any significant 3rd degree burns; 2nd degree burns on face, hands, feet, perineum, or across major joints; electrical burns; chemical burns; inhalation injury; burn victims with chronic medical conditions or immunosuppression.

These criteria reflect high risk for functional/cosmetic complications, infection, and systemic compromise. Specialized burn units provide wound care, fluid management, and surgical expertise.

## Emergency Toxicology

**Q:** What are the classic clinical signs and toxin for cyanide poisoning, and what is the treatment?

**A:** Classic signs: cherry red skin, bitter almond odour (often absent clinically). High lactate suggests cyanide toxicity. Treatment: hydroxocobalamin 70 mg/kg IV over 15 min (max 5 g), up to two doses. Obtain labs immediately as hydroxocobalamin alters lab results.

Cyanide poisoning should be suspected in all fire victims, especially with elevated lactate disproportionate to clinical picture. CO poisoning often co-exists. Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit) is the preferred antidote in Canada.

**Q:** Why does pulse oximetry give falsely normal readings in carbon monoxide poisoning?

**A:** Pulse oximetry cannot differentiate carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so SpO2 reads falsely normal or near-normal. Co-oximetry or direct carboxyhemoglobin measurement is required.

This is a critical clinical pearl: a patient with CO poisoning appears 'normal' on standard pulse oximetry. ABG with co-oximetry is required to detect carboxyhemoglobin. Treatment is 100% O2 ± hyperbaric O2.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic 'DONT' in toxicology universal antidotes, and what does each letter stand for?

**A:** D = Dextrose (glucose); O = Oxygen; N = Naloxone; T = Thiamine. These are the universal antidotes given empirically to any undifferentiated overdose patient.

Thiamine MUST be given BEFORE dextrose to prevent precipitating Wernicke's encephalopathy in thiamine-deficient patients. Naloxone reverses opioid toxicity. Oxygen treats hypoxia. Dextrose treats hypoglycemia.

**Q:** What is the naloxone dosing protocol for an adult opioid overdose, and why is a maintenance infusion often required?

**A:** Adults: 0.05 or 0.1 mg IV with stepwise escalation PRN. During cardiac arrest: 2 mg IV/IM q3 min up to max 12 mg. Maintenance infusion at 2/3 of the initial effective rousing dose per hour, because naloxone's half-life (30–80 min) is shorter than most opioids.

Stepwise delivery minimizes precipitation of acute opioid withdrawal in dependent patients. Without a maintenance infusion, re-narcotization is likely as naloxone wears off before the opioid does.

**Q:** What is the 'GOLDMARK' mnemonic for causes of elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis?

**A:** G = Glycols (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol); O = Oxoproline (acetaminophen metabolite); L = L-lactate; D = D-lactate; M = Methanol; A = ASA; R = Renal failure; K = Ketoacidosis (DKA, EtOH, starvation).

The anion gap = Na – Cl – HCO3 (normal <12 mmol/L). Elevated AG metabolic acidosis from toxic causes most commonly involves methanol, ethylene glycol, ASA, and metformin-associated lactic acidosis.

**Q:** What is the 'MAEDIE' mnemonic for causes of elevated osmolar gap, and what is the formula for osmolar gap?

**A:** M = Methanol; A = Acetone; E = Ethanol; D = Diuretics (glycerol, mannitol, sorbitol); I = Isopropanol; E = Ethylene glycol. Osmolar gap = [2×Na + Glu + Urea] – measured osmolality. Normal <10. A normal osmolar gap does NOT rule out toxic alcohol ingestion.

Toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol) elevate the osmolar gap early before metabolism to their toxic metabolites. As metabolism proceeds, the osmolar gap normalizes but the anion gap rises. A normal osmolar gap does NOT exclude toxic alcohol ingestion.

**Q:** What ECG findings are associated with TCA overdose, and what is the acute treatment for wide QRS or seizures?

**A:** TCA overdose: wide QRS complex. Treatment for wide QRS or seizures: sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) IV bolus. Also consider intralipid therapy, cardiac and hypotension support, seizure control.

TCAs cause sodium channel blockade leading to QRS widening, which predicts seizures and ventricular arrhythmias. NaHCO3 alkalinizes plasma and improves sodium channel function. Flumazenil is contraindicated in combined TCA and benzodiazepine overdose.

**Q:** What are the key features of the anticholinergic toxidrome and the mnemonic to remember them?

**A:** 'Hot as a hare' (hyperthermia), 'Blind as a bat' (dilated pupils), 'Dry as a bone' (dry skin), 'Red as a beet' (vasodilation/flushing), 'Mad as a hatter' (agitation/hallucinations), plus ileus, urinary retention, and tachycardia.

Common causative agents include TCAs, antihistamines (diphenhydramine), cyclobenzaprine, antipsychotics, antiparkinsonians, and belladonna alkaloids (atropine). Dry axillae is a useful clinical sign of anticholinergic toxicity.

**Q:** What is the cholinergic toxidrome and what is the mnemonic 'DUMBELS'?

**A:** D = Diaphoresis/Diarrhea/Decreased BP; U = Urination; M = Miosis; B = Bronchospasm/Bronchorrhea/Bradycardia; E = Emesis/Excitation of skeletal muscle; L = Lacrimation; S = Salivation/Seizures. Caused by organophosphates, carbamates, nerve agents, anticholinesterases.

The cholinergic toxidrome is the opposite of anticholinergic. Treatment involves 100% O2, intubation, atropine (for secretions), and pralidoxime (2-PAM) to reactivate cholinesterase. Succinylcholine has prolonged duration in organophosphate poisoning.

**Q:** What is the serotonin syndrome triad and what drug classes are implicated?

**A:** Triad: (1) Mental status changes; (2) Autonomic hyperactivity; (3) Neuromuscular hyperactivity, plus hyperthermia, diarrhea, HTN. Implicated drugs: MAOIs, TCAs, SSRIs, opiate analgesics (e.g., tramadol, meperidine), cough medicines, weight reduction medications.

Serotonin syndrome typically results from combinations of serotonergic drugs. It is distinguished from neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) by onset (faster, hours vs. days), neuromuscular findings (clonus/hyperreflexia vs. rigidity), and causative agents.

**Q:** What is the single-dose activated charcoal dose and its key contraindications?

**A:** Dose: 10 g/g drug ingested or 1 g/kg body weight. Contraindications: unprotected airway, late presentation (>1–2h post ingestion), small bowel obstruction, poor toxin adsorption.

Activated charcoal decreases toxin bioavailability by adsorption in the GI tract. Evidence for effectiveness is debated among toxicologists and risk of aspiration is significant. Its use should be discussed with Poison Control.

**Q:** What toxins are amenable to multi-dose activated charcoal, and what is the mechanism?

**A:** Carbamazepine, phenobarbital, quinine, theophylline, and toxins undergoing enterohepatic recirculation. Mechanism: draws drug back into GI tract from systemic circulation (reverse diffusion/gut dialysis), removing drug already absorbed.

Multi-dose activated charcoal (MDAC) works as 'gut dialysis' — the charcoal creates a concentration gradient that pulls drug from blood back into the bowel lumen. Typical regimens: 12.5 g q1h or 25 g q2h.

**Q:** What are the criteria for using hemodialysis in poisoning, and which specific toxins are amenable?

**A:** Criteria: high water solubility, low protein binding, low molecular weight, adequate concentration gradient, small volume of distribution, rapid plasma equilibration, or clinical deterioration despite maximal support. Toxins: methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylates, lithium, phenobarbital, chloral hydrate, theophylline, carbamazepine, valproate, methotrexate.

Hemodialysis is particularly important for methanol and ethylene glycol (prevents conversion to toxic metabolites), and for severe salicylate toxicity (intractable acidosis, end-organ damage, inability to diurese).

**Q:** What is urine alkalinization used for in toxicology, and how is it achieved?

**A:** Used for: ASA, methotrexate, phenobarbital, chlorpropamide. Mechanism: weakly acidic substances are 'ion trapped' in alkaline urine (pH >7.5), preventing reabsorption and increasing elimination. Achieve with 3 amps NaHCO3/L of D5W at 1.5× maintenance; add 20–40 mEq/L KCl if able to urinate.

Hypokalemia must be corrected for urine alkalinization to work: the kidney reabsorbs K+ in exchange for H+, acidifying the urine. Monitor serum pH and drug levels closely; K+ supplementation is often required.

**Q:** What is the treatment for severe beta-blocker or calcium channel blocker overdose?

**A:** Decontamination (activated charcoal ± whole bowel irrigation for extended-release). Then: IV glucagon, IV calcium chloride, IV high-dose insulin (with dextrose), IV lipid emulsification (intralipid). Order ECG and electrolytes.

High-dose insulin-euglycemia therapy (HIET) is increasingly used for CCB and beta-blocker overdose. Glucagon bypasses beta-receptor blockade. Lipid emulsion 'sinks' lipophilic drugs away from cardiac tissue.

**Q:** Why are beta-blockers contraindicated in acute cocaine toxicity?

**A:** Beta-blockers in cocaine toxicity cause unopposed alpha-adrenergic stimulation, worsening coronary vasospasm and hypertension. Benzodiazepines are first-line for any major cocaine side effects (agitation, hypertension, tachycardia).

Cocaine has both sympathomimetic and sodium channel blocking effects. Beta-blockade removes the compensatory beta-2 vasodilation, leaving alpha-1 vasoconstriction unopposed, which can precipitate myocardial ischemia.

**Q:** What is the antidote for digoxin toxicity, and what are the indications for its use?

**A:** Digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Fab). Indications: life-threatening dysrhythmias unresponsive to conventional therapy, 6h serum digoxin >12 nmol/L, initial K+ >5 mmol/L, ingestion >10 mg (adult) or >4 mg (child). 10–20 vials IV if acute; 3–6 vials if chronic. 1 vial (40 mg) neutralizes 0.5 mg digoxin.

Common digoxin dysrhythmias include VFib, bidirectional tachycardia, and conduction blocks. Hyperkalemia (K+ >5) from Na/K-ATPase inhibition is an important indication for Fab therapy and correlates with severity.

**Q:** What is the treatment for ethylene glycol and methanol poisoning, and what cofactors are used?

**A:** Fomepizole (4-methylpyrazole): 15 mg/kg IV load over 30 min, then 10 mg/kg q12h. Alternative: ethanol 10% 10 mL/kg over 30 min then 1.5 mL/h. Consider hemodialysis. Cofactors: folinic acid for methanol; pyridoxine and thiamine for ethylene glycol.

Fomepizole inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing conversion of methanol to formic acid and ethylene glycol to oxalic acid (the toxic metabolites). These metabolites cause metabolic acidosis, blindness (methanol), and renal failure (ethylene glycol).

**Q:** What is the acetaminophen toxic dose and what investigation is used to guide treatment?

**A:** Toxic dose: >200 mg/kg or >7.5 g in adults. Measure serum acetaminophen level at 4h post-ingestion and use the Rumack-Matthew nomogram (single ingestion). Treatment: N-acetylcysteine. Monitor liver enzymes, INR, PTT, BUN, Cr. Liver/renal damage delayed >24h.

Acetaminophen poisoning is often clinically silent initially. The Rumack-Matthew nomogram guides NAC therapy based on serum level and time post-ingestion. Hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and encephalopathy are poor prognostic signs.

**Q:** What are the warfarin overdose management thresholds according to INR?

**A:** INR <5.0: cessation and observation. INR 5.1–9.0: hold warfarin; vitamin K 1–2 mg PO if bleeding risk. INR 9.1–20.0: hold warfarin, vitamin K 2–4 mg PO. INR >20.0: hold warfarin, vitamin K 10 mg IV over 10 min. PCC (e.g., Octaplex) for any elevated INR with life-threatening bleeding or surgery within 6h.

IV vitamin K takes 4–6h to work; PCC (prothrombin complex concentrate) provides immediate factor replacement. Vitamin K IV is preferred over PO for fastest reversal in urgent situations.

**Q:** What are the CHIPES radiopaque substances visible on abdominal X-ray in overdose?

**A:** C = Calcium; C = Chloral hydrate; C = CCl4 (carbon tetrachloride); H = Heavy metals; I = Iron; P = Potassium; E = Enteric-coated salicylates; S = Some foreign bodies.

The mnemonic CHIPES helps identify radiopaque ingestions on abdominal X-ray. This can confirm certain ingestions and guide management (e.g., whole bowel irrigation for iron or enteric-coated tablets).

**Q:** What are the indications and contraindications for whole bowel irrigation (WBI) in toxicology?

**A:** Indications: delayed-release product, drug/toxin not bound to charcoal, drug packages (if breakage → emergency surgery), recent toxin ingestion, awake and alert patients. Contraindications: evidence of ileus, perforation, or obstruction. Dose: 500 mL/h (child) to 2000 mL/h (adult) polyethylene glycol until clear rectal effluent.

WBI is used when activated charcoal is ineffective (e.g., iron, lithium, sustained-release medications) or when toxin packets are present. It physically flushes toxin through the GI tract before systemic absorption occurs.

**Q:** What is lipid emulsion therapy used for in toxicology and what is the dosing?

**A:** Used for cardiogenic shock due to lipophilic toxins: local anesthetics (lidocaine), beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, TCA overdose. Dose: 20% lipid solution 1.5 mL/kg bolus over 3 min, then infusion at 0.25 mL/kg/min.

Lipid emulsion ('fat emulsion') therapy creates a 'lipid sink' that sequesters lipophilic drugs away from cardiac tissue, reducing their effect. This is used as rescue therapy when conventional measures fail.

**Q:** What toxidrome does ASA poisoning mimic and what investigations help distinguish it?

**A:** ASA poisoning mimics the sympathomimetic toxidrome. Distinguish with serum salicylate levels, ABG (respiratory alkalosis early, metabolic acidosis late — mixed picture), and anion gap. Hypoglycemia can also occur.

ASA causes direct stimulation of the respiratory centre (hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis), then uncouples oxidative phosphorylation causing metabolic acidosis. The mixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis is classic.

**Q:** What is the treatment for acute dystonic reactions and what is a potential complication of benztropine?

**A:** Benztropine 1–2 mg IM/IV then 2 mg PO for 3 days, OR diphenhydramine 1–2 mg/kg IV then 25 mg PO TID × 3 days. Note: benztropine (Cogentin) has euphoric effects and potential for misuse.

Acute dystonic reactions are extrapyramidal side effects of antipsychotics and other dopamine antagonists. Anticholinergic agents (benztropine, diphenhydramine) provide rapid relief by restoring the dopamine/acetylcholine balance.

**Q:** Why is flumazenil restricted in the ED, and when can it be safely used?

**A:** Flumazenil is only safe in iatrogenic overdose (e.g., operative oversedation). Extensive contraindications include: mixed overdose (especially with TCAs or pro-convulsants), history of alcohol use disorder, history of seizures. Supportive care is the mainstay for benzodiazepine overdose.

Flumazenil can precipitate severe seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients or in mixed overdoses where benzodiazepines are protecting against seizures from another agent (e.g., TCAs, cocaine). It is generally avoided in the ED.

**Q:** What are the features of the sympathomimetic toxidrome and which drugs cause it?

**A:** Features: increased temperature, CNS excitation (including seizures), tachycardia, hypertension, N/V, diaphoresis, dilated pupils. Caused by: amphetamines, caffeine, cocaine, LSD, phencyclidine, ephedrine/decongestants, thyroid hormone, sedative or EtOH withdrawal.

The sympathomimetic toxidrome results from excess catecholamine activity. ASA poisoning and hypoglycemia can mimic this syndrome. Note that EtOH/sedative withdrawal also presents with sympathomimetic features.

**Q:** What ECG changes are associated with calcium channel blocker and digoxin overdose?

**A:** CCB and digoxin: atrioventricular (AV) block. Digoxin: also bradyarrhythmias, bidirectional tachycardia, VFib. Prolonged QT: terfenadine, astemizole, antipsychotics, hydroxychloroquine.

CCBs reduce calcium-dependent conduction in the AV node, causing heart block. Digoxin inhibits the Na/K-ATPase and increases vagal tone, causing bradycardia and various degrees of AV block. Bidirectional VT is pathognomonic of digoxin toxicity.

## Emergency Bites

**Q:** What is the recommended antibiotic for dog/cat bites and why is cefalexin NOT appropriate?

**A:** First line: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. Cefalexin is NOT appropriate because it does not cover Pasteurella multocida or Eikenella corrodens, key pathogens in dog/cat bites.

Cat bites become infected in 10–50% of cases, dog bites in ~5%. A 3–5 day antibiotic course is recommended for hand bites and high-risk wounds. Human bites also require amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and risk assessment for blood-borne infections.

**Q:** What animals are the primary rabies reservoirs in Canada and what is the management after exposure?

**A:** Primary reservoirs: bats and raccoons (warm-blooded animals excluding rodents and lagomorphs). Domestic animal incidence is low. Management: post-exposure vaccine is effective; consult local public health unit for guidance based on local prevalence.

Rodents (mice, rats, squirrels) and lagomorphs (rabbits) are NOT considered rabies reservoirs. Risk is higher for animal bites outside Canada. Post-exposure prophylaxis includes rabies immune globulin and vaccine series.

## Emergency Alcohol

**Q:** What are the signs, symptoms, and management of acute alcohol intoxication in the emergency department?

**A:** Signs/symptoms: slurred speech, CNS depression, disinhibition, incoordination, nystagmus, diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia; may progress to coma. Vitals: hypotension (peripheral vasodilation) with reflex tachycardia, hypothermia, hypoglycemia. Management: thiamine 100 mg PO/IM/IV, hourly neuro vitals, keep in ED until GCS 15 and can ambulate safely.

Alcohol levels correlate poorly with clinical intoxication. If GCS fails to improve, consider ICH, polydrug overdose, metabolic diseases, or post-ictal state. CT head if signs of head injury.

**Q:** What is the timeline of alcohol withdrawal syndromes after the last drink?

**A:** 6–8h: mild withdrawal (tremor, anxiety, agitation, autonomic hyperactivity, insomnia, N/V). 12–48h: alcoholic hallucinations (visual most common, auditory, tactile; vitals often normal). 8h–2d: withdrawal seizures (brief generalized tonic-clonic, may recur within hours). 3–5d (5% of untreated): delirium tremens (DT) — severely confused, fluctuating LOC, agitation, hallucinations, tachycardia, hyperpyrexia, diaphoresis; HIGH mortality.

DT is the most dangerous complication of alcohol withdrawal. Treatment goal is early benzodiazepine administration to prevent progression to DT and seizures. CIWA protocol guides benzodiazepine dosing.

**Q:** What is the pharmacological treatment for alcohol withdrawal in the emergency department?

**A:** First-line: diazepam 10–20 mg IV/PO or lorazepam 2–4 mg IV/PO q1h (preferred if known liver dysfunction) until two sequential CIWA scores <10. Thiamine 200 mg IM/IV then 50–100 mg/d. If no improvement with benzodiazepines: add phenobarbital. If hypomagnesemic: magnesium sulfate 4 g IV over 1–2h.

Lorazepam is preferred over diazepam in liver disease because it does not require hepatic metabolism to active metabolites. CIWA (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment) scores guide dosing; no maximum dose for agitation control.

**Q:** What is Wernicke's encephalopathy and how is it prevented in the ED?

**A:** Wernicke's encephalopathy: triad of oculomotor abnormalities (nystagmus, lateral gaze palsy, disconjugate gaze), cerebellar dysfunction, and global confusion — due to chronic thiamine (B1) deficiency. Prevention: thiamine given BEFORE dextrose in all malnourished, alcohol-dependent, or at-risk patients.

Giving dextrose before thiamine can precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy by exhausting remaining thiamine stores. Thiamine is deficient in malnourished, alcohol use disorder, anorexia, and malnutrition states.

**Q:** What are the cardiovascular complications of chronic alcohol use?

**A:** Hypertension, cardiomyopathy (heart failure with SOB and edema), dysrhythmias ('holiday heart'): AFib (most common), atrial flutter, SVT, VTach (especially Torsades de Pointes if hypomagnesemic/hypokalemic).

'Holiday heart' refers to alcohol-induced arrhythmias often triggered by binge drinking. Hypomagnesemia and hypokalemia from chronic alcohol use predispose to QT prolongation and Torsades de Pointes.

**Q:** What is alcoholic ketoacidosis and what is the treatment?

**A:** Metabolic acidosis with positive urine ketones, low glucose, and normal osmolality. History: chronic alcohol intake with abrupt decrease/cessation, malnourished, abdominal pain with N/V. Treatment: thiamine 250–500 mg IM/IV BEFORE dextrose, then dextrose, volume repletion with NS or LR. Generally resolves in 12–24h.

Alcoholic ketoacidosis results from starvation ketosis combined with alcohol-induced altered fat metabolism. Blood glucose is typically low to normal (unlike DKA). Normal osmolality distinguishes it from toxic alcohol ingestion.
