# Medical Genetics

## Medical Genetics

**Q:** Define penetrance in genetics.

**A:** Penetrance is the probability that a gene variant will be observably expressed in an individual who carries it.

Complete penetrance means everyone with the variant expresses the phenotype (e.g., Huntington disease). Incomplete/reduced penetrance means only some carriers express the phenotype (e.g., BRCA1/2 mutations). Penetrance is different from expressivity, which refers to the degree of expression in those who do express it.

**Q:** What is the difference between penetrance and expressivity in genetics?

**A:** Penetrance is the probability that a gene variant is expressed at all in a carrier. Expressivity is the degree or extent of gene expression — the range of variation in the phenotype among those who do express it.

Example: BRCA1 mutation has incomplete penetrance (not all carriers develop cancer). Neurofibromatosis type 1 has complete penetrance but variable expressivity (ranging from cafe-au-lait spots alone to plexiform neurofibromas). These are distinct but related concepts frequently tested on MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the difference between allelic heterogeneity and locus heterogeneity?

**A:** Allelic heterogeneity: a phenotype caused by different variants within the same gene. Locus heterogeneity: the same phenotype caused by variants in different genes.

Both are forms of genetic heterogeneity. Example of locus heterogeneity: retinitis pigmentosa can be caused by mutations in over 80 different genes. Example of allelic heterogeneity: cystic fibrosis has over 2000 CFTR variants. Understanding heterogeneity is key for genetic counseling and interpreting genetic test results.

**Q:** What is mosaicism in genetics?

**A:** Mosaicism is the presence of two or more genotypes (e.g., different chromosome patterns or gene variants) in cells of the same person, arising from a post-zygotic mutation.

Somatic mosaicism occurs when the mutation arises after fertilization, resulting in a mix of normal and mutant cells. The proportion and distribution of mutant cells determines the clinical phenotype. Example: mosaic Down syndrome (trisomy 21 in some but not all cells). Germline mosaicism can cause inherited disease in offspring even when the parent appears unaffected.

**Q:** What is nondisjunction and what can it cause?

**A:** Nondisjunction is a cell division error in which chromosomes fail to segregate properly, resulting in daughter cells with an abnormal number of chromosomes (aneuploidy), such as trisomy or monosomy.

Nondisjunction can occur during meiosis I or II, or mitosis. In meiosis, it leads to gametes with extra or missing chromosomes. Common clinical examples include: trisomy 21 (Down), trisomy 18 (Edwards), trisomy 13 (Patau), 45,X (Turner), 47,XXY (Klinefelter). Risk increases with advanced maternal age (AMA) for meiosis I errors.

**Q:** What is uniparental disomy (UPD)?

**A:** Uniparental disomy is the inheritance of two full or partial copies of a chromosome from one parent, with no corresponding chromosome from the other parent.

UPD can occur via several mechanisms including trisomy rescue. The clinical significance depends on which parent the copies came from (due to imprinting). Examples: maternal UPD 15 causes Angelman syndrome; paternal UPD 15 causes Prader-Willi syndrome. Both syndromes result from loss of expression of imprinted genes on chromosome 15.

**Q:** What are the ACMG 5 categories for classifying genetic variants?

**A:** 1. Pathogenic (known disease-causing), 2. Likely pathogenic (≥90% likely pathogenic), 3. Variant of uncertain significance (VUS), 4. Likely benign, 5. Benign (not associated with disease).

These American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) classifications are used in clinical reporting of genetic test results. VUS is clinically challenging — it cannot be used to confirm or exclude disease and requires follow-up as more data emerges. 'Likely pathogenic' variants are clinically treated as pathogenic in practice.

**Q:** What does 'mutation' mean in current clinical genetics terminology vs. its older usage?

**A:** In current clinical genetics, 'mutation' specifically refers to the process of genetic change, not the variant itself. The preferred term for disease-causing variants is 'pathogenic variant.' The old usage of 'mutation' as synonymous with pathogenic variant is no longer recommended.

Similarly, 'polymorphism' now has specific meaning — a common variant not typically associated with disease used in population genetics, rather than a benign variant. Using precise terminology avoids stigmatization and confusion in genetic counseling. ACMG 2015 guidelines introduced standardized nomenclature.

**Q:** What are the types of genetic variants that can cause disease?

**A:** 1. Whole gene deletions/duplications (aneuploidy, unbalanced rearrangements, CNVs), 2. Gene disruptions (inversions, balanced rearrangements), 3. Protein-coding alterations (missense, nonsense, frameshift), 4. Variants affecting transcription, 5. Variants affecting splicing.

Missense variants change one amino acid; nonsense variants introduce premature stop codons; frameshift variants (insertions/deletions not divisible by 3) shift the reading frame and typically cause loss of function. Copy number variants (CNVs) can be detected by array CGH or chromosomal microarray — clinically important for conditions like 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge).

**Q:** What is the inheritance pattern of autosomal dominant vs. autosomal recessive disorders?

**A:** Autosomal dominant (AD): ONE copy of a pathogenic variant is sufficient to cause the trait/disorder. Autosomal recessive (AR): BOTH copies of the gene must have pathogenic variants; individuals with one copy are carriers.

AD disorders: typically each generation is affected, 50% offspring risk, males and females equally affected, one parent usually affected. AR disorders: carrier parents (often unaffected) have 25% risk of affected offspring with each pregnancy, 50% carrier risk. X-linked disorders: more commonly/severely affect males. Examples: AD: Huntington disease, Marfan; AR: cystic fibrosis, PKU; X-linked: hemophilia A, Duchenne MD.

**Q:** What characterizes X-linked inheritance and why are males more commonly/severely affected?

**A:** X-linked disorders are caused by pathogenic variants on the X chromosome. Males have only one X chromosome (hemizygous), so a single pathogenic variant on the X causes full disease expression. Females have two X chromosomes and are often carriers with mild or no disease.

Examples of X-linked recessive disorders: hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency), Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), G6PD deficiency, Fabry disease (X-linked, but can also affect females). For X-linked recessive: carrier females pass the variant to 50% of sons (affected) and 50% of daughters (carriers). There is no male-to-male transmission.

**Q:** What genetic variant classification should be clinically treated as pathogenic?

**A:** A 'likely pathogenic' variant (≥90% probability of being pathogenic) is clinically treated as a pathogenic variant in patient management.

The ACMG classification system recognizes that 'likely pathogenic' variants have sufficient evidence to guide clinical management despite not meeting the highest certainty threshold. In contrast, 'variants of uncertain significance (VUS)' cannot be used to make clinical decisions and require periodic reclassification as evidence accumulates.

**Q:** What is genetic anticipation and which mechanism causes it?

**A:** Genetic anticipation is the phenomenon where signs and symptoms of a disorder become more severe and appear at an earlier age in successive generations. It is caused by expansion of triplet (trinucleotide) repeat sequences in certain genes, with the length of the expansion often proportional to clinical severity.

Classic examples include Fragile X syndrome and Huntington disease. The expanding repeat number is transmitted to offspring, who may receive a longer — and thus more pathogenic — repeat than their parent. This explains why children can present earlier or more severely than an affected parent.

**Q:** What is the molecular basis of imprinting disorders, and give two classic examples?

**A:** Imprinted genes are expressed from only one parental allele (maternal or paternal), determined by allele-specific epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modifications. Disorders arise when the normally expressed allele is disrupted (e.g., deletion) or uniparental disomy of the silenced allele occurs. Classic examples: Prader-Willi syndrome (absent paternal 15q11-13 expression) and Angelman syndrome (absent maternal 15q11-13 expression).

In Prader-Willi, the paternal chromosome 15q11-13 is deleted or there is maternal uniparental disomy. In Angelman, the maternal 15q11-13 is deleted or there are pathogenic variants in UBE3A. Both arise from the same chromosomal region but produce distinct phenotypes depending on which parental copy is affected — illustrating parent-of-origin gene expression.

**Q:** How are mitochondrial disorders inherited, and why?

**A:** Mitochondrial disorders caused by pathogenic variants in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) are maternally inherited by all offspring, because all mitochondria in the embryo originate from the maternal ovum with no paternal contribution. Disorders caused by pathogenic variants in nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial proteins follow Mendelian (autosomal or X-linked) inheritance.

An example of maternally inherited mtDNA disease is MELAS (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes). An example of Mendelian mitochondrial disease is Alpers syndrome, caused by autosomal recessive variants in POLG. High phenotypic heterogeneity is characteristic due to heteroplasmy (mixed populations of normal and mutant mitochondria).

**Q:** List four categories of common indications for genetic referral in adults.

**A:** 1. Family history of adult-onset genetic conditions (e.g., Huntington disease). 2. Personal or family history of cancer with suspected genetic cause. 3. Bleeding or clotting disorders. 4. Early-onset vision or hearing loss, intellectual disability, leukodystrophies, muscular dystrophies, movement disorders (ataxia, dystonia, chorea), or metabolic diseases.

Genetic referral in adults is indicated when a hereditary condition may influence personal health management or risk to family members. In Canada, referral to a medical geneticist or genetic counsellor facilitates appropriate molecular testing, cascade testing of relatives, and counselling regarding reproductive implications.

**Q:** Differentiate between a malformation, disruption, deformation, and dysplasia as mechanisms of congenital anomalies.

**A:** Malformation: intrinsically abnormal developmental process (e.g., polydactyly). Disruption: extrinsic breakdown of a normally developing structure (e.g., amniotic band disruption sequence). Deformation: mechanical forces alter the final form of a normally developing structure (e.g., Potter deformation sequence). Dysplasia: abnormal organization of cells into tissues (e.g., skeletal dysplasia).

Understanding the mechanism helps determine recurrence risk and prognosis. Malformations often reflect genetic causes and carry higher recurrence risk. Disruptions are usually non-recurring environmental events. Deformations may resolve if the mechanical force is removed. Dysplasias are often caused by single gene defects affecting a specific tissue type.

**Q:** Distinguish between an association, a sequence, and a syndrome in the context of multiple congenital anomalies.

**A:** Association: non-random co-occurrence of independent anomalies without a single known etiology (e.g., VACTERL). Sequence: multiple anomalies arising from a single initial defect or precipitating factor altering subsequent development (e.g., Potter sequence, Pierre-Robin sequence). Syndrome: a pattern of anomalies with a single known or suspected cause (e.g., CHARGE syndrome).

VACTERL includes vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, and limb anomalies occurring together more than by chance. In Potter sequence, oligohydramnios (often from renal agenesis) causes compression deformities. CHARGE syndrome is caused by CHD7 mutations. Classification guides the workup for an underlying etiology.

**Q:** What is the recommended first-line cytogenetic investigation for a child with developmental delay/autism or two or more congenital anomalies?

**A:** Chromosomal microarray analysis (SNP array) is the first-line cytogenetic investigation for children with developmental delay/autism or two or more congenital anomalies.

Chromosomal microarray can identify small copy number variants (CNVs) — deletions and duplications — anywhere in the genome that are below the resolution of standard karyotype. Meta-analytic data demonstrates its diagnostic utility (0.10) is lower than whole exome/genome sequencing but it remains a widely available and cost-effective first-line test. FISH is preferred when a specific aneuploidy syndrome (e.g., trisomy 13/18/21) is clinically suspected.

**Q:** What are the key features of Down syndrome (Trisomy 21) and its most common cardiac defect?

**A:** Down syndrome features: mild microcephaly, flat occiput, upslanting palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, Brushfield spots, protruding tongue, low flat nasal bridge, single transverse palmar crease, clinodactyly, joint hyperflexibility (including atlantoaxial instability), hypotonia at birth, intellectual disability, and Alzheimer disease onset in 40s. Incidence: 1 in 600–800 births. Most common cardiac defect: atrioventricular septal defect (50% have cardiac defects).

Trisomy 21 is the most common chromosomal aneuploidy affecting autosomes, with risk rising from 1 in 1500 at age 20 to 1 in 20 at age 45. A 1% lifetime risk of leukemia exists. Long-term management follows the AAP Health Supervision for Children with Down Syndrome guidelines. Atlantoaxial instability requires monitoring before contact sports or general anesthesia.

**Q:** Compare the key distinguishing features of Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome) and Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome).

**A:** Trisomy 18 (Edwards): microcephaly, prominent occiput, clenched fist with overlapping digits, rocker-bottom feet, 60% cardiac defects (VSD, PDA, ASD), 13% 1-year survival. Trisomy 13 (Patau): microcephaly, holoprosencephaly, polydactyly, midline anomalies (scalp defect, cleft lip/palate), 80% cardiac defects (ASD, PDA, VSD), single umbilical artery, 20% 1-year survival.

Both trisomies 13 and 18 carry a very poor prognosis with profound intellectual disability in survivors. Trisomy 18 is more common (1 in 6000 vs. 1 in 10,000). Both feature low-set malformed ears and polycystic kidneys. A key distinguishing finding is the clenched fist with overlapping digits in Trisomy 18 versus polydactyly and midline defects in Trisomy 13.

**Q:** What is the genotype and key clinical features of Fragile X syndrome?

**A:** Genotype: CGG trinucleotide repeat expansion (>200 repeats = full mutation) in the FMR1 gene on the X chromosome. Premutation: 55–200 repeats. Features: macrocephaly, prominent jaw/forehead/nasal bridge, long/thin face, large protuberant ears, macroorchidism, hyperextensibility, high-arched palate; mild-to-moderate intellectual disability, ADHD, autism; seizures, scoliosis, mitral valve prolapse. Most common heritable cause of intellectual disability in males.

Premutation carrier females have increased risk of having a child with Fragile X because CGG repeats in the premutation range are more likely to expand to full mutation length during transmission. Male premutation carriers may develop FXTAS (tremor/ataxia syndrome) in later life. Female premutation carriers have higher risk of premature ovarian failure. Diagnosis is by PCR and/or Southern blot of FMR1 repeat length.

**Q:** What are the key features and inheritance of Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)?

**A:** Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) is the most common sex chromosome aneuploidy in males, occurring in 1 in 1000 live male births. Pre-puberty: no distinctive features. Post-puberty: tall slim build, long limbs, gynecomastia, lack of facial hair, small testes, infertility (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism), variable learning/behavioural difficulties including anxiety and impulsivity. Diagnosed by karyotype.

Risk increases with advanced maternal age. Infertility due to hypergonadotropic hypogonadism is the hallmark. Testosterone replacement therapy is indicated at puberty. Behavioural features such as ADHD, anxiety, and shyness are common. The IQ is usually in the mild intellectual disability or low-average range, though 20% have normal IQ.

**Q:** What are the characteristic features of Turner syndrome (45,X0)?

**A:** Turner syndrome (45,X0 in 50%, mosaic 45,X/46,XX in 15%) affects 1 in 2000–2500 live female births. Features: short stature, short webbed neck, low posterior hairline, wide carrying angle (cubitus valgus), broad chest with widely spaced nipples, lymphedema of hands/feet, streak ovaries causing primary amenorrhea and infertility, coarctation of aorta, bicuspid aortic valve. Typically normal intelligence. Diagnosed by karyotype.

Unlike Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome risk does not increase with advanced maternal age. Cardiovascular complications (coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve) require regular monitoring. Hormone replacement therapy is needed at puberty. Fertility may be possible with egg donation in mosaic cases. Cystic hygroma and polyhydramnios may be detected prenatally.

**Q:** What are the 'CATCH 22' features of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge syndrome)?

**A:** CATCH 22 mnemonic: Cardiac anomalies (conotruncal defects), Abnormal facies (craniofacial anomalies, micrognathia, low-set ears), T-cell deficiency (thymic hypoplasia causing immunodeficiency), Cleft palate, Hypocalcemia/Hypoparathyroidism. Additional features: cognitive impairment, high-risk for schizophrenia and other psychiatric disorders. Incidence: 1 in 4000 (second most common genetic diagnosis after Down syndrome). Autosomal dominant microdeletion of 22q11.2.

The 22q11.2 deletion is the most common microdeletion syndrome. The deletion disrupts multiple genes important for pharyngeal arch development, explaining the cardiac, craniofacial, thymic, and parathyroid anomalies. Psychiatric illness, particularly schizophrenia, develops in adulthood in a significant proportion. Diagnosis is confirmed by chromosomal microarray or FISH.

**Q:** Contrast the molecular basis of Prader-Willi syndrome versus Angelman syndrome.

**A:** Both involve chromosome 15q11-13 but differ in which parental allele is affected. Prader-Willi syndrome: lack of gene expression on the paternal chromosome 15q11-13 (due to paternal deletion, maternal uniparental disomy, or imprinting defect) — presents with hypotonia/weakness, hyperphagia, obesity, hypogonadism, short stature, almond-shaped eyes, small hands/feet, and obsessive traits. Angelman syndrome: lack of gene expression on the maternal chromosome 15q11-13 (maternal deletion, paternal uniparental disomy, imprinting defect, or UBE3A variants) — presents with severe intellectual disability, ataxia, seizures, inappropriately happy demeanour/laughter, and fascination with water.

The same genomic region produces completely different phenotypes depending on which parental copy is affected — a key illustration of genomic imprinting. Prader-Willi has a prevalence of 1 in 15,000; Angelman of 1 in 10,000. Methylation analysis of the 15q11-13 region can detect both conditions simultaneously.

**Q:** What is the CHARGE mnemonic and the causative gene?

**A:** CHARGE: Coloboma, Heart disease (right-sided CHD, pulmonary stenosis), Atresia of choanae, Restriction of growth and mental development, GU anomalies, Ear malformations and hearing loss. Approximately 2/3 of affected children have a pathogenic variant in CHD7. Incidence: 1 in 10,000.

CHD7 encodes a chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein involved in chromatin remodelling. Choanal atresia and coloboma are particularly distinctive features. CHARGE is an autosomal dominant condition, often due to de novo variants. It represents a syndrome (single cause producing a recognizable pattern) rather than an association.

**Q:** What are the genes, associated cancers, and key screening recommendations for Lynch syndrome (HNPCC)?

**A:** Genes: MSH2, MLH1, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM (mismatch repair genes). Associated cancers: colorectal, endometrial, ovarian, renal, pancreatic, liver/biliary duct, stomach, brain, breast. Screening: colonoscopy starting age 20–25 (or 2–5 years younger than earliest colorectal cancer in family) every 1–2 years. MSH6 and PMS2 carriers start colonoscopy at age 30 and 35 respectively.

Lynch syndrome accounts for approximately 2–4% of all colorectal cancers. It is autosomal dominant. Tumour microsatellite instability (MSI) and immunohistochemistry for MMR protein expression are used for tumour screening. Cascade testing of family members is essential. Prophylactic surgery (hysterectomy/oophorectomy) is considered in women with Lynch syndrome.

**Q:** What is the gene and surveillance protocol for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (BRCA1/BRCA2)?

**A:** Genes: BRCA1 and BRCA2. Associated cancers: female breast, ovarian, pancreatic; male prostate, breast, pancreatic. Screening: women age 25–65: annual breast MRI; age 30: annual breast MRI plus mammography. Colonoscopy and risk-reducing surgery (salpingo-oophorectomy after childbearing) are also considered.

BRCA1 and BRCA2 are tumour suppressor genes involved in DNA double-strand break repair. Lifetime breast cancer risk in BRCA1 carriers is approximately 70–80%. Risk-reducing bilateral mastectomy and salpingo-oophorectomy significantly reduce cancer risk. Pancreatic cancer screening is recommended for BRCA2 carriers. Genetic counselling precedes testing.

**Q:** What is Li-Fraumeni syndrome, which gene is mutated, and what cancers are associated?

**A:** Li-Fraumeni syndrome is an autosomal dominant hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline pathogenic variants in TP53. Associated cancers include breast cancer, osteosarcoma, leukemia, soft tissue sarcoma, brain tumors, and adrenocortical carcinoma, among numerous others. Screening includes annual breast MRI in women from age 20, annual whole-body MRI from age 18, and colonoscopy/upper endoscopy from age 25.

TP53 is the 'guardian of the genome'; loss of p53 function impairs cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in response to DNA damage, leading to diverse malignancies. Children are at particular risk for adrenocortical carcinoma and require abdominal/pelvic ultrasound every 3–4 months from birth and annual brain MRI. Avoidance of radiation exposure is recommended where possible.

**Q:** What is Marfan syndrome: gene, inheritance, and key clinical features?

**A:** Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the FBN1 gene (fibrillin-1). Key features: tall slender build, disproportionately long limbs and digits (arachnodactyly), aortic dilatation and dissection, ectopia lentis (lens dislocation), and spontaneous pneumothoraces. Incidence: 1 in 3000–5000.

FBN1 encodes fibrillin-1, a structural protein in the extracellular matrix. Aortic root dilatation leading to aortic dissection is the major life-threatening complication; serial echocardiographic surveillance and beta-blocker therapy or angiotensin receptor blockers are used to slow progression. Prophylactic aortic root replacement is performed when diameter exceeds threshold. Ophthalmologic evaluation for lens subluxation is essential.

**Q:** Compare classical Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (cEDS) versus vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS) in terms of gene, collagen affected, and key clinical risks.

**A:** Classical EDS: autosomal dominant, caused by COL5A1 or COL5A2 pathogenic variants affecting type V collagen; features include generalized joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility, atrophic scarring, easy bruising, and subcutaneous spheroids. Vascular EDS: autosomal dominant, caused by COL3A1 pathogenic variants affecting type III collagen; features include arterial fragility/rupture, intestinal/uterine rupture, and characteristic facies (translucent skin, thin lips, pinched nose, prominent ears). vEDS carries highest risk of life-threatening complications.

Type III collagen is the most abundant collagen in arterial walls, bowel, and uterus, explaining the catastrophic vascular and visceral fragility in vEDS. Pregnancy carries high risk in vEDS due to uterine rupture. Patients with vEDS should avoid elective procedures, contact sports, and Valsalva maneuvers. Regular vascular imaging surveillance is recommended.

**Q:** What are the gene, inheritance, and clinical features of hereditary hemochromatosis?

**A:** Hereditary hemochromatosis is autosomal recessive, most commonly caused by pathogenic variants in the HFE gene. Clinical features result from iron overload: elevated transferrin saturation and ferritin, bronze skin, arthralgia, diabetes mellitus, hypogonadism, cirrhosis, and cardiomyopathy. Incidence: 1 in 300. Treatment: phlebotomy for elevated serum ferritin and transferrin saturation.

HFE pathogenic variants (most commonly C282Y homozygosity) disrupt hepcidin-mediated regulation of iron absorption, leading to progressive iron accumulation in parenchymal cells. Early identification and phlebotomy therapy before organ damage can prevent complications. First-degree relatives should be screened with HFE genotyping and iron studies.

**Q:** What is Wilson disease: gene, inheritance, and treatment?

**A:** Wilson disease is autosomal recessive, caused by pathogenic variants in the ATP7B gene, leading to impairment of cellular copper transport. Clinical features: liver disease (acute and/or chronic), movement disorders, psychiatric disturbance, and Kayser-Fleischer rings (copper deposits in Descemet membrane of the cornea). Incidence: 1 in 30,000. Treatment: chelating agents (penicillamine, trientine) or zinc.

ATP7B encodes a copper-transporting ATPase necessary for copper incorporation into ceruloplasmin and biliary copper excretion. Copper accumulates in the liver, basal ganglia, and cornea. Low ceruloplasmin and elevated 24-hour urinary copper support the diagnosis. Kayser-Fleischer rings are virtually pathognomonic but may be absent in early disease. Liver transplantation corrects the hepatic defect.

**Q:** What is Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD): inheritance, protein affected, and key clinical features?

**A:** DMD is X-linked recessive, affecting 1 in 4000 males (1/3 de novo, 2/3 inherited). Caused by pathogenic variants in the DMD gene resulting in absence of dystrophin. Features: proximal muscle weakness by age 3, positive Gowers' sign, waddling gait, pseudohypertrophy of calf muscles, decreased reflexes, cardiomyopathy, non-progressive cognitive delay. Patients typically become wheelchair-bound by age 12; death from respiratory failure or cardiac failure in the second-third decade.

Dystrophin is a structural protein that links the intracellular cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix; its absence leads to muscle fibre fragility and necrosis. Creatine kinase is elevated 50–100x normal. Becker muscular dystrophy results from variants in the same gene producing some residual dystrophin, causing a milder phenotype. Corticosteroids (prednisone or deflazacort) are the standard of care, improving muscle strength and function.

**Q:** What is Gowers' sign and what does it indicate?

**A:** Gowers' sign is the maneuver in which a child uses their hands to 'climb up' the legs to move from a sitting to a standing position. It indicates weakness of the pelvic girdle and proximal lower limb muscles, as seen in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

The sign reflects inability to extend the hips and knees against gravity using the proximal limb muscles alone. While classic for DMD, Gowers' sign can be seen in any cause of proximal lower extremity weakness, including limb-girdle muscular dystrophies and spinal muscular atrophy. It typically becomes evident by age 3–4 in DMD.

**Q:** What is the management of Duchenne muscular dystrophy?

**A:** Management: corticosteroids (prednisone or deflazacort — standard of care); physiotherapy, wheelchairs, braces (prevent obesity); annual cardiac monitoring in males (every 3–5 years in females); bone health monitoring (vitamin D, bisphosphonates); surgical correction of scoliosis; gene therapy trials underway.

Cochrane review evidence shows corticosteroids improve muscle strength and function over 12 months with 0.75 mg/kg/day being the optimal dose. Early cardiac intervention is essential as dilated cardiomyopathy is a major cause of morbidity. Respiratory support (non-invasive ventilation) and careful perioperative planning are critical as disease progresses. Ataluren (for nonsense mutations) and exon-skipping therapies are newer approved options.

**Q:** What is Huntington disease: inheritance, molecular basis, and clinical triad?

**A:** Huntington disease is autosomal dominant, caused by CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the HTT gene. CAG repeats <26: not associated with disease; >36: pathogenic. Clinical triad: 1) progressive movement disorder (chorea of face, trunk, and limbs, progressing to dysarthria, dysphagia, rigidity), 2) neuropsychiatric symptoms (irritability, depression, paranoia, delusions), 3) cognitive disorder (executive dysfunction). Typical onset: age 30–50 (mean 45 years); median survival after motor symptom onset: 18 years.

HD demonstrates genetic anticipation due to CAG repeat expansion in transmission. Early-stage signs include subtle eye movement changes, minor involuntary movements, and mood disturbance. The condition is universally fatal. Genetic counselling is mandatory before predictive testing due to significant consequences for at-risk family members. Treatment is supportive: tetrabenazine/deutetrabenazine for chorea; SSRIs for depression.

**Q:** What is Tay-Sachs disease: population at risk, gene, enzyme defect, and classical presentation?

**A:** Tay-Sachs is autosomal recessive, caused by HEXA gene pathogenic variants resulting in deficiency of hexosaminidase A, leading to GM2 ganglioside accumulation and neurodegeneration. High carrier frequency in Ashkenazi Jewish (1 in 30), French Canadian, Cajun (Louisiana), and Pennsylvania Dutch populations. Classical infantile form: normal development for first few months, then progressive neurological deterioration starting at 5–6 months, exaggerated startle response, retinal cherry-red spot by 3–12 months, median survival ~4 years.

The cherry-red spot is caused by lipid accumulation in the neuroretinal cells surrounding the macula; the fovea appears red against the pale surrounding retina. Juvenile form presents age 2–10 with ataxia, weakness, and spasticity. Adult-onset form is rare and presents with motor neuron disease, dystonia, and psychiatric features. Diagnosis is by serum hexosaminidase A enzyme activity. Treatment remains supportive.

**Q:** What distinguishes the cherry-red spot of Tay-Sachs from Niemann-Pick disease?

**A:** Both Tay-Sachs (HEXA deficiency — no hexosaminidase A) and Niemann-Pick type A/B (sphingomyelinase deficiency) cause a macular cherry-red spot. However, Niemann-Pick type A/B also causes hepatosplenomegaly, while Tay-Sachs does not cause hepatosplenomegaly. Niemann-Pick type C also causes a cherry-red spot. The presence of hepatosplenomegaly with a cherry-red spot suggests a sphingolipidosis other than Tay-Sachs.

This is a high-yield distinguishing point. Tay-Sachs classically presents without organomegaly, whereas lysosomal storage diseases affecting the reticuloendothelial system (e.g., Niemann-Pick, Gaucher) cause hepatosplenomegaly. Gaucher disease also causes a cherry-red spot. All three conditions are included in the lysosomal storage disease category and must be differentiated by specific enzyme assays.

**Q:** What metabolic disorders are included in Ontario's universal newborn screening program? (Recall the categories)

**A:** Ontario's newborn screening includes treatable metabolic disorders from several categories. Examples flagged in the Toronto Notes table: PKU (phenylketonuria), galactosemia, MSUD (maple syrup urine disease), MCADD (medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency), VLCAD, LCHAD, tyrosinemia, homocystinuria, propionic acidemia, and Hurler syndrome (mucopolysaccharidosis type I).

Newborn screening is performed within the first few days of life using a blood spot (Guthrie card). The goal is to identify treatable and potentially fatal disorders before symptoms arise. Early dietary or enzymatic intervention can prevent irreversible damage. MSUD screening is critical as delayed treatment can lead to cerebral edema and death within days of birth.

**Q:** What is phenylketonuria (PKU): pathophysiology, clinical features, and management?

**A:** PKU (1 in 10,000; autosomal recessive) is caused by deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH gene), preventing conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine, causing toxic phenylalanine buildup. Features: musty odour, eczema, hypertonia, tremors, intellectual disability, hypopigmentation (fair hair, blue irises due to low tyrosine). Management: lifelong dietary restriction of phenylalanine (critical in pregnancy to prevent maternal PKU effects on fetus); large neutral amino acid replacement; BH4 treatment (sapropterin); phenylalanine lyase (pegvaliase). Screened at birth.

Maternal PKU is critical: if a PKU mother does not maintain low phenylalanine levels during pregnancy, the fetus is exposed to high phenylalanine and may develop multiple congenital abnormalities regardless of fetal genotype. The PRISM trial demonstrated pegvaliase reduced blood phenylalanine <600 µmol/L in 68.4% of adults at 24 months. GMP-MFs (glycomacropeptide medical foods) may improve dietary adherence compared to traditional amino acid formulas.

**Q:** What is maple syrup urine disease (MSUD): cause, clinical features, and management?

**A:** MSUD (1 in 185,000; autosomal recessive; 1 in 400 in Mennonites) is caused by deficiency of branched-chain alpha-keto acid dehydrogenase complex (BCKDHA, BCKDHB, DBT gene variants), preventing breakdown of leucine, isoleucine, and valine. Features: maple syrup odour in urine/cerumen, feeding intolerance, vomiting, lethargy, progressive encephalopathy with seizures, coma, and death if untreated. Management: protein-restricted high-carbohydrate diet limiting branched-chain amino acid intake; thiamine trial in some infants. Screened by newborn screening.

MSUD is a neonatal emergency when undiagnosed — cerebral edema can develop rapidly from leucine toxicity. Serum amino acid evaluation (leucine, isoleucine, alloisoleucine, valine) and urinary organic acid analysis confirm the diagnosis. The sweet maple syrup odour distinguishes MSUD from other organic acidemias. Leucine is the most neurotoxic of the three branched-chain amino acids.

**Q:** What is galactosemia: pathophysiology, neonatal presentation, and management?

**A:** Galactosemia (1 in 60,000; autosomal recessive) is most commonly caused by deficiency of galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase, leading to inability to process galactose/lactose, with buildup of toxic galactose-1-phosphate. Neonatal features: liver failure, jaundice, failure to thrive, cataracts; high risk of E. coli sepsis. If untreated: irreversible intellectual disability, seizures, and death. Management: eliminate galactose from diet (no dairy, no breast milk — use soy-based formula). Screened in many newborn screening programs but often presents before screening result is reported.

The E. coli sepsis risk in galactosemia is thought to result from impaired neutrophil bactericidal function. Infantile cataracts from galactitol accumulation in the lens are reversible if galactose is eliminated early. Long-term complications despite dietary adherence include developmental delay, speech problems, and ovarian insufficiency in females. Galactose is present in breast milk, formula, and many foods, requiring vigilant dietary restriction.

**Q:** What is Von Gierke disease (GSD type I): pathophysiology, presentation, and management?

**A:** GSD type I (1 in 100,000; autosomal recessive) is caused by pathogenic variants in G6PC (type Ia) or SLC37A4 (type Ib) genes, preventing effective conversion of glucose-6-phosphate to glucose. Glucose-6-phosphate accumulates and is converted to glycogen and fat in liver and kidneys. Presentation (3–6 months): hepatomegaly, hypoglycemia (triggered by fasting), 'doll-like' facies (full cheeks, thin extremities), hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, lactic acidosis. Management: frequent small feedings, avoid fructose/sucrose/galactose, continuous overnight feeds, raw cornstarch (slow sustained glucose release), vitamin supplementation.

The metabolic derangements in Von Gierke disease all stem from the inability to release glucose from liver glycogen: lactic acidosis (pyruvate diverted to lactate), hyperuricemia (nucleotide catabolism), and hypertriglyceridemia (excess acetyl-CoA). Long-term complications include renal disease, hepatic adenomas, osteoporosis, and hypoglycemic seizures. Liver transplantation corrects the hepatic defect but not the renal manifestations.

**Q:** What is ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency: inheritance, presentation, and acute management?

**A:** OTC deficiency (1 in 62,000–77,000; X-linked recessive) is caused by pathogenic variants in the OTC gene, which encodes an enzyme essential for the urea cycle. Dysfunction leads to ammonia accumulation. Neonatal males: severe hyperammonemia causing reduced oral intake, lethargy, tachypnea, hypothermia, encephalopathy, and coma. Late-onset forms (in any sex) may be triggered by fasting, illness, or protein overload. Diagnosis: hyperammonemia + high urinary orotic acid. Acute management: 10% dextrose at 1.5x maintenance, IV arginine, IV ammonium scavengers (sodium benzoate + sodium phenylbutyrate), hemodialysis if ammonia >500 µmol/L.

OTC deficiency is the most common urea cycle defect. Because it is X-linked, males are most severely affected at birth. Female carriers may be asymptomatic or present with late-onset symptoms during catabolic states (e.g., illness, postpartum). High urinary orotic acid distinguishes OTC deficiency from other urea cycle defects (orotic acid is a byproduct of excess carbamoyl phosphate diverted to pyrimidine synthesis). Long-term management includes low-protein diet plus essential amino acids, citrulline, and ammonia scavengers.

**Q:** What are the initial laboratory investigations to order when an inborn error of metabolism is suspected in an acutely ill child?

**A:** Essential investigations: electrolytes and arterial blood gas (calculate anion gap; detect acidosis); blood glucose; CBC with differential and smear; lactate; ammonia; plasma calcium and phosphate; plasma amino acid screen; urinalysis with ketones and urinary organic acids; acylcarnitine profile with carnitine levels. Also: urate, CSF glycine (if MSUD suspected), free fatty acids (3-beta-hydroxybutyrate ratio >4 suggests fatty acid oxidation defect). Check newborn screening results.

During acute decompensation suspected to be from an inborn error of metabolism, feeding should be discontinued to prevent further buildup of toxic metabolites, while providing IV glucose to suppress catabolism. The anion gap differentiates urea cycle defects (hyperammonemia with normal anion gap) from organic acidemias (hyperammonemia with high anion gap). Ketonuria in a neonate must always be investigated — it is abnormal and suggests an organic acidemia.

**Q:** What clinical findings distinguish lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs) from other metabolic disorders on physical examination?

**A:** Lysosomal storage diseases characteristically cause: dysmorphic facial features, macrocephaly (e.g., Niemann-Pick, Gaucher, Tay-Sachs), hepatosplenomegaly (Niemann-Pick A/B, Gaucher — but NOT Tay-Sachs), cherry-red spot on macula (Niemann-Pick A/B, Tay-Sachs, Gaucher), corneal clouding (Hurler), infantile cataracts (Fabry), peripheral neuropathy (Fabry, Krabbe), spasticity, and progressive developmental regression/chronic encephalopathy.

Lysosomal storage diseases result from deficient lysosomal enzyme activity, causing accumulation of substrate within lysosomes. The specific substrate accumulating determines the organ pattern affected. Laboratory findings include elevated urine oligosaccharides (oligosaccharidoses) and glycosaminoglycans (mucopolysaccharidoses). Bone crises are characteristic of Gaucher disease (accumulation in bone marrow macrophages).

**Q:** What is the inheritance and gene involved in Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) and type 2 (NF2), and what are their main tumour associations?

**A:** NF1: autosomal dominant, NF1 gene (neurofibromin). Associated tumours: astrocytoma, optic glioma, neurofibromas, and leukemia. Surveillance: annual physical exam including growth, blood pressure, skin, bone, neurological, and vision screening from childhood. NF2: autosomal dominant, NF2 gene (merlin). Associated tumours: bilateral vestibular schwannomas (pathognomonic), meningioma, ependymoma, astrocytoma. Surveillance: annual audiology assessment; annual brain MRI and spinal MRI every 2–3 years from age 1.

Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are virtually diagnostic of NF2 and cause progressive sensorineural hearing loss. NF1 presents with café-au-lait spots (≥6 measuring ≥1.5 cm post-puberty), Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas), and axillary/inguinal freckling. Optic gliomas in NF1 may cause vision loss and are managed conservatively unless progressive. Neurodevelopmental monitoring is important in NF1 as learning disabilities are common.

**Q:** What is Noonan syndrome: inheritance, pathway affected, and key clinical features?

**A:** Noonan syndrome is autosomal dominant (RASopathy), caused by pathogenic variants in RAS/MAPK pathway genes (most commonly PTPN11, SOS1, RAF1, KRAS). Features: short stature, short webbed neck, hypertelorism, low-set ears, epicanthal folds, ptosis, pectus excavatum, right-sided congenital heart disease (pulmonary stenosis is most common), moderate intellectual disability, delayed puberty. Increased risk of hematological malignancies and cardiomyopathy. Incidence: 1 in 2000.

Noonan syndrome superficially resembles Turner syndrome (short stature, webbed neck, cardiac defects) but affects both sexes and has normal karyotype. RAS/MAPK pathway mutations activate cell proliferation and survival signals. Other RASopathies include LEOPARD syndrome, Costello syndrome, and cardio-facio-cutaneous syndrome. Pulmonary stenosis in Noonan syndrome is the most common cardiac defect, contrasting with coarctation of the aorta in Turner syndrome.

**Q:** What genetic testing modalities are used to diagnose Fragile X syndrome?

**A:** Diagnosis of Fragile X syndrome uses: PCR analysis to determine CGG repeat length in the FMR1 gene, and/or Southern blot analysis for methylation status (detects full mutations >200 repeats and distinguishes methylated full mutation from unmethylated premutation). Methylation analysis confirms that the full mutation is silenced (methylated). Karyotype alone cannot reliably detect the trinucleotide repeat expansion.

PCR is used as a first-line test and can accurately size premutation alleles (55–200 repeats). However, large full mutations (>200 repeats) may not amplify efficiently by standard PCR, making Southern blot important for confirmation. Methylation-specific PCR can also be used. For carrier testing and prenatal diagnosis in at-risk women (premutation carriers), repeat-primed PCR is increasingly used as a sensitive screening tool.

**Q:** What is the Loeys-Dietz syndrome: genes involved and distinguishing features from Marfan syndrome?

**A:** Loeys-Dietz syndrome is autosomal dominant, caused by pathogenic variants in TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, or TGFB2 genes (TGF-beta pathway). Features include vascular findings (aortic aneurysms and dissections, generalized arterial tortuosity), craniofacial features (cleft palate, hypertelorism), skeletal features, easy bruising, and allergic/inflammatory disease. Distinguished from Marfan syndrome by: hypertelorism, bifid uvula/cleft palate, and widespread arterial tortuosity rather than lens dislocation.

Loeys-Dietz syndrome poses a higher risk of aortic dissection at smaller aortic diameters than Marfan syndrome, requiring more aggressive surgical intervention thresholds. The arterial tortuosity can involve vessels throughout the body. Early prophylactic aortic root replacement is recommended. Genetic testing of the TGF-beta receptor genes confirms the diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the VACTERL association components?

**A:** VACTERL association: Vertebral dysgenesis, Anal atresia (imperforate anus) ± fistula, Cardiac anomalies, Tracheoesophageal fistula + esophageal atresia, Renal anomalies, Limb (radial ray) anomalies. At least 3 components are typically required for diagnosis.

VACTERL is an association — a non-random clustering of anomalies without a single known etiology. Most cases are sporadic. The tracheoesophageal fistula component often presents with polyhydramnios in utero and excessive salivation/choking with first feeds after birth. Cardiac and renal anomalies require dedicated echocardiography and renal ultrasound. The diagnosis is one of exclusion after chromosomal and molecular causes are ruled out.

**Q:** What is preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) and when is it indicated?

**A:** Preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) is genetic testing of in vitro fertilized embryos for specific genetic conditions before uterine transfer. It is indicated when a couple is at known risk for a serious genetic condition (e.g., autosomal dominant/recessive single-gene disorders, X-linked conditions, chromosomal rearrangements) and wishes to reduce the risk of having an affected child without proceeding to prenatal diagnosis and possible termination.

PGD involves IVF, embryo biopsy at the 5–6 day blastocyst stage, and molecular genetic testing of the biopsied cells. Only unaffected embryos are transferred. It requires a known familial mutation and prior test validation. Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) screens embryos for chromosomal number abnormalities and is used to improve IVF success rates, particularly in older women.

**Q:** What is the difference between presymptomatic genetic testing and predictive genetic testing, and what ethical considerations apply?

**A:** Presymptomatic genetic testing determines whether individuals without current symptoms but with a known familial pathogenic variant carry that variant (e.g., testing for BRCA1 in a family member of an affected person). Predictive testing (a subset) is used for conditions like Huntington disease where a positive result predicts future disease. Ethical considerations: autonomy (right not to know), informed consent, psychological impact, insurance implications, implications for at-risk relatives, and testing of minors (generally deferred unless intervention in childhood is beneficial).

Genetic counselling before and after predictive testing for conditions like Huntington disease is essential in Canadian practice. The Canadian College of Medical Geneticists and provincial programmes have established protocols. A positive HD test result has implications not only for the individual but for their children who each have a 50% risk. Testing of minors for adult-onset conditions is generally deferred to preserve their future autonomy.

**Q:** What is the significance of a single umbilical artery on a cord examination?

**A:** A single umbilical artery (SUA) — two vessels instead of the normal three (two arteries, one vein) — is associated with an increased risk of other congenital anomalies, particularly renal and cardiac anomalies. It is listed as an associated feature of Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome). Isolated SUA (without other anomalies) is generally a benign variant but warrants a detailed ultrasound examination.

The normal umbilical cord contains two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein. SUA occurs in approximately 1% of singleton pregnancies. When SUA is identified, detailed fetal anatomy ultrasound and consideration of chromosomal testing are recommended because of the association with chromosomal anomalies and structural defects, particularly of the genitourinary and cardiovascular systems.

**Q:** What is Cowden syndrome: gene, associated cancers, and key surveillance?

**A:** Cowden syndrome is autosomal dominant, caused by pathogenic variants in PTEN. Associated cancers: breast, thyroid (follicular and papillary), and endometrial. Surveillance: women: annual mammography from age 30 (or 5–10 years before earliest family breast cancer), consider annual endometrial biopsy/ultrasound; all patients: annual thyroid ultrasound at diagnosis; colonoscopy every 5 years from age 30–35.

PTEN is a tumour suppressor gene encoding a phosphatase that antagonizes the PI3K/AKT pathway. Cowden syndrome features macrocephaly, trichilemmomas, and oral papillomas. Lhermitte-Duclos disease (dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma) is pathognomonic when found in adults. Children should be evaluated for neurodevelopmental disorders. Somatic PTEN mutations are also commonly found in sporadic cancers.

**Q:** What is Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome: gene, associated tumours, and initial screening age?

**A:** VHL syndrome is autosomal dominant, caused by pathogenic variants in the VHL gene. Associated tumours: hemangioblastomas of brain/spinal cord/retina; renal cell carcinoma; pheochromocytoma; kidney cysts; pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour; endolymphatic sac tumour. Surveillance: begins at age 2 with annual physical and ophthalmologic examination; baseline audiometry at school entry; MRI brain, spine, and abdomen at ages 12, 15, and 18; then MRI every 2 years from age 20.

VHL protein is part of an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that targets HIF-1alpha for degradation under normoxic conditions. Loss of VHL leads to constitutive HIF-1alpha activation, promoting VEGF-driven angiogenesis and explaining the vascular tumours (hemangioblastomas) and renal cell carcinoma. Annual catecholamine assessment for pheochromocytoma is recommended from childhood. Retinal hemangioblastomas can cause blindness if untreated.

**Q:** What is Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP): gene, associated cancer, and surveillance protocol?

**A:** FAP is autosomal dominant, caused by pathogenic variants in the APC gene. It causes colorectal cancer (due to hundreds to thousands of colonic adenomatous polyps) and small intestine/stomach tumours. Surveillance: colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy every 1–2 years starting at age 10; once polyps develop, annual colonoscopy until prophylactic colectomy is performed.

Without prophylactic colectomy, virtually all FAP patients develop colorectal cancer by their 40s. APC is a tumour suppressor in the Wnt signalling pathway; biallelic inactivation (germline + somatic) in colonic epithelium leads to polyp formation. Attenuated FAP presents with fewer polyps and later onset. Desmoid tumors (fibrous tissue) occur in ~10–20% and are associated with specific APC mutation location. Duodenal/periampullary adenomas also require surveillance.
