### Quadrilaterals — Definitions, Properties and Areas

A **quadrilateral** is any four-sided figure. The interior angles of *any* quadrilateral add up to **360°** (split it with one diagonal into two triangles: 2 × 180° = 360°).

The special quadrilaterals lie on a "pathway": as you move from a general quadrilateral towards the **square**, properties **accumulate** — each shape keeps everything the earlier ones have and adds its own. The square is the "ultimate" quadrilateral: it is a rectangle *and* a rhombus (and therefore a parallelogram and a kite), so **all** their properties apply to it.

**The shapes (definition · properties · area):**

- **Trapezium** — one pair of opposite sides parallel. Area = ½(a + b)·h, where a and b are the two parallel sides and h the perpendicular distance between them ("half the sum of the parallel sides × the distance between them").
- **Parallelogram** — two pairs of opposite sides parallel. Properties: opposite sides equal; opposite angles equal; **diagonals bisect one another** (but are not equal and not perpendicular in general). Area = base × height (perpendicular height).
- **Rectangle** — a parallelogram with one right angle (so all four angles are 90°). Extra property: **diagonals are equal** (and still bisect each other). Area = ℓ × b.
- **Rhombus** — a parallelogram with one pair of adjacent sides equal (so **all four sides equal**). Diagonals **bisect each other perpendicularly**, bisect the angles, and bisect the area. Area = ½ × product of diagonals (as for a kite) **or** base × height (as for a parallelogram).
- **Kite** — two pairs of *adjacent* sides equal. Diagonals cut perpendicularly, and **one** diagonal bisects the other diagonal, the opposite angles and the area. Area = ½ × product of the diagonals ("half the product of the diagonals").
- **Square** — the rectangle-and-rhombus combination: all sides equal, all angles 90°, diagonals equal, perpendicular, bisecting each other and the angles. Area = s².

**Quick diagonal reference:**
- Equal diagonals: rectangle, square.
- Perpendicular diagonals: rhombus, kite, square.
- Diagonals bisect each other: parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square (in a kite, only one does).

**Worked example:** A rhombus has diagonals 6 cm and 8 cm. Area = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24 cm². (The same ½ × d₁ × d₂ formula works for a kite.)

**Note on the perpendicular diagonals of a rhombus:** at the centre the half-angles satisfy 2x + 2y = 180° (angles of a triangle, or co-interior angles between the parallel sides), so dividing by 2 gives x + y = 90° — confirming the diagonals meet at a right angle.

**Pitfalls:** use the *perpendicular* height for parallelogram/rhombus area, not the slanted side; remember that in a general parallelogram the diagonals are **not** equal; and that a kite has only **one** diagonal that bisects the other.


### Geometry of Straight Lines (Angle Relationships)

**Classifying angles by size:** acute (between 0° and 90°), right (exactly 90°), obtuse (between 90° and 180°), straight (exactly 180°), reflex (between 180° and 360°), revolution (exactly 360°).

**Angles that add up to a special total:**
- **Complementary** angles add up to **90°** (reason: "comp ∠ˢ").
- **Supplementary** angles add up to **180°** (reason: "sup ∠ˢ").

**At a single intersection (two lines crossing):** four angles form.
- **Adjacent** angles share a vertex and an arm; adjacent angles on a straight line are **supplementary** (add to 180°) — reason "∠ˢ on a str line".
- **Vertically opposite** angles sit directly opposite each other and are **EQUAL** — reason "vert opp ∠ˢ".

**Two lines cut by a transversal:** a third line (the **transversal**) cutting two lines makes 8 angles. **Interior** angles lie between the two lines; **exterior** angles lie outside. Three named pairs matter:

| Pair | Shape | Position | Fact when lines are PARALLEL | Reason |
|------|-------|----------|------------------------------|--------|
| Corresponding | F | same position at each crossing | EQUAL | corresp ∠ˢ ; ∥ lines |
| Alternate (interior) | Z | opposite sides of transversal, between lines | EQUAL | alt ∠ˢ ; ∥ lines |
| Co-interior | C / U | same side of transversal, between lines | SUPPLEMENTARY (add to 180°) | co-int ∠ˢ ; ∥ lines |

Important: corresponding and alternate angles are only **equal**, and co-interior angles only **supplementary**, **when the two lines are parallel**. If the lines are not parallel these pairs are *not necessarily* equal/supplementary. (Exterior alternate angles exist but are not used in answers.)

**The FACTS (parallel lines).** When two parallel lines are cut by a transversal: corresponding angles are equal, (interior) alternate angles are equal, and co-interior angles are supplementary.

**The CONVERSE (proving lines parallel).** The two lines are parallel if any one of these holds: corresponding angles equal, OR alternate angles equal, OR co-interior angles supplementary.

**Worked example:** AB ∥ CD are cut by a transversal and one angle is 70°.
- Its corresponding angle = 70° (corresp ∠ˢ ; ∥ lines).
- Its alternate angle = 70° (alt ∠ˢ ; ∥ lines).
- Its co-interior angle = 180° − 70° = 110° (co-int ∠ˢ ; ∥ lines).

**Pitfalls:** always quote "∥ lines" in the reason when you use the parallel-line equality/supplementary facts; do not assume corresponding/alternate angles are equal unless the lines are marked parallel; and identify the pair by its shape — F (corresponding), Z (alternate), C/U (co-interior).


### Euclidean Geometry: Theorem Statements, Acceptable Reasons & Pythagoras

In Grade 9 geometry every statement in a proof must be justified with an **acceptable reason** — the exact short wording the marker expects. Learn the statement AND its reason word-for-word; the abbreviation is worth the mark.

#### Lines and angles
- Adjacent angles on a straight line are supplementary (add to 180°) — **∠s on a str line**.
- If adjacent angles are supplementary, the outer arms form a straight line — **adj ∠s supp**.
- Angles in a revolution add to 360° — **∠s round a pt** OR **∠s in a rev**.
- Vertically opposite angles are equal — **vert opp ∠s**.

#### Parallel lines (always name the parallel lines)
- AB ∥ CD ⇒ alternate angles equal — **alt ∠s; AB ∥ CD**.
- AB ∥ CD ⇒ corresponding angles equal — **corresp ∠s; AB ∥ CD**.
- AB ∥ CD ⇒ co-interior angles supplementary — **co-int ∠s; AB ∥ CD**.
- Converses (to prove lines parallel): **alt ∠s =**, **corresp ∠s =**, **co-int ∠s supp**.

#### Triangles
- Interior angles add to 180° — **∠ sum in △** / **sum of ∠s in △** / **int ∠s in △**.
- Exterior angle = sum of two interior opposite angles — **ext ∠ of △**.
- Isosceles: angles opposite equal sides are equal — **∠s opp equal sides**; converse: sides opposite equal angles are equal — **sides opp equal ∠s**.
- Midpoint theorem: segment joining midpoints of two sides is parallel to the third side and half its length — **Midpt Theorem**; converse: line from a midpoint parallel to another side bisects the third side — **line through midpt ∥ to 2ⁿᵈ side**.
- Proportion: a line parallel to one side divides the other two proportionally — **line ∥ one side of △** / **prop theorem; name ∥ lines**; converse — **line divides two sides of △ in prop**.
- Similarity: equiangular triangles have sides in proportion — **||| △s** / **equiangular △s**; converse — **Sides of △ in prop**.
- Equal areas: triangles/parallelograms on the same base (or equal bases) between the same parallels have equal areas — **same base; same height** / **equal bases; equal height**.

#### Congruency conditions
- **SSS** (three sides), **SAS** / **S∠S** (two sides + included angle), **AAS** / **∠∠S** (two angles + corresponding side), **RHS** / **90°HS** (right angle, hypotenuse, side).

#### Quadrilaterals
- Interior angles add to 360° — **sum of ∠s in quad**.
- Parallelogram: opposite sides parallel and equal — **opp sides of ∥m**; opposite angles equal — **opp ∠s of ∥m**; diagonals bisect each other — **diag of ∥m**; a diagonal bisects its area — **diag bisect area of ∥m**. Converses to prove a parallelogram use phrases like **opp sides of quad are ∥**, **opp sides of quad are =**, **opp ∠s of quad are =**, **diags of quad bisect each other**, **pair of opp sides = and ∥**.
- Rhombus: diagonals bisect each other at right angles and bisect the interior angles, all sides equal — **diags of rhombus** / **sides of rhombus**.
- Square: all sides equal — **sides of square**.
- Rectangle: diagonals equal — **diags of rect**.
- Kite: diagonals intersect at right angles, one diagonal bisects the other and bisects the opposite angles — **diags of kite**.

#### Theorem of Pythagoras
In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides: c² = a² + b², where c (the hypotenuse) is opposite the right angle — reason **Pythagoras** / **Theorem of Pythagoras**.

**Worked example:** legs 6 cm and 8 cm. hyp² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100, so hyp = 10 cm.

**Converse of Pythagoras:** if the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two, the triangle is right-angled — reason **Converse Pythagoras** / **Converse Theorem of Pythagoras**. Use it to PROVE a right angle.

**Worked example:** sides 5, 12, 13. Since 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13², the triangle is right-angled (right angle opposite the 13 side).

**Pitfall:** always quote the reason exactly and, for parallel-line reasons, name the parallel lines (e.g. "alt ∠s; AB ∥ CD"). Marks are lost for vague or missing reasons.


### Geometric Constructions

A construction is a drawing made with only a **compass** and an **unmarked straight-edge** — no protractor for angles and no reading of ruler markings to set lengths (except to lay off a given side). Marks are always given to the construction arcs that reveal your method, so keep all arcs light and visible; do not erase them.

**Perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB.** Open the compass wider than half of AB. From A draw arcs above and below the line; with the same radius from B draw arcs that cut them, at P and Q. The line PQ cuts AB at its midpoint and at 90°. Every point on PQ is the same distance from A and B. If the radius is not wider than half of AB the arcs never meet.

**Bisecting an angle ABC.** From the vertex B draw an arc cutting both arms at D and E. From D and from E (same radius) draw arcs that cross at F. Ray BF bisects the angle, so ∠ABF = ∠FBC.

**Perpendiculars.** *At a point P on the line:* draw an arc from P cutting the line at A and B, then construct the perpendicular bisector of AB. *From a point P off the line:* draw an arc from P cutting the line at A and B, then from A and B draw arcs crossing on the far side at Q; PQ is perpendicular.

**Parallel line through P.** Draw a transversal through P meeting the line at A, then copy the angle at A to P (using the compass to reproduce the arc width). Equal corresponding angles force the lines to be parallel.

**Special angles.** A **60°** angle comes from the equilateral-triangle method: from A draw an arc cutting the line at C; with the same radius from C cut the arc at D; AD makes 60°. **30°** is a bisected 60°; **90°** is a perpendicular at a point; **45°** is a bisected 90°; **15°** is a bisected 30°.

**Triangles and quadrilaterals.** Given three sides (SSS), draw the longest side, then swing two arcs of the other lengths to locate the third vertex — possible only if the two shorter sides add to more than the longest (triangle inequality). Given two sides and the included angle (SAS), draw one side, construct the angle, measure the second side along the new arm. A quadrilateral is built by splitting it along a diagonal into two triangles and constructing each in turn; a rectangle uses two 90° angles and the compass to set the equal sides.


### Triangles — Classification and Angle Properties

Triangles are classified two ways. **By sides:** *equilateral* (all three sides equal, hence all three angles 60°), *isosceles* (two equal sides, with the two base angles opposite them equal), and *scalene* (all sides — and angles — different). **By angles:** *acute* (all angles below 90°), *right-angled* (one 90° angle, with the hypotenuse opposite it as the longest side), and *obtuse* (one angle above 90°). A triangle can hold at most one right or obtuse angle.

**Angle sum.** The three interior angles of any triangle add to 180°. The acceptable reason is "∠ sum of △". To find a missing angle, subtract the known angles from 180°; for angles given in terms of x, add them, set the sum equal to 180°, and solve. For example 3x + 2x + x = 180° gives x = 30°.

**Exterior-angle theorem.** An exterior angle (formed by extending one side) equals the **sum of the two opposite interior angles**; the reason is "ext ∠ of △". This follows because the exterior angle plus its adjacent interior angle make 180° (∠s on a straight line), and the three interior angles also make 180°. So an exterior angle of 125° with one opposite interior angle of 70° gives the other as 125° − 70° = 55°. An exterior angle is always larger than either opposite interior angle.

**Isosceles work.** Identify which side is the odd one out; the equal angles lie *opposite the equal sides* (reason: "∠s opp equal sides"). If a base angle is 65°, the other base angle is also 65° and the apex is 180° − 65° − 65° = 50°. If the apex is given, set each base angle to x and solve 2x + apex = 180°.


### Congruency in Practice

Two triangles are **congruent** (≅) when they have exactly the same shape and size — every pair of corresponding sides and angles is equal — even if one is flipped or turned. When you write △ABC ≅ △DEF the letter order encodes the correspondence: A↔D, B↔E, C↔F, so AB = DE, BC = EF, AC = DF and ∠A = ∠D, and so on. Writing the vertices in the wrong order loses marks.

**The four conditions.** SSS (three sides), SAS (two sides and the *included* angle), AAS (two angles and a corresponding side), and RHS (right angle, hypotenuse, and one other side — for right-angled triangles only). AAA is **not** a congruency condition (it gives only similar triangles) and the arrangement SSA is invalid.

**Setting out a proof.** State the two triangles, then list the three matching pairs each with its own reason ("given", "common", "vertically opposite ∠s", etc.), and finish with the conclusion and the condition in brackets. For SAS the equal angle must sit *between* the two equal sides; for RHS the equal long sides must be the hypotenuses.

**Finding unknowns.** Only *after* proving congruency may you claim that corresponding parts are equal, with the reason "corresp sides/∠s of ≅ △s". So if △ABC ≅ △DEF and DE = 8 cm then AB = 8 cm. Always prove first, then extract the equal part.


### Similarity

Two triangles are **similar** (~) when they have the same shape but not necessarily the same size. This means all corresponding angles are equal *and* all corresponding sides are in the same ratio. Congruency is the special case of similarity where that ratio is 1:1. As with congruency, the letter order in △ABC ~ △DEF fixes the correspondence of angles and sides.

**The equiangular condition.** If two triangles are equiangular — all three pairs of corresponding angles equal — they are similar; the reason is "equiangular △s" or "∠∠∠". You only need to show *two* pairs of equal angles, because the third pair then follows from the angle sum of a triangle.

**Finding a missing side by proportion.** Pair each side with its corresponding side using the letter order, form one ratio equal to another, and cross-multiply. If AB = 4, BC = 6 and DE = 6 in △ABC ~ △DEF, then 4/6 = 6/EF gives EF = 9. The common ratio is the **scale factor**; for a 2:5 scale factor a side of 8 corresponds to a side of 20.

**Applications.** Similar triangles model shadows and heights: a 1,5 m person with a 2 m shadow and a tree with a 10 m shadow give 1,5/2 = h/10, so the tree is 7,5 m tall. The sun's parallel rays make the two triangles equiangular, hence similar.


### Pythagoras Applications

The **Theorem of Pythagoras** applies only to **right-angled triangles**. The side opposite the right angle (90°) is the longest side, called the **hypotenuse**. The theorem states:

> hypotenuse² = (short side)² + (short side)²

If we call the hypotenuse *h* and the two shorter sides *a* and *b*, then:

**h² = a² + b²**     (Pythagoras)

#### Two cases — add or subtract

The single most useful idea is knowing whether to **add** or **subtract**:

- **Finding the hypotenuse** (the longest side): **ADD** the squares of the two shorter sides.
  - h² = a² + b²
- **Finding a shorter side**: **SUBTRACT** the known short side's square from the hypotenuse's square. The hypotenuse (the largest number) goes first.
  - a² = h² − b²

Always finish by taking the **square root** to get the length itself, and remember the answer is a length, so keep the units (cm, m, …).

#### Worked example 1 — finding the hypotenuse

The two shorter sides of a right-angled triangle are 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

```
h² = 6² + 8²     (Pythagoras)
h² = 36 + 64
h² = 100
h  = √100
h  = 10 cm
```

#### Worked example 2 — a real-world (ladder) problem

A 5 m ladder leans against a wall, with its foot 3 m from the base of the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?

First **sketch** the triangle and identify the hypotenuse: it is always opposite the 90° corner. The ladder leans across the right angle formed by the wall and the ground, so the **ladder (5 m) is the hypotenuse**. The ground distance (3 m) is one short side, and the wall height *h* is the other short side. We want a **shorter** side, so subtract:

```
h² = 5² − 3²     (Pythagoras)
h² = 25 − 9
h² = 16
h  = √16
h  = 4 m
```

The ladder reaches **4 m** up the wall.

#### The converse of Pythagoras

The **converse** lets you *test* whether a triangle is right-angled. Compare the square of the **longest** side with the sum of the squares of the other two:

- If longest² = (other)² + (other)², the triangle **is** right-angled (right angle opposite the longest side).
- If they are not equal, it is **not** right-angled.

**Example:** Is a triangle with sides 7, 24 and 25 right-angled?
```
longest²       = 25² = 625
other two: 7² + 24² = 49 + 576 = 625
```
Since 625 = 625, the equation holds. ∴ the triangle **is** right-angled, with the right angle opposite the 25-side.

#### Diagonals of rectangles

A diagonal splits a rectangle into two right-angled triangles, and the diagonal is the **hypotenuse**. For a 9 cm × 12 cm rectangle:
```
d² = 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225
d  = √225 = 15 cm
```

#### Answers as surds

Sometimes the answer is not a whole number and is left as a **surd** (a √ that cannot be evaluated exactly). For short sides 2 cm and 3 cm:
```
h² = 2² + 3² = 4 + 9 = 13
h  = √13 cm
```
√13 cannot be simplified (13 is prime, with no perfect-square factor), so leave it as √13. When a surd *does* have a perfect-square factor, simplify by pulling it out, e.g. √8 = √(4×2) = 2√2.

#### Common pitfalls

- **Adding when you should subtract.** If you are looking for a shorter side, the hypotenuse² goes first and you subtract. Adding gives a wrong (too-large) answer.
- **Forgetting the square root.** h² = 100 is *not* the answer; h = 10 is.
- **Using it on a non-right triangle.** Pythagoras only works when there is a 90° angle.
- **Mislabelling the hypotenuse.** It is always the side opposite the right angle — sketch first and mark it before substituting.


### Transformation Geometry

A **transformation** moves or resizes a shape. The original is the **object** and the result is the **image** (its points are marked with a dash, e.g. A → A'). There are **four** transformations to know:

| Transformation | Plain word | Changes size? | Changes orientation? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Translation | slide | no | no |
| Reflection | flip | no | yes (mirror image) |
| Rotation | turn | no | yes (turned) |
| Enlargement / Reduction | resize | **yes** | no |

Translation, reflection and rotation are **rigid** transformations: they keep size and shape, so the image is **congruent** (≅) to the object. Only an enlargement/reduction changes size, producing an image that is **similar** (~) to the object.

#### 1. Translation (slide)

Every point moves the same distance in the same direction.

**Rule:** (x ; y) → (x + a ; y + b), where *a* is the horizontal shift (right +, left −) and *b* is the vertical shift (up +, down −).

**Example:** Translate A(2 ; −3) by 4 right and 5 up (a = +4, b = +5):
A(2 ; −3) → (2 + 4 ; −3 + 5) = **A'(6 ; 2)**. Size, shape and orientation are unchanged.

#### 2. Reflection (flip)

The image is a **mirror image** across a line; orientation is reversed, but size and shape stay the same.

- Reflection in the **x-axis**: (x ; y) → (x ; −y).  P(3 ; 5) → (3 ; −5)
- Reflection in the **y-axis**: (x ; y) → (−x ; y).  P(3 ; 5) → (−3 ; 5)
- Reflection in the line **y = x**: (x ; y) → (y ; x) — **swap** the coordinates.  P(3 ; 5) → (5 ; 3)

#### 3. Rotation (turn) about the origin

The shape turns about (0 ; 0); size and shape are kept.

- **90° anticlockwise**: (x ; y) → (−y ; x).  B(4 ; 1) → (−1 ; 4)
- **90° clockwise**: (x ; y) → (y ; −x).  B(4 ; 1) → (1 ; −4)
- **180°** (either direction): (x ; y) → (−x ; −y).  B(4 ; 1) → (−4 ; −1)

#### 4. Enlargement / Reduction through the origin

Every coordinate is multiplied by the **scale factor** *k*.

**Rule:** (x ; y) → (kx ; ky).

**Example:** Enlarge C(2 ; −3) by factor 3 (k = 3):
C(2 ; −3) → (3×2 ; 3×−3) = **C'(6 ; −9)**.

- **k > 1** ⇒ enlargement (bigger).
- **0 < k < 1** ⇒ reduction (smaller); e.g. k = ½ halves the shape.

The image is **similar** to the object: same shape, every **length** multiplied by *k*.

#### How enlargement affects length, perimeter and area

- **Length** (and perimeter, which is a total length) is multiplied by **k**.
- **Area** is multiplied by **k²** — the *square* of the scale factor — because area depends on length × length.

**Example:** Under scale factor 4, a shape of area 5 cm² becomes:
image area = 5 × 4² = 5 × 16 = **80 cm²**.
Its perimeter, by contrast, scales by k = 4.

#### Common pitfalls

- **Reflection in y = x:** you swap coordinates (x ; y) → (y ; x); you do **not** change signs.
- **Mixing up the rotation rules:** 90° anticlockwise is (−y ; x); 90° clockwise is (y ; −x). Check by sketching one point.
- **Sign of the shift in a translation:** down and left are **negative**.
- **Area scale factor:** multiply area by **k²**, not by k. This is the most common enlargement error.
- **Confusing congruent and similar:** slides/flips/turns give congruent images (same size); only resizing gives a similar image of different size.


### 3D Objects

A **3D object** (a solid) takes up space and is built from flat or curved surfaces. We describe every solid using three features.

#### Faces, edges and vertices

- **Face:** a flat (or curved) surface of the solid.
- **Edge:** a line segment where two faces meet.
- **Vertex** (plural **vertices**): a corner point where edges meet.

**Example — a cube:** 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices.

To count reliably, work around the solid systematically: count top and bottom faces first, then the side faces; count the edges of the base, the top, and the verticals; count base corners then top corners.

#### Nets

A **net** is a 2D flat pattern that folds up to form a 3D object. It shows every face "opened out" flat, with no gaps or overlaps. Building or drawing a net helps you see exactly which faces a solid has.

- **Cube:** 6 equal squares. A common arrangement is a row of 4 squares with one square above and one below — a cross shape.
- **Cylinder:** 2 circles (the top and bottom) + 1 rectangle (the curved surface "unrolled"). The length of the rectangle equals the **circumference** of the circle, which is 2πr.
- **Rectangular prism (cuboid):** 6 rectangles — 2 identical end rectangles plus the 4 sides folded out around them.
- **Triangular prism:** 2 triangles (the ends) + 3 rectangles (the sides).
- **Square-based pyramid:** 1 square base + 4 triangular faces, one triangle hinged on each side of the square, all meeting at the apex.

A useful check: the number of shapes in a net equals the number of **faces** of the solid.

#### Naming and comparing prisms and pyramids

Both prisms and pyramids are **named after the shape of their base**.

**Prism**
- Has **two identical, parallel bases** joined by rectangles.
- The **cross-section is the same all the way along** its length.
- Examples: triangular prism, rectangular prism (= cuboid), hexagonal prism.

**Pyramid**
- Has **one base**, and all the other faces are **triangles** meeting at a single point called the **apex**.
- Examples: square-based pyramid; triangular pyramid (= tetrahedron).

**Key difference:** a prism has **2 identical bases** and a uniform cross-section; a pyramid has **1 base** and tapers to a **point**.

#### Worked example — describing a triangular prism

A triangular prism has:
- **Faces:** 5 — the 2 triangular ends and the 3 rectangular sides.
- **Edges:** 9 — 3 around each triangular end (3 + 3 = 6) and 3 connecting them lengthways.
- **Vertices:** 6 — 3 at each triangular end.

Its **net** is 2 triangles + 3 rectangles, which matches its 5 faces.

#### Common pitfalls

- **Vertex vs vertices:** "vertex" is one corner; "vertices" is more than one.
- **Edges are where faces meet,** not the same as the number of faces — count them separately.
- **Prism vs pyramid:** if it has two matching ends it is a prism; if it comes to a point it is a pyramid.
- **Naming:** always name by the **base** shape, e.g. a pyramid on a square base is a "square-based pyramid", not just a "pyramid".
- **Net check:** make sure a net has exactly the right number of each face with no piece missing or repeated, and that nothing overlaps when folded.
