# Nephrology

## Acute Kidney Injury

**Q:** A 65-year-old post-operative patient has urine output 15 mL/h and rising creatinine. Urine Na+ is 8 mEq/L, FENa is 0.3%. Diagnosis and management?

**A:** Pre-renal AKI from hypovolemia; treat with IV fluid resuscitation — FENa <1% (or FEUrea <35%) suggests pre-renal cause

KDIGO AKI criteria: creatinine rise ≥26.5 μmol/L in 48h, ≥1.5x baseline in 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6h. FENa <1% indicates avid sodium reabsorption (pre-renal). FENa less reliable in CKD, contrast nephropathy, or myoglobinuria.

**Q:** What are the KDIGO stages of AKI?

**A:** Stage 1: creatinine ×1.5-1.9 baseline or rise ≥26.5 μmol/L, UO <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6-12h; Stage 2: creatinine ×2-2.9 baseline, UO <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥12h; Stage 3: creatinine ×3 or >354 μmol/L, UO <0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥24h or anuria ≥12h

KDIGO 2012 AKI guidelines define staging for prognosis and management. Higher stages are associated with increased mortality and need for renal replacement therapy. Any AKI requires follow-up at 3 months to assess for CKD development.

**Q:** What are the main intrinsic causes of AKI organized by nephron segment?

**A:** Tubular: ATN (ischemia, nephrotoxins — aminoglycosides, contrast, myoglobin); Glomerular: GN, vasculitis, TTP/HUS; Interstitial: AIN (drugs, infection); Vascular: renal artery/vein thrombosis

ATN from ischemia or nephrotoxins is the most common cause of intrinsic AKI in hospital. Urine sediment: muddy brown granular casts = ATN; RBC casts = GN; WBC casts, eosinophils = AIN.

**Q:** What medications should be held during AKI?

**A:** ACEi/ARBs, NSAIDs, diuretics (if volume-depleted), aminoglycosides, metformin (risk of lactic acidosis), contrast agents, methotrexate — adjust doses for renally-cleared drugs

ACEi/ARBs reduce GFR by dilating efferent arteriole, which is beneficial in stable CKD but harmful in hypovolemic states or bilateral renal artery stenosis. NSAID-induced renal vasoconstriction worsens pre-renal AKI.

**Q:** What is contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN) and how is it prevented?

**A:** Transient AKI within 24-48h of contrast administration; prevention: pre-hydration with IV NS or sodium bicarbonate, minimize contrast volume, use iso-osmolar/low-osmolar contrast, hold nephrotoxic drugs

CIN risk factors: CKD (eGFR <60), diabetes, volume depletion, heart failure, large contrast volume, multiple exposures. NAC is no longer recommended for CIN prevention (PRESERVE trial). Metformin held for 48h post-contrast if AKI occurs.

**Q:** A 70-year-old has sudden oliguria, creatinine rose from 90 to 320 μmol/L in 24 hours with no medication changes. U/A shows RBC casts. Diagnosis?

**A:** Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN) — emergency: requires urgent renal biopsy and immunosuppression

RPGN has three patterns: anti-GBM (Goodpasture), pauci-immune (ANCA-associated vasculitis — most common), and immune complex GN (lupus, IgA). Diagnosis requires urgent renal biopsy. Treatment: pulse IV methylprednisolone + cyclophosphamide ± plasma exchange.

**Q:** What are the indications for emergent renal replacement therapy (RRT) in AKI?

**A:** AEIOU: Acidosis (pH <7.2), Electrolyte disorder (K+ >6.5 or refractory), Ingestion (toxin removable by dialysis), Overload (refractory pulmonary edema), Uremia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding)

RRT modalities: hemodialysis (HD) for hemodynamically stable; continuous RRT (CVVHDF) for hemodynamically unstable. Timing: do not delay when absolute indications present. AKIKI and IDEAL trials guide timing for non-urgent situations.

**Q:** What is acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) and what drugs cause it?

**A:** AIN: immune-mediated tubulointerstitial inflammation; most common drug causes: NSAIDs, PPIs (most common), penicillins, sulfonamides, allopurinol, rifampin, checkpoint inhibitors

Classic triad of AIN: fever + rash + eosinophilia (present in only 10% of drug-induced AIN). Urine eosinophils unreliable. Renal biopsy is gold standard. Treatment: stop offending drug; corticosteroids if no improvement in 5-7 days.

**Q:** A 55-year-old has AKI, thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), and neurological symptoms. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) — pentad: MAHA + thrombocytopenia + neurological symptoms + fever + renal failure; ADAMTS13 activity <10%

TTP is a medical emergency: treat with plasma exchange (plasmapheresis) to replace ADAMTS13 and remove antibodies. HUS (hemolytic uremic syndrome) is more renal-predominant, usually from Shiga toxin-producing E. coli 0157:H7 in children.

**Q:** How does rhabdomyolysis cause AKI and how is it managed?

**A:** Myoglobin causes tubular toxicity and cast formation; manage with aggressive IV saline (200-1000 mL/h) to maintain urine output >200 mL/h until myoglobin cleared

Rhabdomyolysis causes: trauma (crush injury), seizures, strenuous exercise, drugs (statins, cocaine), hyperthermia. CK >10x ULN with elevated creatinine indicates rhabdomyolysis. Target urine output high to prevent cast formation in tubules.

**Q:** What is the urinalysis finding in different types of AKI?

**A:** Pre-renal: hyaline casts, concentrated urine; ATN: muddy brown granular casts, tubular epithelial cells; AIN: WBC casts, RBC casts, possibly eosinophiluria; RPGN: RBC casts, dysmorphic RBCs; Post-renal: may be normal or have WBCs

Urinalysis with microscopy is essential in AKI workup. RBC casts indicate glomerular origin of bleeding (GN). WBC casts indicate tubular or interstitial infection/inflammation. Muddy brown casts indicate tubular necrosis.

**Q:** What is the initial evaluation of a patient presenting with AKI?

**A:** History (medications, contrast, urinary symptoms), physical exam (volume status), urine dipstick and microscopy, urine electrolytes (FENa, FEUrea), urine:serum creatinine ratio, renal ultrasound (exclude obstruction)

Renal ultrasound should be performed in all unexplained AKI to exclude obstruction (post-renal AKI). Bilateral hydronephrosis suggests bladder outlet obstruction. Post-renal AKI is reversible with prompt decompression.

**Q:** A 60-year-old man has complete anuria for 24 hours. What is the most likely diagnosis and immediate management?

**A:** Bladder outlet obstruction (BPH, prostate cancer, urethral stricture) causing post-renal AKI — insert Foley catheter immediately

Complete anuria strongly suggests urinary obstruction. Post-renal AKI accounts for 5-10% of AKI cases. Renal ultrasound confirms hydronephrosis. Prompt decompression is essential as prolonged obstruction causes irreversible tubular damage. Post-obstructive diuresis can cause life-threatening electrolyte disorders.

**Q:** What are the main differences between AKI and CKD on investigation?

**A:** AKI: acute onset, often reversible, normal or near-normal kidney size on U/S, no prior creatinine elevation; CKD: gradual onset, bilateral small echogenic kidneys, prior elevated creatinine

Distinguishing AKI from CKD affects treatment decisions. Small kidneys on ultrasound indicate longstanding CKD with fibrosis. Normal or enlarged kidneys in CKD suggest: diabetic nephropathy, amyloidosis, polycystic kidney disease, HIV nephropathy.

**Q:** What is the approach to AKI in a patient with suspected sepsis?

**A:** Fluid resuscitation with 30 mL/kg crystalloid bolus for sepsis-induced AKI, vasopressors (norepinephrine) for MAP <65, remove nephrotoxic drugs, avoid aminoglycosides, treat infection source

Sepsis-associated AKI is the most common cause of AKI in ICU. Surviving Sepsis Campaign and KDIGO guidelines recommend avoiding hyperchloremic solutions (excess NS); balanced crystalloids (LR) preferred to reduce AKI risk. Aggressive fluid therapy beyond initial resuscitation may worsen outcomes.

**Q:** What are KDIGO AKI staging criteria?

**A:** Stage 1: Cr 1.5-1.9x or >=26.5 rise or UO<0.5/6-12h. Stage 2: 2-2.9x. Stage 3: >=3x or >=353.6 or RRT

Unified from RIFLE/AKIN. AKI defined as Cr increase >=26.5 within 48h OR >=1.5x within 7 days.

**Q:** How differentiate prerenal from intrinsic AKI?

**A:** Prerenal: FENa<1%, UOsm>500, BUN/Cr>20. ATN: FENa>2%, UOsm<350, BUN/Cr<15

FENa unreliable with diuretics - use FEUrea<35%. Muddy brown casts = ATN. RBC casts = GN.

**Q:** What are emergent dialysis indications?

**A:** AEIOU: Acidosis refractory, Electrolytes (K>6.5 refractory), Intoxication, Overload, Uremia (encephalopathy/pericarditis)

IHD for stable patients, CRRT for hemodynamically unstable ICU patients.

**Q:** What are the KDIGO staging criteria for AKI?

**A:** Stage 1: Cr rise ≥26.5 μmol/L in 48h OR 1.5-1.9x baseline within 7 days OR UO <0.5 mL/kg/h x 6-12h. Stage 2: Cr 2.0-2.9x baseline OR UO <0.5 mL/kg/h x ≥12h. Stage 3: Cr ≥3x baseline OR Cr ≥353.6 μmol/L OR initiation of RRT OR UO <0.3 mL/kg/h x ≥24h OR anuria x 12h.

KDIGO unified RIFLE and AKIN criteria. Any AKI stage independently increases mortality and CKD risk. Cr may lag 24-48h behind actual GFR decline. Oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h) is often an earlier marker than Cr rise. Novel biomarkers (NGAL, KIM-1) may detect AKI earlier.

**Q:** How do you differentiate prerenal, intrinsic renal, and postrenal AKI using urine indices?

**A:** Prerenal: FENa <1%, UOsm >500, urine Na <20, BUN:Cr >40:1, bland sediment. Intrinsic (ATN): FENa >2%, UOsm <350, urine Na >40, BUN:Cr <20:1, muddy brown casts. Postrenal: variable indices, hydronephrosis on US.

FENa = (UNa × PCr)/(PNa × UCr) × 100. FENa is unreliable with diuretic use — use FEUrea instead (<35% prerenal, >50% intrinsic). Urine microscopy is essential: RBC casts = glomerulonephritis, WBC casts = interstitial nephritis/pyelonephritis, muddy brown granular casts = ATN.

**Q:** What are the emergent indications for dialysis in AKI (mnemonic AEIOU)?

**A:** A=Acidosis (severe metabolic pH <7.1 refractory to bicarb), E=Electrolytes (refractory hyperkalemia >6.5 with ECG changes), I=Ingestion (toxic alcohols, lithium, salicylates), O=Overload (fluid overload refractory to diuretics), U=Uremia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding).

These are absolute indications where delay in RRT increases mortality. Relative indications: progressive azotemia, electrolyte derangements trending toward dangerous levels. CRRT (continuous RRT) preferred in hemodynamically unstable ICU patients. Intermittent HD for stable patients or urgent toxin removal.

**Q:** What is the stepwise management of hyperkalemia?

**A:** 1) Stabilize myocardium: IV calcium gluconate 10% 10mL over 2-3 min (if ECG changes). 2) Shift K+ intracellularly: IV insulin 10U + D50W, salbutamol nebs 10-20mg. 3) Remove K+: sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate), loop diuretics (if volume tolerant), dialysis if refractory.

Calcium gluconate does NOT lower K+ — it stabilizes cardiac membrane within minutes, lasts 30-60 min. Insulin+glucose works in 15-30 min, lasts 4-6h (monitor for hypoglycemia). Sodium bicarbonate only effective if concurrent acidosis. IV calcium chloride via central line if cardiac arrest. Patiromer/SZC for chronic hyperkalemia.

**Q:** What are the common nephrotoxic drugs that cause AKI?

**A:** Prerenal: NSAIDs (afferent constriction), ACEi/ARBs (efferent dilation), calcineurin inhibitors. ATN: aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin, IV contrast (CI-AKI), vancomycin. AIN: NSAIDs, penicillins, cephalosporins, PPIs, sulfonamides, allopurinol. Crystalline: acyclovir, methotrexate, sulfonamides.

Drug-induced AKI accounts for 20% of hospital AKI. Contrast-induced AKI: prevent with IV saline hydration pre/post-procedure; NAC benefit unproven. AIN triad (rash, fever, eosinophilia) present in <30%; eosinophiluria supportive. Stop offending agent; steroids for AIN if no recovery in 5-7 days.

**Q:** What is the approach to oliguric AKI in the ICU?

**A:** 1) Assess volume status (JVP, lung exam, IVC US). 2) If hypovolemic: IV crystalloid challenge (250-500mL bolus). 3) If euvolemic/hypervolemic with no response: avoid further fluids, trial of furosemide stress test (1-1.5 mg/kg IV). 4) Persistent oliguria: consider CRRT.

Furosemide stress test: UO >200 mL in 2h after 1.5 mg/kg IV furosemide predicts recovery. Failure predicts stage 3 AKI and need for RRT. Avoid dopamine for 'renal dose' — no benefit and potential harm. Goal-directed fluid management prevents both hypovolemia and fluid overload-associated AKI.

**Q:** What is contrast-induced AKI (CI-AKI) and how is it prevented?

**A:** Cr rise ≥26.5 μmol/L or ≥25% within 48-72h of contrast. Prevention: IV NS or NaHCO3 1-3 mL/kg/h starting 1h pre-procedure. Minimize contrast volume. Avoid concurrent nephrotoxins. Hold metformin (restart 48h post if Cr stable). NAC benefit uncertain.

Risk factors: CKD (eGFR <30 highest risk), diabetes, heart failure, hypovolemia, high contrast volume, arterial > venous contrast. Iso-osmolar contrast may be safer. CI-AKI is usually self-limited (peaks day 3-5, resolves 7-14 days) but can progress to dialysis-requiring AKI in high-risk patients.

**Q:** What is hepatorenal syndrome and how is it differentiated from other causes of AKI in cirrhosis?

**A:** HRS: AKI in cirrhosis with ascites, no improvement after 2 days of albumin (1g/kg/day, max 100g/day) and diuretic withdrawal, no shock, no nephrotoxins, no parenchymal kidney disease (bland urine sediment, no proteinuria, normal renal US).

HRS is a diagnosis of exclusion. FENa is <1% (similar to prerenal) because the tubules are functioning normally — the problem is extreme renal vasoconstriction. Treatment: terlipressin + albumin (or norepinephrine + albumin in ICU). Liver transplant is definitive treatment.

**Q:** What is rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI and how is it managed?

**A:** Massive muscle breakdown → myoglobin release → renal tubular obstruction + direct tubular toxicity + renal vasoconstriction. Diagnosis: CK >5x ULN (typically >5000-10000), dark urine (dipstick positive for blood, no RBCs on microscopy). Management: aggressive IV NS (target UO 200-300 mL/h), avoid bicarbonate unless severe acidosis.

Common causes: crush injury, statins, seizures, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, compartment syndrome. Complications: hyperkalemia (severe, early), hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia (early), hypercalcemia (recovery phase), DIC. Monitor CK, K+, Ca, phosphate q6-8h. Dialysis for refractory hyperkalemia or volume overload.

**Q:** What is the RIFLE classification for AKI?

**A:** Risk: Cr 1.5x or UO <0.5 mL/kg/h x 6h. Injury: Cr 2x or UO <0.5 x 12h. Failure: Cr 3x or Cr >354 or UO <0.3 x 24h or anuria x 12h. Loss: persistent AKI >4 weeks. ESKD: persistent >3 months.

RIFLE was the first consensus AKI definition (2004), followed by AKIN (2007), then KDIGO (2012) which unified both. KDIGO is now the standard. All systems use Cr rise and/or UO decrease. KDIGO added the absolute Cr rise ≥26.5 criterion for earlier detection (from AKIN).

**Q:** What is thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and how do HUS and TTP present?

**A:** TMA triad: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes, negative Coombs), thrombocytopenia, organ damage. HUS: AKI-predominant (often diarrhea-associated E. coli O157:H7 in children — Shiga toxin). TTP: neurological symptoms predominant, ADAMTS13 <10%.

Typical HUS (STEC-HUS): supportive care only, NO antibiotics (may increase toxin release), NO platelets. Atypical HUS: complement-mediated, treat with eculizumab (anti-C5). TTP: URGENT plasma exchange (PEX) + steroids + caplacizumab (anti-vWF). Do not delay PEX for ADAMTS13 result if clinical suspicion high.

**Q:** What is the cardiorenal syndrome classification?

**A:** Type 1: acute heart failure → AKI. Type 2: chronic heart failure → progressive CKD. Type 3: AKI → acute cardiac dysfunction. Type 4: CKD → chronic cardiac disease. Type 5: systemic condition (sepsis, amyloid) → both cardiac and renal dysfunction.

Type 1 is most common in clinical practice. Mechanism: low cardiac output → renal hypoperfusion + venous congestion → AKI. Management: careful diuresis (decongestion improves renal function despite Cr rise from hemoconcentration), avoid excessive fluid restriction. Ultrafiltration if diuretic-resistant.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for AKI in hospitalized patients?

**A:** Pre-existing CKD (strongest risk factor), diabetes, heart failure, liver disease, age >65, hypovolemia/sepsis, nephrotoxic drug exposure, major surgery (especially cardiac), IV contrast, obstruction, multiple myeloma

Hospital-acquired AKI occurs in 10-15% of admissions, >50% in ICU. Prevention: avoid nephrotoxins when possible, goal-directed fluid therapy, monitor UO and Cr, hold ACEi/ARBs and diuretics in acute illness. AKI survivors have increased long-term CKD and mortality risk — follow-up with Cr monitoring.

**Q:** What is abdominal compartment syndrome and how does it cause AKI?

**A:** Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) >20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction. Causes AKI by compressing renal veins and parenchyma → decreased renal perfusion and GFR. Measured via intravesical (bladder) pressure transducer.

Risk factors: massive fluid resuscitation, abdominal surgery, pancreatitis, trauma, liver transplant. IAP >12 = intra-abdominal hypertension; >20 with organ failure = abdominal compartment syndrome. Treatment: decompressive laparotomy if medical measures (NG drainage, sedation, neuromuscular blockade) fail.

**Q:** What is tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) and how does it cause AKI?

**A:** Rapid lysis of tumor cells (high tumor burden hematologic malignancies) releasing K+, phosphate, uric acid, nucleic acids. AKI from uric acid crystal and calcium phosphate deposition in tubules. Labs: hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia.

Prevention (high risk: Burkitt, ALL, aggressive NHL): aggressive IV hydration + allopurinol or rasburicase (urate oxidase, breaks down uric acid — contraindicated in G6PD deficiency). Monitor q6-8h. Treatment: rasburicase, RRT if refractory. Cairo-Bishop criteria define laboratory and clinical TLS.

**Q:** What urinalysis findings point to specific diagnoses?

**A:** WBC+nitrites: UTI. RBC casts: glomerulonephritis. WBC casts: pyelonephritis/interstitial nephritis. Muddy brown casts: ATN. Oval fat bodies: nephrotic syndrome. Crystals: kidney stones (calcium oxalate, uric acid)

Dipstick protein detects albumin only (not light chains — need urine protein electrophoresis for myeloma). Myoglobinuria: positive blood on dipstick, negative RBCs on microscopy.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of acute kidney injury in prerenal, intrinsic, and postrenal causes?

**A:** Prerenal: decreased renal perfusion (hypovolemia, HF, sepsis) with intact nephrons. Intrinsic: direct nephron damage (ATN, GN, AIN). Postrenal: urinary outflow obstruction (BPH, stones, tumor)

Prerenal → intrinsic if prolonged: sustained hypoperfusion causes ATN. Intrinsic damage: ischemic ATN (tubular necrosis from hypoperfusion), toxic ATN (aminoglycosides, contrast), GN (immune complex), AIN (drug-induced).

## Chronic Kidney Disease

**Q:** How is CKD defined and staged per KDIGO guidelines?

**A:** CKD: kidney damage or eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m2 for ≥3 months; Stages: G1 (≥90), G2 (60-89), G3a (45-59), G3b (30-44), G4 (15-29), G5 (<15); also staged by albuminuria (A1/A2/A3)

KDIGO 2012 CKD guidelines define CKD by chronicity (≥3 months) to distinguish from AKI. Prognosis determined by combined GFR stage + albuminuria category. High-risk CKD: G3b-G5 or high albuminuria (A3 = ACR ≥30 mg/mmol).

**Q:** What are the two most common causes of CKD in Canada?

**A:** Diabetes mellitus (30-40%) and hypertension (25-30%) — together account for most CKD cases requiring dialysis

Diabetic nephropathy typically presents with microalbuminuria progressing to macroalbuminuria then declining GFR. CKD Canada guidelines recommend RAAS blockade (ACEi/ARB) for all CKD patients with albuminuria regardless of BP.

**Q:** What pharmacological interventions slow CKD progression?

**A:** ACEi/ARB (reduce proteinuria and intraglomerular pressure), SGLT2 inhibitors (canagliflozin/dapagliflozin reduce CKD progression), strict BP control, blood glucose control in diabetes, avoid nephrotoxins

CREDENCE and DAPA-CKD trials showed SGLT2 inhibitors reduce CKD progression and cardiovascular events in CKD patients regardless of diabetes status. CKD Canada 2022 guidelines recommend SGLT2 inhibitors for CKD G2-4 with ACR ≥20 mg/mmol.

**Q:** A 58-year-old diabetic with CKD has Hgb 95 g/L, MCV normal, ferritin 180 ng/mL, transferrin saturation 22%. Diagnosis and treatment?

**A:** Anemia of CKD (normocytic normochromic anemia from reduced erythropoietin); treat with erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) + iron to maintain Hgb 100-115 g/L — ensure iron replete first

CKD Canada guidelines recommend iron supplementation to achieve ferritin >200 ng/mL and TSAT >20% before starting ESA. ESA (darbepoetin, epoetin alfa) target Hgb 100-115 g/L (not higher — increased stroke/thrombosis risk). Oral vs. IV iron based on severity.

**Q:** What is the cause and management of hyperphosphatemia in CKD?

**A:** Reduced phosphate excretion in CKD → secondary hyperparathyroidism → renal osteodystrophy; treat with dietary phosphate restriction, phosphate binders (calcium carbonate, sevelamer, lanthanum)

CKD-MBD (mineral bone disease): hyperphosphatemia + hypocalcemia → elevated PTH → bone resorption. Sevelamer and lanthanum preferred over calcium-based binders to avoid calcium loading. Activated vitamin D (calcitriol) suppresses PTH.

**Q:** What BP target and agents are recommended for CKD patients with hypertension?

**A:** Target <130/80 mmHg; ACEi or ARB first-line for CKD with proteinuria (A2/A3) — provide nephroprotection beyond BP lowering

RAAS blockade reduces intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria. Monitor creatinine and potassium at 1-2 weeks after initiation. Up to 30% creatinine rise acceptable if stable. Combination ACEi + ARB increases AKI and hyperkalemia risk — avoid.

**Q:** What metabolic acidosis occurs in CKD and when should it be treated?

**A:** Type 4 RTA (hyperkalemic distal RTA) in early CKD; later, reduced NH4+ excretion; treat metabolic acidosis when serum bicarbonate <22 mmol/L with sodium bicarbonate supplements

KDIGO guidelines recommend treating metabolic acidosis in CKD (target bicarbonate ≥22 mmol/L) to slow CKD progression, prevent muscle catabolism, and prevent bone buffering. Sodium bicarbonate 0.5-1 mmol/kg/day titrated to bicarbonate >22.

**Q:** When should patients with CKD be referred to nephrology?

**A:** eGFR <30 (G4-G5), rapid eGFR decline (>5 mL/min/year), ACR >300 mg/mmol, resistant hypertension, unexplained AKI, hereditary kidney disease, suspected GN

CKD Canada guidelines recommend nephrology referral to prepare for renal replacement therapy, manage CKD-MBD, anemia, and cardiovascular risk. Early referral (eGFR 30-60) improves outcomes and reduces late referral (<3 months before dialysis).

**Q:** What are the renal replacement therapy options for end-stage kidney disease?

**A:** Hemodialysis (in-centre or home), peritoneal dialysis (CAPD or APD), or kidney transplantation — preferred in order of outcome benefit: transplant > PD or home HD > in-centre HD

CKD Canada recommends kidney transplant as the optimal RRT. Living donor transplant preferred over deceased donor. Peritoneal dialysis is equivalent to hemodialysis in appropriate patients and may be preferred for residual renal function preservation.

**Q:** What dietary recommendations are made for CKD patients?

**A:** Protein restriction 0.6-0.8 g/kg/day (stage 3-5 not on dialysis), sodium <2.3 g/day, phosphate restriction, potassium restriction (if hyperkalemic), adequate caloric intake

CKD Canada dietary guidelines recommend low-protein diet to reduce uremic solute accumulation and slow CKD progression. Dialysis patients need higher protein (1.0-1.2 g/kg/day) to replace dialysis-related losses.

**Q:** A 65-year-old with CKD G4 has PTH 450 pg/mL and calcium 2.55 mmol/L. Management?

**A:** Secondary hyperparathyroidism in CKD; treat with vitamin D receptor activator (calcitriol or paricalcitol) and phosphate binder — cinacalcet if PTH remains elevated despite these measures

PTH targets in CKD-MBD: CKD G3-G5 not on dialysis: maintain PTH in 2-9x upper limit of normal. Calcitriol suppresses PTH synthesis. Cinacalcet (calcimimetic) reduces PTH by sensitizing calcium-sensing receptor. Parathyroidectomy for severe refractory hyperparathyroidism.

**Q:** What cardiovascular risk does CKD impose and how should it be managed?

**A:** CKD is an independent cardiovascular risk factor; high-intensity statin recommended for all CKD patients (SHARP trial), blood pressure control, lifestyle modification, aspirin for secondary prevention

CKD patients have 10-20x higher cardiovascular mortality than age-matched general population. SHARP trial showed rosuvastatin/ezetimibe reduces major atherosclerotic events in CKD. Statin reduces LDL but survival benefit less clear once dialysis dependent.

**Q:** What is the approach to managing potassium in CKD patients?

**A:** Dietary potassium restriction (avoid high-K+ foods: bananas, citrus, potatoes), adjust medications (reduce/hold ACEi/ARB if K+ >5.5), consider patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate

Hyperkalemia is common in CKD, especially with RAAS blockade. Novel potassium binders allow continuation of life-saving GDMT (ACEi/ARB, MRA, SGLT2i). Emergency hyperkalemia: calcium gluconate, insulin + dextrose, sodium bicarbonate, kayexalate, dialysis.

**Q:** What is diabetic nephropathy and how does it progress?

**A:** Diabetic nephropathy progression: hyperfiltration → microalbuminuria (ACR 3-30 mg/mmol) → macroalbuminuria (ACR >30 mg/mmol) → declining GFR → ESKD

Tight glycemic control (HbA1c <7%) and RAAS blockade slow progression. SGLT2 inhibitors further reduce proteinuria and CKD progression independent of glucose control. Annual ACR and eGFR monitoring in all diabetics per CDA guidelines.

**Q:** When is vascular access planning initiated for hemodialysis in CKD?

**A:** Nephrology referral at eGFR <30; arteriovenous fistula (AVF) creation when eGFR <15-20 or within 6 months of anticipated dialysis start — AVF is preferred over graft over tunneled catheter

AVF has lowest infection and thrombosis rates and best long-term patency. CKD Canada recommends protecting non-dominant forearm veins (avoid IV lines and blood draws in that arm) in CKD patients who may need hemodialysis.

**Q:** What are KDIGO CKD stages?

**A:** G1:>=90(with damage), G2:60-89, G3a:45-59, G3b:30-44, G4:15-29, G5:<15

CKD = abnormalities >3 months. Albuminuria: A1<30, A2 30-300, A3>300 mg/g. Both determine risk.

**Q:** What BP target for CKD with albuminuria?

**A:** <130/80 with ACEi or ARB first-line. Without albuminuria: <140/90

No ACEi+ARB combo. Tolerate 30% Cr rise. SGLT2i now recommended for CKD eGFR 20-75.

**Q:** What are the KDIGO CKD stages by GFR and albuminuria?

**A:** G1: GFR ≥90; G2: 60-89; G3a: 45-59; G3b: 30-44; G4: 15-29; G5: <15. Albuminuria: A1 <3 mg/mmol; A2 3-30 (moderately increased); A3 >30 (severely increased). CKD diagnosis requires abnormality >3 months.

Staging uses both GFR and albuminuria (heat map). A patient with GFR 55 and ACR 35 is G3aA3 — high risk for progression. eGFR calculated by CKD-EPI equation (race-free in Canada since 2022). Cause-GFR-Albuminuria (CGA) classification recommended.

**Q:** When should nephrology referral occur in CKD?

**A:** eGFR <30 (G4-G5), rapidly declining GFR (>5 mL/min/year), ACR >60 mg/mmol, refractory hypertension, persistent hyperkalemia, unexplained CKD, suspected glomerulonephritis, hereditary kidney disease

Early nephrology referral (eGFR <30) allows dialysis access planning (AVF creation takes 3-6 months to mature), transplant workup, management of CKD complications, and advance care planning. Canadian Kidney Knowledge Translation and Generation Network (CANN-NET) supports multidisciplinary CKD care.

**Q:** What is the management of CKD-mineral bone disease (CKD-MBD)?

**A:** Monitor Ca, PO4, PTH, vitamin D. Restrict dietary phosphate; phosphate binders (calcium-based if Ca normal, or non-calcium: sevelamer, lanthanum). Correct 25-OH vitamin D deficiency. Active vitamin D (calcitriol) or analogues for secondary hyperparathyroidism in G4-5. Target: avoid extreme PTH values.

KDIGO recommends treating trends rather than specific PTH targets. Progressively elevated PTH with high phosphate indicates secondary hyperparathyroidism. Calcimimetics (cinacalcet) for dialysis patients with persistent hyperparathyroidism. Parathyroidectomy for refractory tertiary hyperparathyroidism. CKD-MBD increases fracture and cardiovascular risk.

**Q:** How is anemia of CKD managed?

**A:** Target Hb 100-115 g/L (do not normalize). First: replete iron (ferritin >500, TSAT >30% target in dialysis patients; IV iron preferred). Then: ESA (erythropoietin/darbepoetin) if Hb <100 despite iron repletion. HIF-PHI (roxadustat) is oral alternative.

CKD anemia results from decreased EPO production + iron deficiency + uremic toxins + shortened RBC survival. Higher Hb targets (>130) with ESAs increase cardiovascular events, stroke, and death (TREAT, CREATE trials). Check reticulocyte count, iron studies, B12/folate before starting ESA.

**Q:** What medications slow CKD progression?

**A:** ACEi/ARBs (first-line for proteinuric CKD, target BP <130/80). SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin — benefit regardless of diabetes status if eGFR ≥20 and ACR >20). Finerenone (non-steroidal MRA) for diabetic CKD. GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide) for diabetic CKD.

DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY trials showed SGLT2i reduce CKD progression by 30-40% in both diabetic and non-diabetic CKD. Start even with initial eGFR dip (hemodynamic, expected, reversible). Do not combine ACEi + ARB (ONTARGET — no benefit, more hyperkalemia). Canadian guidelines integrate SGLT2i as standard of care.

**Q:** What are the dialysis modalities and how do patients choose?

**A:** Hemodialysis (HD): in-centre (3x/week x 4h) or home HD (nocturnal or short daily). Peritoneal dialysis (PD): continuous ambulatory (CAPD, 4 exchanges/day) or automated (APD, cycler overnight). PD offers lifestyle flexibility, home-based; HD is more efficient per session.

In Canada, PD-first approach is encouraged when appropriate. AVF is preferred vascular access (lowest infection/thrombosis); create when eGFR <15-20 or expected dialysis within 6-12 months. Catheter HD has highest complication rates. Shared decision-making considering patient preference, social situation, and comorbidities is essential.

**Q:** What is the conservative/palliative approach to advanced CKD?

**A:** Conservative kidney management (CKM): maximal medical management without dialysis for patients who choose not to start or to withdraw from dialysis. Includes symptom management, advance care planning, palliative care referral, dietary modification, and psychosocial support.

For elderly patients (>80) with multiple comorbidities, dialysis may not improve quality or quantity of life compared to CKM. Canadian Society of Nephrology supports shared decision-making and recognizes CKM as a valid choice. Important to address prognosis honestly — surprise question: 'Would you be surprised if this patient died within 12 months?'

**Q:** What dietary modifications are recommended in CKD?

**A:** Protein: 0.8 g/kg/day (G3-5, not on dialysis); higher on dialysis (1.2 g/kg/day). Sodium <2g/day. Potassium restriction if hyperkalemic (G4-5). Phosphate restriction (processed foods, dairy). Adequate caloric intake (25-35 kcal/kg/day).

Protein restriction slows CKD progression but must balance against malnutrition risk (PEW: protein-energy wasting). Renal dietitian referral is essential. On dialysis, protein needs increase (dialysate losses). Plant-based proteins may be preferred (lower phosphorus bioavailability). DASH diet modified for CKD (watch K+ and PO4).

**Q:** What cardiovascular risk management is needed in CKD?

**A:** CKD is a coronary risk equivalent. BP target <130/80 (ACEi/ARB first-line). Statins: atorvastatin in G3-5 not on dialysis (SHARP trial). ASA for secondary prevention. SGLT2i for cardiorenal protection. Avoid starting statins on dialysis (4D, AURORA trials showed no benefit).

CV disease is the #1 cause of death in CKD (not ESKD). CKD accelerates atherosclerosis AND vascular calcification. LVH is common due to volume overload, anemia, and hypertension. Smoking cessation, glycemic control (A1C target ~7%, but less reliable in advanced CKD — use CGM), and weight management are all important.

**Q:** What is the metabolic acidosis management in CKD?

**A:** CKD causes non-anion gap metabolic acidosis (impaired ammoniagenesis) progressing to anion gap in advanced CKD. Maintain serum bicarb ≥22 mmol/L. Treatment: oral sodium bicarbonate 0.5-1 mEq/kg/day. Slows CKD progression and reduces muscle wasting.

Metabolic acidosis in CKD contributes to muscle catabolism, bone disease, hyperkalemia, and accelerated GFR decline. Fruits and vegetables (alkali-producing diet) can also help correct mild acidosis. Monitor sodium load with bicarbonate supplementation in patients with edema/hypertension.

**Q:** What are the indications and types of kidney transplantation?

**A:** Indications: ESKD or eGFR <20 with progressive decline. Types: living donor (related or unrelated — best outcomes), deceased donor (brain death or donation after circulatory death). Pre-emptive transplant (before dialysis) has best outcomes.

Kidney transplant offers superior survival and quality of life compared to dialysis. Canadian Blood Services manages organ allocation. Contraindications: active malignancy, active infection, severe cardiac/pulmonary disease, active substance abuse, non-adherence. ABO-incompatible and paired kidney exchange programs expand donor pools.

**Q:** What drugs require dose adjustment in CKD?

**A:** Renally cleared drugs needing adjustment: metformin (hold if eGFR <30), DOACs (reduce dose per eGFR), digoxin, gabapentin/pregabalin, opioids (avoid morphine/meperidine — active metabolites), aminoglycosides, vancomycin, allopurinol, lithium, methotrexate.

Always check eGFR before prescribing and at dose changes. Metformin: safe down to eGFR 30, dose reduce at 30-45, contraindicated <30. DOACs: apixaban safest in CKD (partly hepatic), rivaroxaban/dabigatran avoid if CrCl <25-30. Morphine's M6G metabolite accumulates causing respiratory depression. Use hydromorphone or fentanyl instead.

**Q:** What are the causes of CKD in Canada by prevalence?

**A:** 1. Diabetes (35-40%), 2. Hypertension/renovascular (20%), 3. Glomerulonephritis (10-15%), 4. Polycystic kidney disease (5-10%), 5. Obstructive uropathy, 6. Interstitial nephritis, 7. Unknown/other. Indigenous populations have disproportionately higher rates.

Diabetic kidney disease is the leading cause of ESKD in Canada. ADPKD is the most common hereditary cause — screen family members with renal US at age 18-40. Indigenous Canadians have 2-4x higher ESKD rates due to diabetes prevalence and barriers to care. Canadian Organ Replacement Register (CORR) tracks data.

**Q:** What is the approach to hypertension in CKD?

**A:** Target <130/80 (or <120 systolic per SPRINT in non-diabetic CKD). First-line: ACEi or ARB (especially if proteinuria). Add: CCB (amlodipine) or diuretic (thiazide-like if eGFR >30, loop if <30). Monitor K+ and Cr after ACEi/ARB initiation. Tolerate up to 30% Cr rise if stable.

ACEi/ARBs reduce proteinuria and slow progression independently of BP lowering. Initial Cr rise reflects reduced glomerular hyperfiltration — beneficial hemodynamic effect. Discontinue only if Cr rises >30% or K+ >5.5 despite optimization. Do not combine ACEi + ARB. SGLT2i provide additional BP lowering (~5 mmHg).

**Q:** What vaccinations are recommended in CKD/ESKD?

**A:** All standard vaccines PLUS: hepatitis B (high-dose 40 μg x 4 series, check anti-HBs titers), annual influenza, pneumococcal (PCV20), COVID-19 per NACI, shingles (Shingrix). Administer vaccines early in CKD — response diminishes as GFR declines.

CKD causes uremic immunodeficiency with impaired vaccine responses. HBV vaccination ideally before dialysis (better seroconversion). Anti-HBs <10 IU/L requires booster. Live vaccines generally avoided in transplant patients on immunosuppression. NACI recommends additional doses for immunocompromised.

**Q:** What skin finding is specific to chronic renal failure?

**A:** Pruritus, pigmentation ('half-and-half nails' — proximal white, distal brown), perforating dermatosis, calciphylaxis.

Half-and-half nails (Lindsay's nails) are seen in up to 50% of CKD patients. Calciphylaxis (calcific uremic arteriolopathy) causes painful ischemic skin necrosis and is a life-threatening complication of CKD.

**Q:** What did the CREDENCE trial demonstrate about canagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitor) in T2DM with CKD?

**A:** Canagliflozin 100 mg daily in T2DM patients with CKD significantly reduced the composite primary outcome (end-stage kidney disease, doubling of serum creatinine, or death from renal or CV causes) by 30% (HR 0.70). The trial was stopped early after median 2.6 years of follow-up.

CREDENCE (n=4,401) established the first renal outcome benefit of an SGLT2 inhibitor in T2DM with CKD, leading to Health Canada approval of canagliflozin for renal protection. SGLT2 inhibitors are now recommended as second-line agents in T2DM with CKD to reduce progression of kidney disease.

**Q:** What did the DAPA-CKD trial demonstrate about dapagliflozin in CKD (with or without T2DM)?

**A:** Dapagliflozin 10 mg/day in patients with CKD (eGFR 25-75 mL/min/1.73m², urinary ACR 200-5000) reduced the composite outcome (sustained ≥50% eGFR decline, end-stage kidney disease, or death from renal or CV causes) by 39% (9.2% vs. 14.5%, HR 0.61). Effects were similar in patients with and without T2DM.

DAPA-CKD (n=4,304) expanded the renal protection indication of SGLT2 inhibitors beyond T2DM — patients with CKD from any cause (including IgA nephropathy) benefited. This led to guideline recommendations for dapagliflozin in CKD regardless of diabetes status.

**Q:** What did the EMPA-KIDNEY trial demonstrate about empagliflozin in CKD patients?

**A:** Empagliflozin in at-risk CKD patients (n=6,609) reduced the incidence of kidney disease progression or cardiovascular death (13.1% vs. 16.9% placebo) with consistent results across diabetes status and eGFR ranges, and no significant difference in adverse events.

EMPA-KIDNEY (NEJM 2023) further confirmed the benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors across the spectrum of CKD regardless of diabetes. The consistent benefit across different eGFR ranges supports use even in lower eGFR ranges than previously studied.

## Electrolyte Disorders

**Q:** A 70-year-old on HCTZ has Na+ 122 mEq/L, clinically euvolemic. Urine Na+ 40 mEq/L, urine osmolality 520 mOsm/kg. Diagnosis and management?

**A:** SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion); restrict water (500-1000 mL/day), correct Na+ slowly (max 8-10 mEq/L per 24h to prevent osmotic demyelination)

SIADH diagnosis: hyponatremia, hypo-osmolality, urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg (concentrated urine despite hyponatremia), urine Na+ >20 mEq/L, euvolemia. Causes: CNS, pulmonary, malignancy, drugs (HCTZ, SSRIs). Vaptans (tolvaptan) for refractory cases.

**Q:** What is the danger of correcting hyponatremia too rapidly and what is the safe correction rate?

**A:** Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS/central pontine myelinolysis): correction >10 mEq/L/24h or >18 mEq/L/48h in chronic hyponatremia causes permanent neurological damage

Chronic hyponatremia (>48h) is most dangerous to correct rapidly because brain cells adapt by losing osmolytes. ODS presents with locked-in syndrome, dysarthria, quadriplegia days after correction. Target: 4-8 mEq/L in first 24h for chronic hyponatremia. Symptomatic hyponatremia: 1-2 mEq/L/hour for 1-2 hours with hypertonic saline then slow down.

**Q:** Classify hyponatremia by volume status with urine findings.

**A:** Hypovolemic (urine Na+ <20: extrarenal loss — GI, skin; urine Na+ >20: renal loss — diuretics, cerebral salt wasting); Euvolemic (SIADH, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency); Hypervolemic (urine Na+ <20: cirrhosis, HF, nephrotic; urine Na+ >20: CKD, AKI)

Volume status assessment is the first step in hyponatremia evaluation. Clinical exam + urine Na+/osmolality guide etiology. Pseudohyponatremia: high lipids or protein. Hyperosmolar hyponatremia: hyperglycemia, mannitol.

**Q:** A patient develops confusion, muscle weakness, and ECG changes (peaked T waves, wide QRS) with K+ 6.8 mEq/L. Immediate treatment?

**A:** IV calcium gluconate (1-2 g) immediately to stabilize cardiac membrane, then insulin 10 units IV + dextrose 50g, sodium bicarbonate if acidotic, then definitive removal (kayexalate, patiromer, or dialysis)

Calcium gluconate stabilizes cardiac membrane within minutes but does not lower K+. Insulin + dextrose shifts K+ into cells (K+ drops 0.5-1.5 mEq/L within 30 min). Beta-agonists (albuterol) also shift K+. Definitive removal requires 1-2 hours (kayexalate, patiromer) or dialysis.

**Q:** What are the ECG findings and clinical features of hypokalemia?

**A:** K+ <3.5: muscle weakness, fatigue, constipation; ECG: U waves, T wave flattening, QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmias (risk of TdP); severe (<2.5): paralysis, rhabdomyolysis

Hypokalemia causes: GI losses (vomiting, diarrhea), diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, renal tubular acidosis, hypomagnesemia. Treat underlying cause. Replace K+ cautiously: maximum IV rate 10-20 mEq/h through peripheral line. Correct Mg2+ simultaneously.

**Q:** What is the approach to hypercalcemia and what are the most common causes?

**A:** Causes by frequency: hyperparathyroidism (PTH-mediated, ambulatory) and malignancy (PTHrP or bone metastases, inpatient); others: sarcoidosis, vitamin D toxicity, milk-alkali syndrome

Symptoms (symptoms of hypercalcemia): bones (fractures), moans (constipation, peptic ulcer), groans (muscle weakness, polyuria/polydipsia), stones (nephrolithiasis), psychic overtones (depression, confusion). Severe hypercalcemia (>3.5 mmol/L or symptomatic): IV NS, bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid), dialysis.

**Q:** How is hypernatremia classified and managed?

**A:** Hypernatremia = Na+ >145 mEq/L; always hyperosomolar; causes: water deficit (diabetes insipidus, fever, diarrhea) or sodium gain (hypertonic saline); correct slowly (max 10 mEq/L/24h) with free water (D5W PO/IV or NG water)

Correction rate: max 10 mEq/L per 24h to prevent cerebral edema. Central DI: DDAVP (desmopressin) treatment. Nephrogenic DI: treat cause, thiazide diuretics reduce free water excretion paradoxically. Water deficit = 0.6 × weight × [(Na/140) - 1].

**Q:** A patient with chronic hyponatremia is given IV hypertonic saline and overcorrects to Na+ 136 mEq/L from 118 mEq/L in 18 hours. What should be done?

**A:** Re-lower sodium with DDAVP + D5W infusion (3% saline relowering protocol) to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome

Overcorrection is a medical emergency. DDAVP (desmopressin) 2 mcg IV plus D5W 6 mL/kg/h can re-lower sodium by 1-2 mEq/L/hour. Prevention is key: use DDAVP-clamp method in high-risk patients (Na+ <120, malnutrition, alcohol use, hypokalemia).

**Q:** What causes metabolic alkalosis and how is it classified?

**A:** Saline-responsive (urine Cl- <25 mEq/L): vomiting, nasogastric suction, diuretic use — treat with IV normal saline; Saline-resistant (urine Cl- >25 mEq/L): hyperaldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, severe hypokalemia

Metabolic alkalosis: HCO3- >26 mEq/L, pH >7.40. Maintenance: hypovolemia stimulates aldosterone (retains HCO3-) and hypokalemia drives H+ into cells. Saline-responsive metabolic alkalosis is most common (GI losses, diuretics).

**Q:** What is the anion gap and when is it elevated?

**A:** AG = Na - (Cl + HCO3), normal 8-12 mEq/L; elevated in: MUDPILES: Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol, Isoniazid/Iron, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates

Elevated AG metabolic acidosis requires urgent identification and treatment of cause. Delta ratio: if (elevated AG - 12) / (24 - HCO3) >2, suggests concurrent metabolic alkalosis; <1 suggests concurrent non-AG metabolic acidosis.

**Q:** What is the management of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) with electrolyte focus?

**A:** IV insulin infusion + isotonic saline, potassium replacement (IV K+ when K+ <5.5 and urine output adequate), sodium bicarbonate only for pH <6.9, monitor glucose hourly and electrolytes q2-4h

DKA causes total body K+ depletion despite initial serum K+ appearing normal/high (acidosis shifts K+ extracellularly). Potassium must be given before insulin if K+ <3.5 mEq/L. Phosphate replacement for severe hypophosphatemia (<0.32 mmol/L).

**Q:** A 45-year-old has serum phosphate 0.4 mmol/L, muscle weakness, and respiratory failure. What is the management?

**A:** Severe hypophosphatemia (<0.32 mmol/L): IV phosphate replacement (sodium phosphate or potassium phosphate); mild-moderate: oral phosphate supplements

Causes of hypophosphatemia: refeeding syndrome, DKA treatment, alcoholism, hyperparathyroidism, antacids. Severe hypophosphatemia causes: hemolytic anemia, rhabdomyolysis, cardiac dysfunction, respiratory muscle weakness. IV replacement rate: 0.16-0.32 mmol/kg over 4-6h.

**Q:** What are the causes and treatment of hypomagnesemia?

**A:** Causes: poor intake (alcoholism), GI losses (diarrhea, malabsorption), renal losses (diuretics, cisplatin, PPIs), diabetes; treat with IV magnesium sulfate for symptomatic or severe (<0.4 mmol/L)

Hypomagnesemia is common in hospitalized patients. It causes: hypokalemia (resistant to K+ replacement unless Mg2+ corrected), hypocalcemia, cardiac arrhythmias (TdP). PPIs can cause chronic magnesium wasting. IV MgSO4 2-4g for acute symptomatic hypomagnesemia.

**Q:** What is refeeding syndrome and how is it prevented?

**A:** Hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia occurring 1-4 days after refeeding malnourished patients; prevent by correcting electrolytes before feeding, start feeds slowly, supplement thiamine

Refeeding syndrome occurs due to rapid intracellular shift of phosphate, K+, and Mg2+ during anabolism after starvation. Risk groups: malnutrition, alcoholism, anorexia nervosa, prolonged fasting, post-surgery. Thiamine deficiency causes Wernicke encephalopathy if carbohydrates given without thiamine.

**Q:** How is diabetes insipidus (DI) diagnosed and differentiated from psychogenic polydipsia?

**A:** Water deprivation test: DI fails to concentrate urine (osmolality <300 mOsm/kg); central DI responds to DDAVP; nephrogenic DI does not respond to DDAVP; psychogenic polydipsia: concentrated urine after restriction

Central DI: ADH deficiency from pituitary/hypothalamic damage (trauma, tumors, surgery). Nephrogenic DI: ADH resistance (lithium toxicity, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, hereditary). Serum ADH/copeptin levels can aid diagnosis before water deprivation.

**Q:** How correct hyponatremia based on chronicity?

**A:** Acute/symptomatic: 3% NaCl 1-2mEq/h. Chronic: limit <=8-10mEq/24h to prevent ODS

ODS risk: Na<120, hypokalemia, malnutrition, liver disease. If overcorrected: D5W + desmopressin.

**Q:** What is emergency hyperkalemia treatment with ECG changes?

**A:** 1)IV calcium gluconate 2)Insulin 10U+D50W 3)Salbutamol nebs 4)Kayexalate/dialysis

ECG: peaked T -> prolonged PR -> wide QRS -> sine wave. Calcium stabilizes membrane, doesn't lower K.

**Q:** What is the sodium correction formula for hypernatremia and what is the safe rate of correction?

**A:** Free water deficit = TBW × [(Na/140) - 1], where TBW = 0.6 × weight (men) or 0.5 × weight (women). Correct Na by ≤10 mmol/L per 24h for chronic hypernatremia. Replace deficit with D5W or hypotonic saline over 48-72h.

Rapid correction of chronic hypernatremia risks cerebral edema. Acute hypernatremia (<48h, e.g., salt poisoning) can be corrected faster (1-2 mmol/L/h). Most common cause in elderly: inadequate water intake (cognitive impairment, immobility). Also: diabetes insipidus, osmotic diuresis. Monitor Na q4-6h during correction.

**Q:** What is the approach to hyponatremia and what correction rate prevents osmotic demyelination?

**A:** Acute symptomatic (<48h): 3% hypertonic saline 100-150 mL bolus, target 4-6 mmol/L rise in first 6h. Chronic: do NOT exceed 8 mmol/L per 24h (some guidelines say 10). High risk for osmotic demyelination: hypokalemia, malnutrition, alcoholism, liver disease.

Osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis) is devastating and irreversible — presents 2-6 days after overcorrection with dysarthria, quadriparesis, locked-in syndrome. If overcorrected: give DDAVP 2 μg IV q8h + D5W to re-lower sodium. Always assess volume status: hypovolemic, euvolemic (SIADH), hypervolemic.

**Q:** What is SIADH and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Diagnosis requires ALL: serum Na <135, serum osm <275, urine osm >100, urine Na >30, euvolemia, normal thyroid/adrenal function, no diuretics. Causes: CNS (stroke, SAH, meningitis), pulmonary (pneumonia, TB, small cell lung cancer), drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide).

SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion — must rule out hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and renal impairment first. Treatment: fluid restriction (1-1.5 L/day) first-line; salt tablets + loop diuretic; vaptans (tolvaptan) for refractory cases (monitor closely for overcorrection). Treat underlying cause. Demeclocycline rarely used (nephrotoxic).

**Q:** What are the ECG changes of hyperkalemia in order of severity?

**A:** Mild (5.5-6.5): peaked T waves (earliest), shortened QT. Moderate (6.5-7.5): prolonged PR, flattened P waves, widened QRS. Severe (>7.5): sine wave pattern, VF, asystole. Loss of P waves = atrial standstill.

ECG changes do not always correlate perfectly with K+ levels — some patients arrest at 6.5, others tolerate 8.0. Any ECG changes with hyperkalemia warrant immediate IV calcium. Pseudohyperkalemia: hemolyzed sample, prolonged tourniquet, extreme leukocytosis or thrombocytosis — always repeat if unexpected.

**Q:** What is the differential diagnosis and workup for hypokalemia?

**A:** Losses: GI (diarrhea, vomiting — actually renal loss from alkalosis), renal (diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, RTA, Mg depletion). Shift: insulin, beta-agonists, alkalosis. Workup: spot urine K/Cr ratio or 24h urine K (>30 mmol/day = renal loss). Check Mg. Check BP (elevated: think hyperaldosteronism).

Always check and replace magnesium — hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia (Mg needed for ROMK channel function). Vomiting causes hypokalemia via metabolic alkalosis-driven renal K wasting (not GI K loss). Hypokalemia + hypertension: screen for primary aldosteronism (aldosterone:renin ratio).

**Q:** How is hypokalemia corrected and what are the dangers?

**A:** Oral preferred if mild (3.0-3.5, asymptomatic): KCl 40-80 mEq/day. IV if severe (<3.0 or symptomatic/ECG changes): KCl max 10-20 mEq/h peripherally (40 mEq/h via central line with cardiac monitoring). Replace Mg concurrently. Each 10 mEq raises K+ by ~0.1.

IV KCl must be diluted and given slowly — rapid infusion causes cardiac arrest. Never give IV potassium as bolus push. K+ <2.5 is life-threatening (respiratory paralysis, rhabdomyolysis, cardiac arrest). Recheck K+ after each 40 mEq replacement. Total body deficit can be 200-400 mEq even with modest hypokalemia.

**Q:** What is the approach to metabolic acidosis using the anion gap?

**A:** AG = Na - (Cl + HCO3). Normal ~12. AGMA (AG >12): MUDPILES (Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol, Isoniazid/Iron, Lactic acid, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates). NAGMA: diarrhea, RTA, normal saline excess, acetazolamide, ureteral diversion.

Always calculate delta-delta ratio (ΔAG/ΔHCO3): >2 = concurrent metabolic alkalosis; <1 = concurrent NAGMA. Serum osmolal gap >10 suggests toxic alcohol ingestion. Lactic acidosis: Type A (tissue hypoxia — shock, sepsis) vs Type B (no hypoxia — metformin, liver failure, malignancy). Treat underlying cause.

**Q:** What are the types of renal tubular acidosis (RTA)?

**A:** Type 1 (distal): cannot secrete H+, urine pH >5.5, hypoK+, nephrolithiasis/nephrocalcinosis. Type 2 (proximal): cannot reabsorb HCO3, urine pH variable, hypoK+, Fanconi syndrome. Type 4 (hyperkalemic): hypoaldosteronism, urine pH <5.5, hyperK+.

Type 1 causes: autoimmune (Sjögren, SLE), amphotericin B, lithium, medullary sponge kidney. Type 2: multiple myeloma, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, heavy metals. Type 4: diabetic nephropathy, ACEi/ARBs, spironolactone, TMP-SMX, heparin. Type 3 is rare (combined 1+2). All are NAGMA.

**Q:** What are the causes and management of hypercalcemia?

**A:** Most common: primary hyperparathyroidism (outpatient) and malignancy (inpatient). Others: granulomatous disease, vitamin D toxicity, thyrotoxicosis, immobilization, thiazides, milk-alkali. Management: IV NS (200-300 mL/h), calcitonin (rapid but transient), zoledronic acid (definitive for malignancy), treat underlying cause.

PTH-dependent: primary hyperparathyroidism (elevated PTH, elevated Ca). PTH-independent (PTH suppressed): malignancy (PTHrP, bone metastases, 1,25-vit D from lymphoma). Severe hypercalcemia (>3.5 mmol/L): emergency — aggressive saline, calcitonin 4 IU/kg q12h, denosumab if bisphosphonate-refractory. Dialysis for life-threatening.

**Q:** What is the approach to hypocalcemia?

**A:** Symptoms: perioral tingling, Chvostek sign (facial twitch), Trousseau sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff), QTc prolongation, seizures, laryngospasm. Correct albumin first: corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 - albumin g/L). Acute symptomatic: IV calcium gluconate 1-2 amps in 50-100 mL D5W over 10 min. Chronic: oral calcium + vitamin D.

Most common cause: post-thyroidectomy (parathyroid damage). Also: CKD, vitamin D deficiency, hypomagnesemia (impairs PTH secretion), pancreatitis. Always check and correct Mg — hypomagnesemia causes PTH resistance. Phosphate level helps differentiate: high PO4 + low Ca = hypoparathyroid or CKD; low PO4 + low Ca = vitamin D deficiency.

**Q:** What are the causes and management of hypomagnesemia?

**A:** Causes: GI losses (diarrhea, malabsorption, PPI use), renal losses (diuretics, alcohol, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, calcineurin inhibitors). Management: mild: oral Mg oxide/gluconate. Severe (<0.5 mmol/L) or symptomatic: IV MgSO4 1-2g over 1h. Replace before correcting K+ or Ca.

Hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia and hypocalcemia. PPI-associated hypomagnesemia occurs after >1 year of use and resolves with PPI cessation. Torsades de pointes: IV MgSO4 2g bolus even if Mg is normal. Chronic alcohol use is the most common cause — depletes Mg via renal wasting and poor intake.

**Q:** What is the approach to hyperphosphatemia in CKD?

**A:** Dietary phosphate restriction (processed foods, dairy). Phosphate binders with meals: calcium carbonate/acetate (avoid if Ca >2.54 or PTH suppressed), sevelamer (no calcium load, may reduce vascular calcification), lanthanum carbonate. Dialysis removes phosphate. Target PO4: within normal range.

Hyperphosphatemia drives secondary hyperparathyroidism and vascular calcification (CKD-MBD). Calcium × phosphate product >4.4 mmol²/L² increases soft tissue calcification risk. Sevelamer may be preferred in patients with vascular calcification. Phosphate binders must be taken WITH meals to bind dietary phosphate.

**Q:** What is diabetes insipidus and how do you differentiate central from nephrogenic?

**A:** Both: polyuria (>3L/day), dilute urine (osm <300), polydipsia. Central DI (low ADH): responds to DDAVP (urine concentrates >50% increase). Nephrogenic DI (ADH resistance): no response to DDAVP. Water deprivation test + DDAVP trial differentiates.

Central DI causes: pituitary surgery/trauma, tumors (craniopharyngioma), infiltrative (sarcoid, histiocytosis), idiopathic. Nephrogenic DI: lithium (most common drug cause), hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, CKD. Treatment: central = DDAVP intranasal/oral; nephrogenic = thiazide diuretic (paradoxical) + amiloride + low-salt diet + NSAIDs.

**Q:** How is the serum osmolal gap calculated and when is it clinically useful?

**A:** Osmolal gap = measured Osm - calculated Osm. Calculated Osm = 2×Na + glucose (mmol/L) + urea (mmol/L). Normal gap <10 mOsm/kg. Gap >10: suspect unmeasured osmoles — toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol), propylene glycol.

Osmolal gap is most useful early after toxic alcohol ingestion (before metabolism). As toxic alcohol is metabolized to acids, osmolal gap falls while anion gap rises. Ethylene glycol: calcium oxalate crystals in urine, fluorescence under Wood lamp. Methanol: visual symptoms, optic disc edema. Treat both with fomepizole + dialysis.

**Q:** What is the cerebral salt wasting vs SIADH differentiation?

**A:** Both: hyponatremia, elevated urine Na, elevated urine Osm. Key difference: CSW = hypovolemic (negative fluid balance, orthostatic hypotension, elevated BUN, elevated hematocrit); SIADH = euvolemic. CSW: treat with IV NS + salt. SIADH: fluid restrict.

CSW occurs after neurosurgery, SAH, or brain injury — natriuretic peptide release causes renal Na wasting with volume depletion. Critical to differentiate because treatment is opposite: NS for CSW vs fluid restriction for SIADH. Volume status assessment is key. CSW is self-limited (resolves in weeks).

**Q:** How do you interpret an ABG for acid-base disorders?

**A:** 1) pH acidemia/alkalemia. 2) Primary disorder: CO2 for respiratory, HCO3 for metabolic. 3) Compensation: Winter's formula (metabolic acidosis: expected pCO2 = 1.5×HCO3+8±2). 4) Anion gap = Na-(Cl+HCO3), normal 8-12

MUDPILES for anion gap metabolic acidosis: Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol, INH/Iron, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates. Non-gap: RTA, diarrhea, normal saline excess.

**Q:** A patient on an ACE inhibitor and spironolactone is given trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for a UTI. What electrolyte complication can occur and why?

**A:** Hyperkalemia. Trimethoprim blocks sodium reabsorption in the distal tubule (similar to potassium-sparing diuretics), and combined with RAAS inhibitors/spironolactone, can cause dangerous potassium retention.

This is a pharmacodynamic interaction causing additive reduction in renal potassium excretion. TMP/SMX-induced hyperkalemia is particularly dangerous in elderly patients on RAAS inhibitors and has been associated with sudden cardiac death.

**Q:** What ECG changes are associated with hyperkalemia, in order of progression?

**A:** Early: tall peaked T-waves. Then: PR prolongation, QRS widening, loss of P waves. Finally: sinusoidal pattern, then PEA/VFib/Asystole.

Hyperkalemia progressively impairs cardiac conduction. The progression from peaked T-waves to sinusoidal pattern and cardiac arrest can be rapid. Treatment priorities: calcium gluconate/chloride for membrane stabilization; bicarbonate, insulin + dextrose, and salbutamol to shift K+ intracellularly; dialysis or Kayexalate for removal.

**Q:** What ECG findings are associated with hypokalemia?

**A:** P wave flattening, QRS widening and flattening, U waves appear, flattened T waves.

U waves (positive deflection after T wave, best seen in V2–V3) are the hallmark of hypokalemia. Hypokalemia increases the risk of ventricular arrhythmias, especially in the setting of digoxin use or cardiac disease. Treatment: oral or IV potassium replacement based on severity.

**Q:** What is the maximum safe rate of sodium correction in hypernatremia, and why?

**A:** No more than 12 mmol/L in 24 hours (0.5 mmol/L/h). Rapid correction risks cerebral edema, seizures, and death.

In chronic hypernatremia, brain cells have adapted by generating osmoles to maintain volume. Rapid lowering of serum sodium causes water to move into brain cells, causing cerebral edema. Begin with normal saline until hemodynamically stable, then switch to half-normal saline.

**Q:** What is the maximum safe rate of sodium correction in hyponatremia, and what syndrome does rapid correction risk?

**A:** Maximum 8 mmol/L in 24 hours (0.25 mmol/L/h). Rapid correction risks Osmotic Demyelinating Syndrome (ODS), previously called central pontine myelinolysis.

In chronic hyponatremia, the brain has adapted to low osmolarity. Rapid correction causes rapid water efflux from brain cells, damaging the myelin sheath of neurons—particularly in the pons. ODS presents with quadriplegia, dysphagia, dysarthria, and can be fatal.

**Q:** When is 3% hypertonic saline indicated for hyponatremia?

**A:** When hyponatremia causes seizures or coma (severe symptomatic hyponatremia).

Severe symptomatic hyponatremia with seizures or coma requires urgent partial correction with 3% hypertonic saline to relieve cerebral edema. The goal is to raise sodium by 1-2 mmol/L/h until symptoms resolve, not to fully normalize sodium. Total 24-hour rise should still not exceed 8 mmol/L.

**Q:** What are the ECG changes seen in hyperkalemia, in order of severity?

**A:** Peaked/narrow T waves → decreased P waves → prolonged PR interval → widening of QRS → sine wave → AV block → VFib/bradycardia.

Hyperkalemia progressively depolarizes the myocardium. Early changes (peaked T waves) reflect altered repolarization; later changes (widened QRS, sine wave) reflect conduction failure. VFib is the terminal event. This progression guides treatment urgency—calcium gluconate for membrane stabilization when ECG changes are present.

**Q:** What are the three strategies for treating hyperkalemia and give an example of each?

**A:** 1) Protect heart (membrane stabilization): calcium gluconate. 2) Shift K+ into cells: D50W + insulin, NaHCO3, salbutamol. 3) Remove K+ from body: IV fluids + furosemide, or dialysis.

Treatment of hyperkalemia uses a tiered approach: membrane stabilization is immediate (minutes) but temporary; intracellular shifting buys time (1-4 hours); actual removal of potassium from the body provides lasting correction. All three strategies may be needed simultaneously in severe cases.

**Q:** What ECG finding is most important in hypokalemia?

**A:** U waves (most important finding). Also: flattened/inverted T waves, prolonged QT, depressed ST segment.

U waves are deflections following the T wave, most prominent in leads V2-V3, and are the hallmark ECG finding of hypokalemia. They indicate delayed ventricular repolarization. Severe hypokalemia can cause ventricular dysrhythmias. Note that magnesium often needs to be repleted alongside potassium, as hypomagnesemia causes renal potassium wasting.

**Q:** What are the signs of hypocalcemia and what ECG change does it cause?

**A:** Laryngospasm, hyperreflexia, paresthesia, tetany, Chvostek's sign (tapping CN VII causes facial twitch), Trousseau's sign (BP cuff inflation causes carpal spasm). ECG: prolonged QT interval (can cause dysrhythmias).

Hypocalcemia increases neuromuscular excitability. Ionized calcium <0.7 mmol/L requires immediate IV calcium gluconate 1-2 g over 10-20 minutes, followed by slow infusion. Common causes include hypoalbuminemia (check ionized calcium), post-thyroid surgery, and hypoparathyroidism.

## Glomerulonephritis & Nephrotic Syndrome

**Q:** A 5-year-old boy develops periorbital edema, massive proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminemia, and hypercholesterolemia after an URI. What is the diagnosis and treatment?

**A:** Minimal change disease (MCD) presenting as nephrotic syndrome in a child; first-line treatment is oral prednisone (60 mg/m2/day or 2 mg/kg/day) — expect complete remission in 80-90%

MCD is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children. Responds dramatically to steroids. Lipoid nephrosis is another term. Diagnosis: clinical response to steroids; biopsy only if steroid-resistant or atypical features.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of nephrotic syndrome vs. nephritic syndrome?

**A:** Nephrotic: massive proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminemia (<25 g/L), edema, hyperlipidemia, lipiduria — no hematuria; Nephritic: hematuria (RBC casts), proteinuria (less massive), hypertension, oliguria, AKI

Nephrotic causes: MCD, FSGS, membranous nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy, amyloid. Nephritic causes: IgA nephropathy, post-streptococcal GN, anti-GBM disease, ANCA vasculitis, lupus nephritis.

**Q:** A 35-year-old has hypertension, microscopic hematuria, and mild proteinuria. Renal biopsy shows IgA deposits in mesangium. Diagnosis and prognosis?

**A:** IgA nephropathy (Berger disease) — most common primary GN worldwide; 20-30% progress to ESKD over 20-30 years; high-risk features: persistent hypertension, heavy proteinuria, elevated creatinine

CKD Canada/KDIGO KDIGO 2021 guidelines: ACEi/ARB for proteinuria >500 mg/day, blood pressure control. Targeted release budesonide (Nefecon) approved for high-risk IgA nephropathy. Sparsentan (dual endothelin/AT1 blocker) reduces proteinuria.

**Q:** A 60-year-old presents with hemoptysis and hematuria. ANCA (MPO-ANCA/p-ANCA) is positive. Renal biopsy shows pauci-immune crescentic GN. Diagnosis and treatment?

**A:** Microscopic polyangiitis or granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener) — ANCA vasculitis; treat with pulse IV methylprednisolone + cyclophosphamide (or rituximab) induction, then azathioprine maintenance

ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) requires immediate immunosuppression to preserve kidney function. PR3-ANCA (c-ANCA) = GPA; MPO-ANCA (p-ANCA) = MPA or eosinophilic GPA (eGPA). Plasma exchange added for dialysis-dependent or pulmonary hemorrhage.

**Q:** A 25-year-old woman with SLE has proteinuria 4g/day, RBC casts, and low complement. Renal biopsy shows diffuse proliferative GN (Class IV lupus nephritis). Treatment?

**A:** Class III/IV lupus nephritis: mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or cyclophosphamide induction + high-dose corticosteroids; MMF + belimumab or voclosporin as newer combination approach

ISN/RPS classification: Class III (focal) and IV (diffuse) LN require immunosuppressive induction. MMF has equivalent efficacy and better tolerability than cyclophosphamide for most patients. Maintenance: MMF + hydroxychloroquine + low-dose steroids.

**Q:** A 55-year-old man has nephrotic syndrome with proteinuria 8g/day and subepitheial deposits on electron microscopy. Serology negative. Diagnosis and treatment?

**A:** Membranous nephropathy; 30% spontaneous remission; treat high-risk patients (persistent heavy proteinuria, declining GFR) with rituximab (preferred) or cyclophosphamide-based regimen

Membranous nephropathy: most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults >50. 80% have PLA2R antibodies. Rituximab has replaced older regimens. Check for secondary causes (malignancy, infections, drugs, lupus) before assuming primary.

**Q:** What is post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN) and when does it occur?

**A:** PSGN: immune complex GN occurring 1-3 weeks after group A strep pharyngitis or 3-6 weeks after strep skin infection; presents with hematuria, edema, hypertension, low C3

PSGN is most common in children. Diagnosis: low serum C3, elevated ASO titer, throat/skin culture. Renal biopsy not usually required. Treatment: supportive (diuretics, antihypertensives); antibiotics to eradicate strep but do not prevent GN. Prognosis excellent in children.

**Q:** What distinguishes nephrotic from nephritic syndrome?

**A:** Nephrotic: proteinuria>3.5g/d, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia. Nephritic: hematuria/RBC casts, mild proteinuria, HTN

Nephrotic: minimal change(kids), membranous, FSGS. Nephritic: IgA, post-strep GN, lupus, ANCA.

**Q:** What are the key features distinguishing nephrotic from nephritic syndrome?

**A:** Nephrotic: proteinuria >3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia, lipiduria (oval fat bodies, maltese crosses). Nephritic: hematuria (RBC casts), mild-moderate proteinuria (<3.5g), hypertension, oliguria, elevated Cr, edema.

Nephrotic syndrome reflects podocyte damage (increased glomerular permeability to protein). Nephritic syndrome reflects glomerular inflammation with basement membrane disruption. Some GN presents with nephrotic-nephritic overlap (e.g., membranoproliferative GN, lupus nephritis). Renal biopsy is usually required for definitive diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the major causes of nephrotic syndrome in adults?

**A:** Membranous nephropathy (#1 primary in adults — anti-PLA2R antibodies), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS — #1 in Black patients), minimal change disease (MCD — #1 in children, responds to steroids), diabetic nephropathy (#1 secondary), amyloidosis, lupus

Workup: 24h urine protein or spot uACR, serum albumin, lipids, complement levels (C3/C4), ANA, anti-dsDNA, hepatitis B/C, HIV, PLA2R antibody, SPEP/UPEP (amyloid/myeloma). Complications: thromboembolism (renal vein thrombosis especially in membranous), infection, AKI, hyperlipidemia.

**Q:** What are the major causes of nephritic syndrome (glomerulonephritis)?

**A:** Post-infectious GN (post-streptococcal — low C3, normal C4), IgA nephropathy (most common GN worldwide — episodic hematuria with URI), lupus nephritis (low C3 and C4), anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture — linear IgG on IF), ANCA vasculitis (GPA, MPA — pauci-immune on IF)

Complement levels help narrow differential: Low C3 only: post-infectious, MPGN (C3 glomerulopathy). Low C3+C4: lupus, cryoglobulinemia. Normal complement: IgA, ANCA, anti-GBM. ANCA pattern: c-ANCA/PR3 = GPA (Wegener); p-ANCA/MPO = MPA. Rapidly progressive GN (RPGN) with crescents requires urgent biopsy and treatment.

**Q:** What is IgA nephropathy and how is it managed?

**A:** Most common primary GN worldwide. Presents with episodic gross hematuria 1-2 days after URI ('synpharyngitic'), persistent microscopic hematuria, mild proteinuria. Biopsy: mesangial IgA deposits. Management: ACEi/ARB (all), SGLT2i, +corticosteroids if proteinuria >1g/day despite 3 months of optimized RAAS blockade.

IgA nephropathy has variable prognosis: 20-40% progress to ESKD over 20 years. TESTING study showed targeted-release budesonide (Nefecon/Tarpeyo) reduces proteinuria. Fish oil (omega-3) is controversial. Tonsillectomy not recommended routinely (may be considered in Japan). Oxford MEST-C scoring on biopsy guides prognosis.

**Q:** What is rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN) and what are its causes?

**A:** RPGN: loss of >50% renal function over days-weeks with crescent formation on biopsy. Type I: anti-GBM (linear IF). Type II: immune complex (granular IF — lupus, post-infectious, IgA). Type III: pauci-immune (ANCA — GPA, MPA, EGPA). All require urgent biopsy and treatment.

RPGN is a nephrological emergency. Treatment: Type I (anti-GBM): cyclophosphamide + steroids + plasma exchange. Type II: treat underlying disease. Type III (ANCA): cyclophosphamide (or rituximab) + steroids ± plasma exchange. Delay reduces renal survival. Dialysis-dependent at presentation has poor renal prognosis, especially anti-GBM.

**Q:** What is membranous nephropathy and how is it managed?

**A:** Nephrotic syndrome with subepithelial immune deposits (spike and dome on EM). Primary (70%): anti-PLA2R antibodies. Secondary: hepatitis B, lupus, malignancy, NSAIDs. Rule of thirds: 1/3 spontaneous remission, 1/3 persistent proteinuria, 1/3 progressive CKD.

Anti-PLA2R positive + typical biopsy = primary membranous (no malignancy workup needed). PLA2R-negative: search for secondary causes. Treatment: observe 6 months if low risk. High risk (declining GFR, heavy proteinuria): rituximab (first-line per MENTOR trial), or cyclophosphamide + steroids (Ponticelli regimen), or calcineurin inhibitors.

**Q:** What is the typical presentation and management of minimal change disease?

**A:** Children (most common cause of nephrotic syndrome): sudden-onset nephrotic syndrome after viral illness. Normal complement. Biopsy: normal light microscopy, podocyte foot process effacement on EM. Treatment: prednisone 1mg/kg (max 80mg) x 4-6 weeks then taper. >90% respond to steroids.

MCD in adults: biopsy required (cannot assume diagnosis like in children). Relapses common (50% of children). Frequent relapsers: add steroid-sparing agent (cyclophosphamide, calcineurin inhibitor, rituximab, mycophenolate). AKI can occur (especially in elderly with MCD — possible from tubular injury). Always rule out NSAIDs and lymphoma as secondary causes.

**Q:** What is lupus nephritis classification and management?

**A:** ISN/RPS classification (I-VI based on biopsy): Class I-II (mesangial): mild, observe. Class III (focal proliferative) & IV (diffuse proliferative): aggressive — induction with mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide + steroids. Class V (membranous): if proteinuric, add immunosuppression. Class VI (sclerotic): supportive.

All SLE patients should be screened for nephritis (urinalysis, Cr, urine protein). Proliferative nephritis (III/IV) is the most serious — requires prompt biopsy and treatment. Maintenance: mycophenolate (preferred) or azathioprine for 3-5 years minimum. Belimumab and voclosporin are newer additions for lupus nephritis.

**Q:** What renal biopsy immunofluorescence patterns correspond to which diseases?

**A:** Linear IgG: anti-GBM disease. Granular IgG: immune complex GN (lupus, post-infectious, membranous). Mesangial IgA: IgA nephropathy, Henoch-Schönlein purpura. Pauci-immune (negative IF): ANCA vasculitis. Full house (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q): lupus nephritis.

IF pattern is one of the most important diagnostic features on renal biopsy. Combined with light microscopy (proliferation, crescents, sclerosis) and electron microscopy (deposit location: subepithelial, subendothelial, mesangial), a specific diagnosis can usually be made. C3 dominant without immunoglobulins suggests C3 glomerulopathy.

**Q:** What is Goodpasture syndrome (anti-GBM disease) and how is it managed?

**A:** Anti-GBM antibodies target type IV collagen α3 chain in glomerular AND alveolar basement membranes → RPGN + pulmonary hemorrhage (Goodpasture syndrome). Management: plasma exchange (daily x 14 days to remove antibodies) + cyclophosphamide + high-dose steroids.

Pulmonary hemorrhage triggered by smoking or inhaled hydrocarbons. Without lung involvement = anti-GBM disease; with lung = Goodpasture syndrome. Prognosis depends on Cr at presentation: if dialysis-dependent at diagnosis, renal recovery unlikely (< 10%). 10% have concurrent ANCA positivity (double-positive — slightly better prognosis). Monitor anti-GBM titers.

**Q:** A 45-year-old male presents with painless gross hematuria. Urinalysis shows no RBC casts or proteinuria. What is the most concerning diagnosis, and what is the workup?

**A:** Bladder cancer (or upper tract urothelial carcinoma/renal cell carcinoma). Non-glomerular painless gross hematuria in adults >40 requires cystoscopy and CT urogram to evaluate the entire urinary tract for malignancy. Risk factors include smoking, occupational exposure (aniline dyes), and male sex.

The absence of RBC casts and proteinuria suggests non-glomerular (urological) bleeding. Glomerular hematuria produces dysmorphic RBCs and RBC casts on urine microscopy. Painless hematuria in an older adult is malignancy until proven otherwise, even if intermittent.

**Q:** How do you differentiate glomerular from non-glomerular hematuria on urinalysis and urine microscopy?

**A:** Glomerular: dysmorphic RBCs (acanthocytes), RBC casts, concurrent proteinuria (>500 mg/day), brown/tea-colored urine. Non-glomerular: isomorphic (normal) RBCs, no RBC casts, minimal proteinuria, bright red or pink urine, may have blood clots (clots essentially exclude glomerular source).

RBC casts are pathognomonic for glomerular bleeding as they form in the renal tubules. Dysmorphic RBCs result from passage through the damaged glomerular basement membrane. The presence of blood clots effectively rules out a glomerular source since urokinase in the renal tubules lyses any clots formed glomerularly.

**Q:** A 10-year-old boy develops periorbital edema, tea-colored urine, and hypertension 2 weeks after a streptococcal pharyngitis. Urinalysis shows RBC casts and proteinuria. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN). This is a nephritic syndrome with hematuria, RBC casts, proteinuria, hypertension, and edema. Labs show low C3 complement, positive ASO titer or anti-DNase B. It is typically self-limited in children with supportive care (salt/fluid restriction, antihypertensives).

PSGN occurs 1-3 weeks after pharyngitis or 3-6 weeks after skin infection with nephritogenic strains of Group A Streptococcus. The low C3 with normal C4 indicates alternative complement pathway activation. Renal biopsy is usually not needed in children with a classic presentation. Prognosis is excellent in children but worse in adults.

**Q:** What is the systematic workup algorithm for hematuria in an adult?

**A:** 1) Confirm true hematuria (>3 RBC/HPF on microscopy; exclude myoglobinuria, hemoglobinuria, menstruation, beet ingestion). 2) Urine microscopy: dysmorphic RBCs/casts → glomerular workup (serology: ANA, ANCA, anti-GBM, complements, renal biopsy). Isomorphic RBCs → non-glomerular workup. 3) Non-glomerular: CT urogram + cystoscopy (especially if >40 years old) to evaluate for stones, malignancy, infection.

A positive urine dipstick for blood must be confirmed by microscopy, as myoglobin and hemoglobin also trigger the dipstick. The age of 40 is a critical threshold — all patients >40 with unexplained non-glomerular hematuria require malignancy workup with cystoscopy regardless of whether the hematuria is microscopic or gross.

**Q:** Why do drugs like phenytoin have drug-specific nomograms for dosing?

**A:** Because they follow non-linear (zero-order) kinetics, making dosing complex and requiring individualized guidance to avoid toxicity

Saturation of metabolic enzymes causes dose-concentration relationships to become unpredictable, hence the need for specialized dosing charts.

**Q:** Why can eyebrows and eyelashes not regrow after plucking, but scalp hair can?

**A:** Eyebrow, eyelash, and axillary hairs have a short anagen (growth) phase relative to their telogen (resting) phase, making regrowth impossible after removal; scalp hairs have longer anagen phases allowing regrowth

The ratio of anagen to telogen duration determines whether hair can regrow after removal. Hairs with short anagen:telogen ratios cannot regrow because their follicles enter permanent rest.

**Q:** What contraception requirements exist for women of childbearing potential taking acitretin?

Acitretin has an extremely long half-life. Even after discontinuation, the drug remains in the system and continues to pose teratogenic risk for up to 3 years, requiring continued contraception.

**Q:** Which hair types have short anagen phases making regrowth impossible if lost?

**A:** Eyebrow, eyelash, and axillary hairs

These hairs have a short growth phase relative to their resting phase, so if destroyed, the follicles cannot regenerate hair.

## Nephrology

**Q:** What is the normal GFR, and how does the CKD-EPI equation estimate it?

**A:** Normal GFR is ~125 mL/min/1.73 m² (180 L/day). CKD-EPI uses serum creatinine, age, biological sex, and race to estimate GFR.

GFR is the sum of filtration across all nephrons, with 99% of the filtrate reabsorbed. CKD-EPI is the most accurate equation currently available. Creatinine-based estimates are limited by factors affecting creatinine production such as muscle mass.

**Q:** What is the filtration fraction and how is it maintained in renal artery stenosis?

**A:** FF = GFR/RPF, normally 20%. In renal artery stenosis, angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole, increasing FF to maintain GFR despite decreased renal blood flow.

This is why ACEIs/ARBs can cause a significant drop in GFR in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis — they dilate the efferent arteriole, removing the compensatory mechanism that maintains GFR.

**Q:** What does a FENa <1% vs. >2% indicate in AKI?

**A:** FENa <1% suggests prerenal cause (kidneys appropriately retaining sodium). FENa >2% suggests intrinsic renal cause (e.g. ATN, kidneys unable to reabsorb sodium).

FENa = (urine Na × plasma Cr) / (plasma Na × urine Cr) × 100. In prerenal states, the kidneys respond to hypoperfusion by maximally reabsorbing sodium. In ATN, damaged tubules cannot reabsorb sodium effectively.

**Q:** What urine findings help differentiate prerenal AKI from ATN?

**A:** Prerenal: bland sediment, urine Na <20, urine osmolality >500, FENa <1%. ATN: pigmented granular (muddy brown) casts, urine Na ~40, urine osmolality <350, FENa >1%.

In prerenal AKI, the tubules are intact and appropriately concentrate urine and reabsorb sodium. In ATN, tubular damage leads to impaired concentrating ability and sodium wasting. Muddy brown casts are the hallmark of ATN.

**Q:** What is the maximum rate of sodium correction in chronic hyponatremia, and what complication occurs with overcorrection?

**A:** Maximum 8 mmol/L in 24 hours. Overcorrection causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis).

In chronic hyponatremia, brain cells have adapted by losing intracellular osmolytes. Rapid correction causes brain cell shrinkage and demyelination, presenting as dysarthria, dysphagia, paresis, and potentially quadriplegia with locked-in syndrome.

**Q:** What are the ECG changes in hyperkalemia in order of progression?

**A:** Peaked T waves → decreased P wave amplitude/loss of P waves → prolonged PR → widened QRS → sine wave pattern → ventricular fibrillation/asystole.

Hyperkalemia depolarizes the resting membrane potential, impairing cardiac conduction. Peaked T waves are the earliest finding. ECG changes do not correlate well with serum potassium levels, so treatment should not be delayed.

**Q:** What is the acute treatment of life-threatening hyperkalemia?

**A:** 1. Calcium gluconate (stabilize myocardium). 2. Insulin + D50W and/or nebulized salbutamol (shift K into cells). 3. Furosemide, cation exchangers, or dialysis (enhance K removal).

Calcium gluconate does not lower potassium but protects the heart within minutes. Insulin shifts K into cells within 15-30 min, lowering serum K by ~0.8 mmol/L. Definitive removal requires renal excretion or dialysis. Mnemonic: C BIG K DICED.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic for causes of increased anion gap metabolic acidosis?

**A:** MUDPILES CAT: Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Paraldehyde, Iron/Isopropyl/Ibuprofen/Indomethacin, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates, Cyanide/CO, Alcoholic ketoacidosis, Toluene.

The anion gap (AG = Na - Cl - HCO3) is normally 10-14. For each 10 g/L fall in albumin, lower the baseline AG by 3. An elevated AG indicates accumulation of unmeasured anions from these conditions.

**Q:** How do you use the urine anion gap to distinguish renal from non-renal causes of normal anion gap metabolic acidosis?

**A:** UAG = (urine Na + K) - urine Cl. If UAG is negative → adequate NH4+ excretion (non-renal cause, e.g. diarrhea). If UAG is positive → inadequate NH4+ in urine (renal cause, e.g. distal RTA).

In normal AG metabolic acidosis, the kidney should excrete NH4+ (with Cl-) to compensate. A negative UAG reflects high unmeasured cation (NH4+) in the urine, indicating intact renal acidification. A positive UAG means the kidneys are not appropriately excreting acid.

**Q:** What are the diagnostic criteria for SIADH?

**A:** 1. Urine inappropriately concentrated for serum osmolality. 2. Urine sodium >20-40 mmol/L (reflecting euvolemia). 3. FENa >1%.

SIADH causes euvolemic hyponatremia through inappropriate ADH-mediated water retention. Common causes include CNS disorders, pulmonary disease, medications (SSRIs, carbamazepine), and malignancy (especially small cell lung cancer). Treatment includes fluid restriction and addressing the underlying cause.

**Q:** How do you differentiate central from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus using the water deprivation test?

**A:** After water deprivation fails to concentrate urine (Uosm <300): give DDAVP. If Uosm rises >300 → central DI (responds to exogenous ADH). If Uosm does not rise → nephrogenic DI (kidneys resist ADH).

Central DI is caused by deficient ADH production (neurosurgery, trauma, granulomatous disease). Nephrogenic DI is caused by renal resistance to ADH (lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia). Central DI is treated with DDAVP; nephrogenic DI with thiazides and treating the underlying cause.

**Q:** What are the four features that define nephrotic syndrome?

**A:** 1. Heavy proteinuria (>3.5 g/1.73 m²/day). 2. Hypoalbuminemia. 3. Peripheral edema. 4. Hyperlipidemia.

Additional features include lipiduria (oval fat bodies, fatty casts) and hypercoagulable state from urinary losses of antithrombin III, protein C, and protein S. Common causes include minimal change disease (children), membranous nephropathy (adults), FSGS, and diabetic nephropathy.

**Q:** What is the triad of nephritic syndrome?

**A:** Hematuria (with RBC casts/dysmorphic RBCs), azotemia (elevated Cr and urea), and hypertension (from salt and water retention).

Nephritic syndrome reflects glomerular inflammation. Proteinuria is present but typically <3.5 g/day (sub-nephrotic). Mnemonic: PHAROH — Proteinuria, Hematuria, Azotemia, RBC casts, Oliguria, Hypertension.

**Q:** What is IgA nephropathy and how does it typically present?

**A:** Most common primary glomerular disease worldwide. Presents with gross hematuria after a viral upper respiratory tract infection, with mesangial IgA and C3 deposits on immunofluorescence.

IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease) is more common in White and Asian populations in the 2nd-3rd decades. Unlike post-streptococcal GN where hematuria occurs 2-3 weeks after infection, IgA nephropathy presents within days (synpharyngitic). Treatment includes RAAS blockers for proteinuria.

**Q:** What are the three types of crescentic (rapidly progressive) glomerulonephritis classified by immunofluorescence?

**A:** Type I: anti-GBM mediated (15%). Type II: immune complex mediated (24%). Type III: pauci-immune/ANCA-associated (60%).

RPGN is the most aggressive form of GN with crescent formation on biopsy. Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's) shows linear IgG deposits. Type II shows granular deposits. Type III (most common) shows little or no immune staining and is associated with ANCA vasculitis.

**Q:** What are the urinalysis findings that suggest active parenchymal kidney disease vs. bland sediment?

**A:** Active: RBC casts, WBC casts, muddy-brown granular casts, >2 RBCs/HPF, >4 WBCs/HPF. Bland: only hyaline casts, small quantities of crystals, <2 RBCs/HPF.

Active sediment suggests intrinsic kidney disease requiring further workup. RBC casts are pathognomonic of glomerulonephritis, WBC casts suggest pyelonephritis or interstitial nephritis, and muddy-brown casts indicate ATN.

**Q:** What drugs commonly cause acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)?

**A:** Antibiotics (beta-lactams, sulfonamides, rifampin, quinolones), NSAIDs, allopurinol, PPIs, furosemide, thiazides, phenytoin.

AIN presents with AKI typically 7-10 days after drug exposure. The classic triad of fever, rash, and eosinophilia is seen mainly with antibiotics. Urine shows sterile pyuria, WBC casts, and eosinophils. Treatment is stopping the offending drug; steroids may be used for allergic or immune causes.

**Q:** What are the stages of diabetic nephropathy?

**A:** Stage 1: hyperfiltration (GFR 120-150%). Stage 2: A2 albuminuria (30-300 mg/day), normal GFR. Stage 3: A3 albuminuria (>300 mg/day), declining GFR. Stage 4: sclerosed glomeruli, ESRD.

Microalbuminuria (A2) is the earliest sign of diabetic nephropathy and is a risk factor for both renal and cardiovascular progression. Once overt nephropathy (A3) is established, 50% reach ESRD within 7-10 years. First-line treatment is ACEI or ARB plus SGLT2 inhibitor.

**Q:** What are the priorities in managing diabetic nephropathy?

**A:** 1. Vascular protection (ACEI, BP control, glycemic control, SGLT2i, lipid control). 2. BP optimization (<130/80). 3. Renal protection with ACEI/ARB even without HTN. 4. Smoking cessation.

SGLT2 inhibitors (canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) provide renoprotection independent of glycemic effects. Finerenone (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist) adds renoprotection on top of RAAS blockade. Combination ACEI + ARB is NOT recommended.

**Q:** What are the KDIGO stages of CKD and what GFR defines each?

**A:** G1: ≥90 (normal/high). G2: 60-89 (mildly decreased). G3a: 45-59. G3b: 30-44. G4: 15-29 (severely decreased). G5: <15 (kidney failure).

CKD is classified by both GFR and albuminuria categories. Higher albuminuria and lower GFR both independently increase risk of progression and complications. 'D' is added to G5 for patients on dialysis.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic NEPHRON for managing CKD complications?

**A:** N: non-AG metabolic acidosis (NaHCO3). E: electrolytes and BP. P: phosphate (binders with meals). H: HTN. R: RBCs/anemia (EPO, iron). O: osteodystrophy (vitamin D, calcium). N: nephrotoxins (avoid NSAIDs).

CKD management requires addressing multiple complications simultaneously. Target SBP <120 mmHg, use ACEI/ARB and SGLT2i, treat anemia with ESA if Hb <100, and manage calcium/phosphate disorders to prevent renal osteodystrophy.

**Q:** What is autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) and what are its key extrarenal manifestations?

**A:** Caused by PKD1 (chr 16) or PKD2 (chr 4) mutations. Extrarenal: hepatic cysts (33%), mitral valve prolapse (25%), intracranial aneurysms (10%), diverticulosis, hernias.

ADPKD accounts for ~10% of ESRD cases. Kidneys are normal at birth but enlarge massively. Clinical manifestations are rare before age 20-25. Treatment includes BP control with ACEI, free water intake >2-3 L/day, and tolvaptan (V2R antagonist) to slow decline.

**Q:** What are the absolute indications for dialysis in CKD (mnemonic AEIOU)?

**A:** A: Acidosis (refractory). E: Electrolyte imbalance (refractory hyperkalemia). I: Intoxication. O: Overload (volume, refractory). U: Uremia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding).

Dialysis is generally started when GFR reaches ~10 mL/min, but initiation should be symptom-driven. Either hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis can be used. Renal transplantation provides the best outcomes and should be offered to all suitable patients.

**Q:** What are the differences between hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis?

**A:** HD: fast, uses semipermeable artificial membrane, hydrostatic ultrafiltration, requires vascular access. PD: slow, uses peritoneal membrane, osmotic ultrafiltration, home-based, preserves residual renal function better.

AV fistula is the preferred vascular access for HD (lower infection and thrombosis rates). PD advantages include independence and fewer dietary restrictions. PD complications include peritonitis and encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis; HD complications include disequilibrium syndrome and central venous stenosis.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of renal artery stenosis, and what clinical features suggest it?

**A:** Atherosclerotic plaques (90%), affecting proximal 1/3 of renal artery in males >55. Features: severe/refractory HTN, asymmetric renal size, epigastric bruits, spontaneous hypokalemia, rising Cr with ACEI/ARB, flash pulmonary edema.

Fibromuscular dysplasia (10%) affects the distal 2/3 in young females. Renovascular HTN is the most common cause of secondary HTN (1-2% of all hypertensive patients). Per the CORAL trial, stenting offers no benefit over medical therapy alone.

**Q:** What renal complications occur in multiple myeloma?

**A:** Light chain cast nephropathy ('myeloma kidney'), hypercalcemia, AL amyloidosis, monoclonal Ig deposition disease, hyperuricemia, and infection.

Light chains are filtered at the glomerulus and appear as Bence-Jones proteins in urine (not detected on dipstick, requires immunoelectrophoresis). Cast nephropathy can rapidly progress to kidney failure. Myeloma patients are poor candidates for kidney transplantation.

**Q:** What is scleroderma renal crisis and how is it treated?

**A:** Occurs in 10-15% of scleroderma patients (especially early disease, on prednisone >15 mg/d): malignant HTN, acute renal failure, microangiopathy, volume overload. Treatment: ACEI (mainstay, even if AKI present).

Scleroderma renal crisis is associated with 'onion skin' hypertrophy of small renal arteries and fibrinoid necrosis. 50% of scleroderma patients have some renal involvement. ACEI slows disease progression and is the cornerstone of treatment even during the crisis.

**Q:** What is the Cockcroft-Gault equation for estimating creatinine clearance?

**A:** CrCl = 1.2 × (140 - age) × weight (kg) / [Cr]. For women, multiply by 0.85.

This equation is mainly used to dose renally excreted medications (e.g. antibiotics) in CKD. It is less accurate than CKD-EPI for estimating GFR. Key limitations: requires steady-state creatinine, affected by muscle mass, and overestimates GFR due to tubular creatinine secretion.

**Q:** What urine crystal shapes are associated with specific conditions?

**A:** Envelope-shaped (calcium oxalate): ethylene glycol poisoning. Coffin-lid shaped (triple phosphate/struvite): UTI with urease-producing bacteria. Rosette/needle-shaped (uric acid): acidic urine, tumor lysis syndrome.

Crystal identification on urine microscopy can provide important diagnostic clues. Calcium oxalate crystals in the setting of metabolic acidosis should raise suspicion for ethylene glycol poisoning. Struvite crystals suggest infection with Klebsiella or Proteus.

**Q:** What are the complications of renal transplantation?

**A:** Leading cause of mortality: cardiovascular disease. Other: infections (bacterial, viral including CMV, fungal), new-onset DM (from prednisone/tacrolimus), graft rejection, BK virus nephropathy, malignancy (skin cancer, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma), calcineurin inhibitor nephropathy.

1-year allograft survival is ≥90%. Maintenance immunosuppression typically includes corticosteroids, a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or cyclosporine), and an anti-metabolite (mycophenolate). Leading causes of late graft loss are interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy and death with functioning graft.

**Q:** Why does hypomagnesemia make hypokalemia and hypocalcemia resistant to correction?

**A:** Magnesium is required for proper function of the Na-K-ATPase pump (K homeostasis) and for PTH secretion/action (Ca homeostasis). Without correcting Mg first, K and Ca cannot be effectively repleted.

Causes of hypomagnesemia include alcoholism, diuretics (loop and thiazide), diarrhea, PPIs, and early post-renal transplant. Clinical features include tremors, seizures, Chvostek/Trousseau signs, and cardiac arrhythmias including Torsades de Pointes.

**Q:** What is the treatment of methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning?

**A:** Fomepizole (alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor), vitamins (folate for methanol; pyridoxine and thiamine for ethylene glycol), and dialysis if metabolic acidosis or symptoms of toxicity develop.

Methanol is metabolized to formic acid (toxic to brain and retina, can cause blindness). Ethylene glycol is metabolized to oxalic acid (toxic to brain and kidneys; envelope-shaped calcium oxalate crystals in urine). Both cause increased anion gap metabolic acidosis with increased osmolar gap.

**Q:** What is the Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion?

**A:** Nodular glomerulosclerosis, the classic histological lesion of diabetic nephropathy, found in 15-20% of cases.

The more common histological pattern is diffuse glomerulosclerosis with uniform increase in mesangial matrix. Diabetic nephropathy is the most common cause of ESRD in North America. The classic lesion was first described by Kimmelstiel and Wilson in 1936.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of secondary hypertension related to renal disease?

**A:** Renovascular hypertension from renal artery stenosis (1-2% of all hypertensive patients). Mechanism: decreased renal perfusion → increased renin → angiotensin II (vasoconstriction) and aldosterone (Na/water retention).

Clues suggesting renovascular HTN include severe/refractory HTN, negative family history, asymmetric renal size, epigastric bruits, spontaneous hypokalemia, and rising creatinine with ACEI/ARB. Duplex Doppler U/S is a good screening test; renal arteriography is the gold standard.

**Q:** From which embryological structures does the definitive kidney (metanephros) develop?

**A:** The metanephros develops from: (1) the metanephric blastema (forms the nephrons: glomeruli, Bowman's capsule, PCT, loop of Henle, DCT) and (2) the ureteric bud of the mesonephric duct (forms the collecting system: collecting ducts, calyces, renal pelvis, ureters).

The pronephros degenerates by week 4, the mesonephros degenerates and its remnants form the Wolffian duct of the male reproductive system, and the metanephros develops in week 5 as the definitive kidney. Understanding this embryology is testable on MCCQE1 in the context of renal developmental anomalies.

**Q:** What is the sequence of kidney embryological development in humans?

**A:** Pronephros (end of week 3, degenerates by end of week 4) → Mesonephros (week 4, degenerates; remnants form Wolffian/mesonephric duct of male reproductive system) → Metanephros (week 5, becomes the definitive kidney). All originate from the urogenital ridge of the intermediate mesoderm.

The kidneys ascend from the pelvis into the retroperitoneum during development, gaining their blood supply from the abdominal aorta. Failure of ascent results in a pelvic kidney.

**Q:** What are the five major kidney functions?

**A:** 1. Waste excretion (glomerular filtration, tubular secretion, tubular catabolism of drugs/hormones)
2. Electrolyte balance and osmoregulation (NaCl/water reabsorption, K⁺/H⁺ secretion, HCO₃⁻ synthesis)
3. Hormonal synthesis (erythropoietin, vitamin D activation, renin production)
4. Blood pressure regulation (Na⁺ excretion alters ECF volume; renin alters vascular resistance)
5. Glucose homeostasis (gluconeogenesis; clearance of circulating insulin)

These functions underlie many clinical manifestations of chronic kidney disease, including anemia (erythropoietin deficiency), renal osteodystrophy (vitamin D deficiency), hypertension (RAAS activation), and metabolic acidosis (impaired H⁺ secretion).

**Q:** What are the three cell types that form the glomerular filtration barrier?

**A:** 1. Fenestrated capillary endothelial cells (with glycocalyx) — provide size and charge selectivity
2. Glomerular basement membrane (GBM) — collagen IV and laminin
3. Visceral epithelial cells (podocytes) — interdigitated foot processes with slit diaphragms

The barrier restricts molecules >5000 daltons and negatively charged molecules (e.g., albumin).

Disruption of any component of this barrier leads to proteinuria. For example, minimal change disease affects podocyte foot processes, membranous nephropathy affects the GBM, and anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture) attacks the GBM directly.

**Q:** What is the role of juxtaglomerular cells and what stimulates renin release?

**A:** Juxtaglomerular cells are modified smooth muscle cells in the afferent arteriole adjacent to the macula densa. They produce, store, and secrete renin.

Renin is released in response to:
1. Low afferent arteriolar blood pressure (decreased stretch)
2. Sympathetic nervous system activation (via β-adrenergic receptors)
3. Decreased Cl⁻/NaCl delivery to the macula densa (indicator of decreased RPF)

Renin cleaves angiotensinogen → angiotensin I, which is converted by ACE (in the lungs) → angiotensin II. This activates the RAAS pathway leading to vasoconstriction, aldosterone release, sodium retention, and ADH release — all aimed at restoring perfusion pressure.

**Q:** What is the filtration fraction (FF) and what is its normal value? How does angiotensin II affect FF?

**A:** FF = GFR / RPF; normal value is approximately 0.2 (20%).

Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, which:
- Increases hydrostatic pressure in glomerular capillaries
- Increases FF
- Thereby maintains GFR even when RPF is reduced (e.g., in volume depletion)

This is why ACE inhibitors (which reduce angiotensin II) can cause acute kidney injury in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis — they dilate the efferent arteriole, reduce FF and GFR, and the kidney can no longer maintain filtration pressure. This is a classic MCCQE1 clinical scenario.

**Q:** What are the three mechanisms of renal autoregulation that maintain constant GFR over mean arterial pressures of 70–180 mmHg?

**A:** 1. Myogenic mechanism: low BP directly causes afferent arteriole smooth muscle to dilate (and high BP causes constriction), preserving renal blood flow
2. Release of vasoactive factors: low GFR → decreased perfusion pressure → prostaglandin release → afferent arteriolar dilation → increased GFR
3. Tubuloglomerular feedback: reduced NaCl delivery to macula densa → decreased afferent arteriolar tone → increased GFR (and vice versa)

NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis and impair mechanism #2, which is why NSAIDs can precipitate AKI in patients with low effective circulating volume (e.g., heart failure, cirrhosis, dehydration). This is an important clinical pearl for MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the gold standard for measuring GFR and what is used clinically?

**A:** Gold standard: inulin clearance or iothalamate radiotracer (freely filtered, not secreted or reabsorbed) — rarely used clinically.

Clinical estimation:
1. CKD-EPI equation (most accurate; uses serum Cr, age, sex, race)
2. 24-hour urine creatinine clearance: CrCl = (urine [Cr] × 24h urine volume) / plasma [Cr]
3. Cockcroft-Gault equation: CrCl = 1.2 × (140 – age) × weight (kg) / [Cr] (×0.85 for women) — primarily used for drug dosing in CKD
4. New CKD-EPI equation with Cr + cystatin C (excludes race variable; more accurate)

Normal average GFR is 180 L/day or 125 mL/min/1.73 m². GFR peaks in early adulthood and declines after age 40. The CKD-EPI equation is the preferred method in Canadian clinical practice for staging CKD.

**Q:** What are the five major limitations of using serum creatinine to estimate GFR?

**A:** 1. Must be in steady state — serum Cr lags behind acute falls in GFR by days
2. GFR must fall substantially before plasma [Cr] rises above normal (compensatory hyperfiltration by remaining nephrons)
3. Cr production varies with muscle mass (falsely normal Cr in elderly, female, low weight)
4. Tubular secretion of Cr increases as GFR decreases → overestimates GFR; drugs (cimetidine, trimethoprim) inhibit tubular Cr secretion raising Cr without falling GFR
5. Measurement errors: high bilirubin → falsely low Cr; acetoacetate and cefoxitin → falsely high Cr

A classic MCCQE1 example: an 80-year-old woman with plasma [Cr] of 100 µmol/L may have a GFR of only 30.6 mL/min, while a 20-year-old 100 kg man with the same Cr may have a GFR of 144 mL/min. Always consider muscle mass and age when interpreting creatinine.

**Q:** What is cystatin C and when is it preferred over creatinine for GFR estimation?

**A:** Cystatin C is a newly discovered biomarker for GFR estimation that is NOT affected by muscle mass. It can be used with age and sex (excluding race variable) to estimate GFR more accurately than CKD-EPI with race omitted.

Preferred in:
- Patients with abnormal muscle mass (elderly, amputees, bodybuilders)
- Situations where creatinine-based equations are unreliable

The combined Cr + cystatin C CKD-EPI equation is now considered more accurate than creatinine-alone equations and is being used more widely. Its clinical use remains limited pending greater availability and testing standardization.

**Q:** What is the normal urea-to-creatinine ratio in serum (SI units) and what causes a disproportionate rise in urea?

**A:** Normal ratio: urea:creatinine = 1:12 (using mmol/L for urea and µmol/L for creatinine).

Disproportionately HIGH urea (high ratio) occurs with:
- Increased protein catabolism: GI hemorrhage, sepsis, trauma, corticosteroids, cytotoxic agents
- High protein diet
- ECF volume depletion (50%+ urea reabsorption increases)

Disproportionately LOW urea:
- Low protein diet
- Liver disease (decreased urea synthesis)

In prerenal AKI due to volume depletion, the urea:creatinine ratio rises disproportionately because urea (unlike creatinine) is significantly reabsorbed in the tubule when flow is slow — a useful clinical distinction from intrinsic renal disease.

**Q:** What is 'isosthenuria' and in what condition is it characteristically seen?

**A:** Isosthenuria is a urine specific gravity of approximately 1.010, reflecting a urine osmolality equal to that of plasma (~300 mOsm/kg).

It is characteristically seen in chronic kidney disease (CKD), where impairment of both urinary concentration AND dilution results in urine that is fixed near plasma osmolality, regardless of fluid intake.

Normal specific gravity ranges from 1.001–1.030. Values <1.010 reflect dilute urine; values >1.020 reflect concentrated urine. In CKD, the remaining nephrons lose the ability to modulate urine concentration, resulting in isosthenuria — this is a marker of tubular dysfunction.

**Q:** What are the four causes of glycosuria (glucose in urine)?

**A:** 1. Hyperglycemia >9–11.1 mmol/L (filtration exceeds tubular reabsorption capacity — most common, e.g., diabetes mellitus)
2. Increased GFR (e.g., pregnancy — PCT unable to reabsorb filtered glucose/amino acids)
3. Proximal tubule dysfunction (e.g., Fanconi syndrome — generalized PCT defect)
4. SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) — lower renal glucose threshold intentionally

Glucose is freely filtered and normally completely reabsorbed in the proximal tubule via SGLT transporters. Glycosuria without hyperglycemia (e.g., Fanconi syndrome, SGLT2 inhibitor use) is important to recognize on MCCQE1.

**Q:** What proteins does the urine dipstick detect, and what is the gold standard for measuring proteinuria?

**A:** Dipstick only detects albumin — it MISSES:
- Bence-Jones proteins (monoclonal light chains in myeloma)
- Immunoglobulins
- Tamm-Horsfall protein

Gold standard: 24-hour timed urine collection for total protein (always order 24h urine creatinine to confirm collection completeness).

Microalbuminuria (ACR 2.0–20 mg/mmol morning) is NOT detected by standard dipstick. To estimate 24h albumin excretion from ACR: multiply ACR × 10 (women) or × 15 (men).

In multiple myeloma, urine dipstick may be negative despite significant proteinuria from light chains (Bence-Jones proteins). Always order urine protein electrophoresis or urine immunofixation when myeloma is suspected. This is a classic MCCQE1 clinical trap.

**Q:** What is the significance of leukocyte esterase and nitrites on urine dipstick? What bacteria produce nitrites?

**A:** Leukocyte esterase: enzyme found in WBCs; positive = WBCs in urine, indicating infection (UTI) or inflammation (AIN, prostatitis, ureteritis, pyelonephritis).

Nitrites: endogenous nitrates converted to nitrites by bacteria. High specificity but LOW sensitivity for UTI.
- Nitrite-POSITIVE bacteria: mostly Gram-negative (Enterobacteriaceae e.g., E. coli, Klebsiella)
- Nitrite-NEGATIVE bacteria: Enterococci, Staphylococci

A negative nitrite does NOT rule out UTI, since common organisms like Enterococcus and Staphylococcus saprophyticus (common in young women) do not produce nitrites. Always interpret dipstick findings with clinical context.

**Q:** What causes persistent sterile pyuria (>4 WBCs/HPF with negative culture)?

**A:** Causes of sterile pyuria:
- Chronic urethritis
- Prostatitis
- Interstitial nephritis (AIN)
- Urolithiasis/calculi
- Allergic cystitis
- Interstitial cystitis
- Papillary necrosis
- Renal tuberculosis
- Viral infections
- N. gonorrhoeae or C. trachomatis infection (STIs — require special cultures)

Sterile pyuria is a classic finding in renal tuberculosis on MCCQE1. TB affects the kidney hematogenously; organisms are not detected on standard urine cultures and require specific mycobacterial cultures. Always ask about TB risk factors when sterile pyuria is found.

**Q:** How do you interpret urine casts on microscopy? Match each cast type to its clinical significance.

**A:** - Hyaline casts: physiologic (concentrated urine, fever, exercise)
- Granular casts: AKD (general), ATN (less specific)
- RBC casts: glomerular bleeding (proliferative GN, vasculitis) — pathognomonic
- WBC casts: pyelonephritis (infection); interstitial nephritis (inflammation)
- Pigmented granular casts (muddy brown/heme): ATN; acute proliferative GN
- Fatty/lipid casts: nephrotic syndrome (proteinuria >3.5 g/day)

Casts form from intratubular precipitation of uromodulin (Tamm-Horsfall protein) with trapped cells; they originate ONLY from the nephron.

RBC casts are pathognomonic for glomerulonephritis — their presence distinguishes glomerular hematuria from lower urinary tract bleeding. Muddy brown granular casts are the hallmark of ATN. Fatty casts with oval fat bodies (maltese cross pattern) indicate nephrotic-range proteinuria.

**Q:** What is the difference between dysmorphic and isomorphic RBCs on urine microscopy?

**A:** - Dysmorphic RBCs (acanthocytes) ± RBC casts → glomerular origin bleeding (e.g., proliferative GN, IgA nephropathy, vasculitis)
- Isomorphic RBCs, no casts → extraglomerular (non-glomerular) bleeding (e.g., bladder cancer, kidney stones, UTI)

Hematuria is defined as ≥3 RBCs per HPF.

Dysmorphic RBCs are distorted as they pass through the damaged glomerular filtration barrier and tubules. This distinction is critical: glomerular hematuria warrants workup for GN (complement, ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, serology), while non-glomerular hematuria in adults over 35–40 warrants cystoscopy to rule out bladder malignancy.

**Q:** What do the following urine crystal shapes indicate: (1) envelope-shaped, (2) coffin-lid shaped, (3) rosette or needle-shaped?

**A:** 1. Envelope-shaped crystals → calcium oxalate; consider hyperoxaluria, ethylene glycol poisoning, nephrolithiasis
2. Coffin-lid shaped crystals → triple phosphate (struvite); consider UTI with urease-producing organisms (e.g., Klebsiella, Proteus)
3. Rosette or needle-shaped crystals → uric acid; consider acidic urine, hyperuricosuria, tumour lysis syndrome

Other crystals: calcium phosphate (alkaline urine), sulfur crystals (sulfa antibiotics), acyclovir crystals (crystalluria from acyclovir). Ethylene glycol poisoning producing calcium oxalate crystals with an osmolar gap is a classic toxicology-nephrology crossover question on MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) and how is it used to differentiate causes of AKI?

**A:** FENa = (urine [Na⁺] × plasma [Cr]) / (plasma [Na⁺] × urine [Cr]) × 100

Interpretation:
- FENa <1%: prerenal AKI (kidneys avidly retaining sodium — appropriate response to decreased perfusion)
- FENa >2%: intrinsic renal AKI (e.g., ATN — tubules unable to reabsorb sodium properly)

Caveat: FENa may be low (<1%) in contrast nephropathy, myoglobinuria, and early obstruction despite intrinsic damage.

FENa is most useful when diuretics have NOT been given (diuretics will artificially raise urine Na⁺ and falsely elevate FENa). In patients on diuretics, use fractional excretion of urea (FEUrea <35% = prerenal) instead.

**Q:** What are the two key principles of sodium homeostasis and how do hypo- and hypernatremia differ from disorders of Na⁺ balance?

**A:** Key principles:
1. Hypo- and hypernatremia are disorders of WATER balance (not sodium balance)
   - Hyponatremia = too much water relative to Na⁺ in ECF
   - Hypernatremia = too little water relative to Na⁺ in ECF

2. ECF volume is determined by Na⁺ CONTENT (not concentration)
   - Na⁺ deficiency → ECF volume contraction
   - Na⁺ excess → ECF volume expansion

Solutes that cannot cross the plasma membrane (Na⁺, K⁺, glucose) contribute to effective osmolality and drive transcellular water shifts.

This fundamental distinction is critical: treating hyponatremia requires addressing the water excess, not sodium deficiency. Symptoms of hypo/hypernatremia are primarily neurological because brain cells swell (hyponatremia) or shrink (hypernatremia) in response to osmotic changes.

**Q:** Define hyponatremia and classify it by volume status.

**A:** Hyponatremia: serum [Na⁺] <135 mmol/L

Classification by volume status:
1. Hypovolemic hyponatremia: water loss proportionally less than sodium loss (e.g., vomiting, diarrhea, diuretics, adrenal insufficiency)
2. Hypervolemic hyponatremia: water gain proportionally greater than sodium gain (e.g., CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome)
3. Euvolemic hyponatremia: water gain with neutral sodium balance (e.g., SIADH, hypothyroidism, psychogenic polydipsia)

Can be associated with hypo-osmolality (most common), iso-osmolality, or hyperosmolality.

Distinguishing volume status guides treatment: hypovolemic hyponatremia requires volume repletion with isotonic saline; hypervolemic requires fluid restriction and diuresis; euvolemic (SIADH) requires free water restriction ± vasopressin antagonists. Osmolality assessment is the next diagnostic step.

**Q:** What is the maximum safe rate of sodium correction in chronic hyponatremia, and why?

**A:** Maximum correction rate: ≤8 mmol/L per 24 hours in chronic hyponatremia (>48 hours duration).

Rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)/central pontine myelinolysis because:
- Brain cells adapted to low Na⁺ by losing intracellular electrolytes (hours) and organic osmolytes (days)
- Rapid restoration of tonicity causes rapid shrinkage of brain cells that have adapted
- Results in demyelination of pontine and extrapontine neurons

Exception: acute hyponatremia (<48 h) — can correct up to 12 mmol/L/24h; if symptomatic, rapidly correct by 4–6 mmol/L in first hour using 3% NaCl boluses (100–150 cc).

Osmotic demyelination syndrome causes dysarthria, dysphagia, paresis — symptoms delayed 2–6 days. Risk factors: serum [Na⁺] <120 mmol/L, duration >48h, hypokalemia, malnutrition, liver disease, alcoholism. High urine output of dilute urine is the FIRST sign of dangerously rapid autocorrection.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) in hyponatremia?

**A:** Risk factors for ODS:
1. Low serum [Na⁺] ≤120 mmol/L at presentation (especially <106 mmol/L)
2. Duration of hyponatremia >2 days (chronic)
3. Associated hypokalemia
4. Malnutrition
5. Liver disease
6. Alcoholism
7. Overly rapid correction of [Na⁺]: rise >8 mmol/L/24h

Inadvertent rapid correction often occurs when the stimulus for ADH is abruptly removed — e.g., stopping thiazides, replacing corticosteroids in Addison disease, stopping SSRIs, or giving an antiemetic (resolution of nausea → cessation of SIADH → free water diuresis). Monitoring urine output and osmolality is critical.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of acute versus chronic hyponatremia?

**A:** Acute hyponatremia (<48 h):
- More symptomatic (cerebral edema, raised ICP)
- Symptoms: headache, nausea, malaise, lethargy, seizures, decreased LOC, brain herniation, death
- Less cellular adaptation has occurred
- Can correct more rapidly (up to 12 mmol/L/24h)

Chronic hyponatremia (>48 h):
- Less symptomatic (brain adapts by losing intracellular electrolytes and organic osmolytes)
- Risk of osmotic demyelination if corrected too rapidly
- Correct ≤8 mmol/L/24h

The brain's adaptation to chronic hyponatremia is protective against edema but creates the risk of ODS upon rapid correction. Postoperative hyponatremia and marathon runner hyponatremia are typically acute and require rapid correction if symptomatic.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for symptomatic acute hyponatremia?

**A:** If symptomatic (seizures, decreased LOC):
1. Correct rapidly with 3% NaCl boluses: 100–150 cc (or 2 cc/kg)
2. Aim for rapid increase of 4–6 mmol/L within the first hour → should resolve symptoms
3. Continue 3% NaCl at ~1 mmol/L/h to increase serum [Na⁺] to ~130 mmol/L
4. Limit rise to 12 mmol/L/24h if duration >24h

Key concentration facts:
- 0.45% NaCl: [Na⁺] = 77 mmol/L
- 0.9% NaCl: [Na⁺] = 154 mmol/L
- 3% NaCl: [Na⁺] = 513 mmol/L
- D5W: [Na⁺] = 0 mmol/L

For chronic hyponatremia with severe symptoms (seizures, decreased LOC), use the same acute strategy but limit total rise to 8 mmol/L in 24h. General measures for all hyponatremia: restrict free water, treat underlying cause, monitor serum Na⁺, urine volume, and urine osmolality frequently.

**Q:** What are the clinical signs used to assess ECF volume status (hypovolemia vs hypervolemia)?

**A:** Hypovolemia signs:
- Low BP with orthostatic drop
- Tachycardia
- Decreased skin turgor
- Absent dependent edema
- Decreased urine output
- Decreased body weight
- Increased hematocrit and serum protein
- IVC diameter <2.1 cm; IVC collapse on inspiration >50%

Hypervolemia signs:
- Normal to increased BP; S3 heart sound
- Inspiratory crackles on lung auscultation
- Edema present
- Increased body weight
- Decreased hematocrit and serum protein
- IVC diameter >2.1 cm; IVC collapse <50%
- B-lines on lung ultrasound

IVC ultrasound during respiration assesses central venous pressure/right atrial pressure and is increasingly used at the bedside. Urine sodium in hypovolemia can be increased (renal losses) or decreased (extrarenal losses), making it less reliable than clinical assessment alone.

**Q:** Which tubular segment of the nephron reabsorbs the most sodium and what percentage of filtered sodium does it reclaim?

**A:** The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) reabsorbs approximately 80% of filtered sodium (also reabsorbs 90% of HCO₃⁻, 90% of amino acids, and the majority of glucose, phosphate, and potassium).

Additional reabsorption:
- Loop of Henle (ascending thick limb): ~10% of filtered Na⁺ via Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransporter (target of loop diuretics)
- Distal tubule: ~7% via NaCl cotransporter (target of thiazide diuretics)
- Collecting duct: ~3% via ENaC (target of aldosterone and amiloride/spironolactone)

Understanding which diuretic targets which tubular segment is essential for MCCQE1. Loop diuretics (furosemide) are most potent because they block the highest-capacity Na⁺ transporter. Osmotic diuretics act at the proximal tubule and descending limb.

**Q:** What are oval fat bodies in urine microscopy and what is their clinical significance?

**A:** Oval fat bodies are tubular epithelial cells or macrophages laden with lipid droplets. They are seen in heavy proteinuria (proteinuria >3.5 g/day), characteristic of nephrotic syndrome.

They appear as a 'Maltese cross' pattern under polarized light. They are found alongside fatty/lipid casts.

Heavy proteinuria causes tubular cells to absorb filtered lipoproteins, producing oval fat bodies. Their presence, along with fatty casts, supports a diagnosis of nephrotic syndrome. Causes include minimal change disease, membranous nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), and diabetic nephropathy.

**Q:** What urine biochemistry findings support a diagnosis of prerenal AKI versus intrinsic renal AKI?

**A:** Prerenal AKI (avid sodium retention):
- Urine [Na⁺] <20 mmol/L
- Urine [Cl⁻] <20 mmol/L
- FENa <1%
- Urine osmolality >500 mOsm/kg (concentrated urine — ADH acting)
- Urine:plasma creatinine ratio >40

Intrinsic renal AKI (tubular dysfunction — unable to concentrate or retain Na):
- Urine [Na⁺] >20–40 mmol/L
- FENa >2%
- Urine osmolality <350 mOsm/kg (isosthenuric)

In patients on diuretics: use FE urea (<35% = prerenal) instead of FENa.

In hypokalemia, the expected renal response is to retain K⁺ (urine K⁺ excretion <20 mmol/day). If urine K⁺ >20 mmol/day in hypokalemia, this suggests renal K⁺ wasting (e.g., hyperaldosteronism, diuretics, renal tubular acidosis).

**Q:** What urine osmolality suggests water diuresis (DI or psychogenic polydipsia) in the workup of polyuria?

**A:** Urine osmolality <100 mOsm/kg

Urine osmolality <100 mOsm/kg indicates dilute urine consistent with water diuresis, pointing to diabetes insipidus or psychogenic polydipsia. Values 100-300 suggest mixed (partial DI or CKD); >300 suggests osmotic diuresis.

**Q:** What urine volume defines significant polyuria in adults on 24-hour urine collection?

**A:** >3 L in adults

A 24-hour urine collection >3 L in adults defines clinically significant polyuria requiring further investigation. This cutoff initiates a systematic workup including urine osmolality and water deprivation testing.

**Q:** What urine osmolality range suggests osmotic diuresis (e.g., hyperglycemia, excess solute intake) in the workup of polyuria?

**A:** Urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg

Urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg suggests an osmotic diuresis caused by hyperglycemia, azotemia, or excess solute intake. This is distinguished from water diuresis (<100 mOsm/kg) and mixed causes (100-300 mOsm/kg).

**Q:** What are the KDIGO stages of AKI based on serum creatinine?

**A:** Stage 1: 1.5-1.9x baseline or ≥0.3 mg/dL increase; Stage 2: 2.0-2.9x baseline; Stage 3: ≥3x baseline, Cr ≥4.0 mg/dL, initiation of RRT, or eGFR <35 mL/min/1.73m² in patients <18 yr

KDIGO staging classifies AKI severity by creatinine rise or urine output. Stage 1 is the mildest; Stage 3 is the most severe and includes anyone requiring renal replacement therapy. Staging guides management intensity.

**Q:** What urine output criteria define oliguria in the context of AKI?

**A:** <0.5 mL/kg/h for >6 hours

AKI can manifest as anuria, oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h for >6 h), or occasionally polyuria. Oliguria is an important clinical feature of AKI alongside azotemia and edema.

**Q:** What are the two most common causes of AKI in hospitalized patients?

**A:** Prerenal azotemia (decreased perfusion) and acute tubular necrosis (ATN)

Prerenal azotemia from decreased renal perfusion is the most common cause; ATN (often from ischemia or nephrotoxins) is the most common intrinsic renal cause. Importantly, sustained prerenal failure can progress to ATN.

**Q:** How do you differentiate prerenal AKI from ATN using urine indices?

**A:** Prerenal: Urine Na <20 mEq/L, FENa <1%, urine osmolality >500 mOsm/kg, BUN:Cr ratio >20, normal urine microscopy. ATN: Urine Na ~40 mEq/L, FENa >2%, urine osmolality <350 mOsm/kg, pigmented granular casts

In prerenal AKI, intact tubular function conserves sodium (low urine Na, low FENa) and concentrates urine. In ATN, damaged tubules cannot reabsorb sodium or concentrate urine. Pigmented granular (muddy brown) casts are hallmark of ATN.

**Q:** What clinical clues suggest a prerenal etiology in AKI?

**A:** Decreased BP, increased HR, orthostatic changes, oliguria, urea rise disproportionate to creatinine rise (BUN:Cr >20), urine Na <10-20 mmol/L, urine osmolality >500 mOsm/kg, FENa <1%

Prerenal AKI results from reduced renal perfusion. Volume depletion signs and intact tubular conservation (low urine Na, concentrated urine, low FENa) are hallmarks. The disproportionate urea rise reflects enhanced proximal tubular reabsorption.

**Q:** What clues suggest postrenal AKI?

**A:** Known solitary kidney, older male with prostatic hypertrophy, recent retroperitoneal surgery, anuria, palpable bladder, hydronephrosis on ultrasound, FENa >2-3%, urine osmolality 250-300 mOsm/kg

Postrenal AKI from obstruction requires functional solitary kidney or bilateral obstruction. Anuria is a key clue. Ultrasound showing hydronephrosis confirms obstruction. FENa is elevated because obstruction impairs tubular function.

**Q:** What drugs cause disordered autoregulation leading to prerenal AKI?

**A:** NSAIDs (impair afferent arteriole dilation), ACEIs/ARBs (impair efferent arteriole constriction), calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus), hypercalcemia

These medications interfere with the renal autoregulatory mechanisms that maintain GFR under low-perfusion states. NSAIDs block prostaglandin-mediated afferent vasodilation; ACEIs/ARBs block angiotensin-mediated efferent vasoconstriction.

**Q:** What are indications for renal biopsy in AKI?

**A:** Diagnosis uncertain, prerenal azotemia or ATN is unlikely, RPGN or significant glomerular disease (proteinuria + RBC casts) with normal kidney size/echogenicity, and to inform prognosis (degree of scarring/fibrosis guides decision on immunosuppression)

Renal biopsy is reserved for cases where the diagnosis cannot be established clinically and where the result will change management. It is particularly useful when glomerulonephritis or interstitial nephritis is suspected.

**Q:** According to the STARRT-AKI trial (NEJM 2020), what is the optimal timing for initiating renal replacement therapy in critically ill patients with AKI?

**A:** Standard (non-accelerated) strategy is preferred. Accelerated RRT initiation was not associated with lower 90-day mortality and had higher rates of adverse events including catheter-related complications.

The STARRT-AKI RCT found no mortality benefit to accelerated RRT initiation (90-day mortality ~43.9% in both groups). Accelerated strategy led to more adverse events. Standard strategy waits for specific clinical indications before starting RRT.

**Q:** What are the indications for dialysis in AKI?

**A:** Refractory volume overload, refractory metabolic acidosis, refractory hyperkalemia, uremic encephalopathy, uremic pericarditis (AEIOU mnemonic: Acidosis, Electrolytes, Ingestions, Overload, Uremia)

Dialysis is initiated when conservative management fails to control life-threatening complications of AKI. Early (prophylactic) dialysis in critically ill patients without these indications has not been shown to improve outcomes.

**Q:** What histological terms describe the number and extent of glomeruli affected in glomerular disease?

**A:** Number affected: Diffuse (majority abnormal) vs. Focal (some abnormal). Extent of individual glomerulus: Global (entire glomerulus) vs. Segmental (only part of glomerulus)

These histological descriptors are crucial for classifying glomerular diseases. For example, FSGS = focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (some glomeruli affected, only in part). Diffuse global changes indicate more severe, widespread disease.

**Q:** What is crescent formation in the kidney and what does it signify?

**A:** Parietal epithelial cell (Bowman's capsule lining) proliferation and mononuclear cell infiltration forming crescent shapes in Bowman's space. It is the hallmark of inflammatory glomerulonephritis and is most commonly seen in RPGN.

Crescents represent severe glomerular injury with proliferation of parietal epithelial cells into Bowman's space. Crescentic GN is the most aggressive form of glomerular disease and is frequently seen in RPGN, requiring urgent immunosuppression.

**Q:** What are the clinical syndromes of glomerular disease on the nephritic-nephrotic spectrum?

**A:** Pure nephrotic end: proteinuria, minimal hematuria (MCD, membranous, FSGS). Intermediate: MPGN, focal proliferative GN, IgA nephropathy. Pure nephritic end: hematuria, RBC casts, decreased GFR (diffuse proliferative GN, crescentic GN)

Glomerular disease exists on a spectrum. Nephrotic syndrome is characterized by heavy proteinuria and edema. Nephritic syndrome features hematuria, RBC casts, hypertension, and azotemia. Many conditions overlap across the spectrum.

**Q:** What is the upper limit of normal daily protein excretion, and what defines microalbuminuria?

**A:** Normal: <150 mg total protein/day and <30 mg albumin/day. Microalbuminuria: ACR ≥2.0 mg/mmol (or 30-300 mg albumin/day). Nephrotic range proteinuria: >3500 mg total protein/1.73m²/day (ACR >220 mg/mmol)

Microalbuminuria (ACR ≥2.0 mg/mmol) is the earliest sign of diabetic nephropathy and a marker of vascular endothelial dysfunction. Persistent albuminuria >3 months is considered CKD. Nephrotic range proteinuria defines nephrotic syndrome.

**Q:** What are the three mechanisms of pathologic proteinuria?

**A:** 1. Glomerular: damage to filtration barrier allows albumin and high MW proteins to pass. 2. Tubular: impaired reabsorption of normally filtered low MW proteins (proximal tubule dysfunction). 3. Overflow: overproduction of low MW proteins exceeding reabsorptive capacity (e.g., light chains in myeloma)

Understanding the mechanism of proteinuria guides diagnosis. Glomerular proteinuria (albumin-predominant) suggests GN. Tubular proteinuria suggests AIN or Fanconi syndrome. Overflow proteinuria suggests paraprotein disorders like multiple myeloma.

**Q:** What is IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease) and how does it present?

**A:** Most common primary glomerular disease worldwide. Presents most frequently with gross hematuria episodically after upper respiratory tract infection (synpharyngitic). More common in White and Asian populations, 2nd-3rd decades. Immunofluorescence shows mesangial IgA and C3 deposits.

IgA nephropathy differs from post-streptococcal GN by its synpharyngitic onset (hematuria concurrent with URI, not 2-3 weeks later). It may be associated with cirrhosis, HIV, celiac disease. Treatment includes RAAS blockers for proteinuria; immunosuppression for progressive disease.

**Q:** What is Alport Syndrome and what are its key features?

**A:** Hereditary nephritis from Type IV collagen mutation. X-linked nephritis associated with sensorineural hearing loss and ocular abnormalities. Presents with hematuria and proteinuria <2 g/d. Progresses to ESRD.

Alport syndrome results from mutations in Type IV collagen genes (COL4A3, COL4A4, COL4A5). The X-linked form (COL4A5) is most common and primarily affects males. The triad of nephritis, sensorineural hearing loss, and ocular abnormalities distinguishes it from thin basement membrane disease.

**Q:** What is the classic triad of nephritic syndrome?

**A:** 1. Hematuria (microscopic or macroscopic) with RBC casts and/or dysmorphic RBCs. 2. Azotemia (increased Cr and urea). 3. Hypertension (due to salt and water retention)

Nephritic syndrome reflects glomerular inflammation causing disruption of the filtration barrier (hematuria), reduced GFR (azotemia), and sodium/water retention (HTN and edema). Proteinuria is usually sub-nephrotic (<3.5 g/day).

**Q:** What are the three immunofluorescence types of RPGN and their associated conditions?

**A:** Type I (15%): Anti-GBM mediated (Goodpasture's). Type II (24%): Immune complex mediated (post-infectious GN, lupus, IgA, MPGN). Type III (60%): Pauci-immune (ANCA-associated vasculitis — GPA, MPA). Type IV: Double-antibody positive (anti-GBM + ANCA)

Classifying RPGN by immunofluorescence pattern directs targeted therapy. Anti-GBM disease (Type I) requires plasmapheresis + immunosuppression. ANCA vasculitis (Type III, most common) requires steroids + cyclophosphamide or rituximab.

**Q:** What is the treatment for RPGN?

**A:** Depends on type. General: corticosteroids (pulse steroids) + cyclophosphamide or other cytotoxic agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab). Plasmapheresis for anti-GBM disease (Type I). RAAS blockade for BP control. Monitor for ESRD progression.

RPGN is a nephrology emergency — rapid diagnosis and treatment is essential to preserve renal function. Anti-GBM disease requires urgent plasmapheresis to remove circulating antibodies. ANCA-associated RPGN responds to immunosuppression. Post-infectious RPGN may resolve with treatment of underlying infection.

**Q:** What are the defining features of nephrotic syndrome?

**A:** Heavy proteinuria >3.5 g/24h (or >3.5 g/1.73m²/day), hypoalbuminemia (<3 g/dL), peripheral edema, hyperlipidemia (elevated LDL), lipiduria (fatty casts and oval fat bodies), and hypercoagulable state (loss of antithrombin III, Protein C and S)

Nephrotic syndrome results from increased glomerular permeability. Hypoalbuminemia reduces oncotic pressure causing edema. Increased hepatic lipoprotein synthesis causes hyperlipidemia. Urinary loss of anticoagulants predisposes to renal vein thrombosis and VTE.

**Q:** What is minimal change disease (MCD) and what is its classic presentation and treatment?

**A:** Glomeruli appear normal on light microscopy (foot-process effacement on EM). Classic presentation: child with sudden-onset nephrotic syndrome. Secondary causes: Hodgkin's lymphoma. Drug causes: NSAIDs. Treatment: corticosteroids (highly responsive)

MCD is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children. The term 'minimal change' refers to normal light microscopy — electron microscopy reveals diffuse podocyte foot-process effacement. It is exquisitely steroid-sensitive, distinguishing it from FSGS.

**Q:** What are the secondary causes of membranous glomerulopathy?

**A:** HBV, SLE, solid tumours (lung, breast, GI). Drug causes: Gold, penicillamine. Treatment: Reduce BP, ACEI, steroids, rituximab, cytotoxic agents (cyclophosphamide), calcineurin inhibitors, immunosuppressive agents

Membranous glomerulopathy is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults. It should prompt a search for secondary causes, especially malignancy in older patients. PLA2R antibody is positive in ~75% of primary cases.

**Q:** What are the secondary causes of FSGS?

**A:** Reflux nephropathy, HIV (collapsing FSGS), obesity, sickle cell disease, heroin (drug cause). Treatment: ACEI/ARB for proteinuria, steroids and cytotoxic agents (cyclophosphamide) for RPGN or declining function

FSGS can be primary (podocyte injury) or secondary to hyperfiltration states (obesity, solitary kidney, sickle cell) or direct podocyte toxins (HIV, heroin). HIV-associated nephropathy causes collapsing FSGS and is most common in African Americans with Apo-L1 risk genotypes.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of nodular glomerulosclerosis?

**A:** Diabetes mellitus (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules) and amyloidosis. These are the most common secondary causes of glomerular disease overall.

Nodular glomerulosclerosis forms discrete nodular deposits in the mesangium. In DM, these are Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules from advanced glycation end products. In amyloidosis, amyloid deposits form similar-appearing nodules but stain with Congo red.

**Q:** What serological workup is used when investigating glomerular disease?

**A:** ANA (lupus), p-ANCA/MPO and c-ANCA/PR3 (vasculitis), C3/C4 (complement levels), anti-GBM, HBV, HCV, HIV, ASOT (post-streptococcal), VDRL, serum immunoelectrophoresis (amyloid/myeloma), cryoglobulins, ESR, serum free light chain assay

A comprehensive serological screen is necessary to identify secondary causes of glomerular disease. Low complement with ANA and anti-dsDNA suggests lupus nephritis. ANCA is essential for vasculitis. Anti-GBM is critical for Goodpasture's disease.

**Q:** What are the ISN/RPS classes of lupus nephritis and their treatments?

**A:** Class I (minimal mesangial): no treatment. Class II (mesangial proliferative): lowest dose steroids + observation. Class III (focal): steroids + cytotoxic drugs. Class IV (diffuse, most severe): steroids + cytotoxic drugs. Class V (membranous): controversial steroids. Class VI (advanced sclerotic): ESRD planning (dialysis or transplant)

Lupus nephritis classification guides management. Class IV diffuse lupus nephritis is the most severe and requires aggressive immunosuppression with steroids and cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate mofetil. Low complement (C3/C4) and high anti-dsDNA indicate active lupus nephritis.

**Q:** What are the key features of IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura)?

**A:** Systemic IgA vasculitis with IgA1-dominant immune complex deposition in small vessels. More common in children. Classic tetrad: palpable purpura (buttocks and legs), abdominal pain, arthralgia, and renal disease. IgA + C3 on mesangial immunofluorescence. Usually self-limiting; 10% progress to CKD.

HSP/IgA vasculitis is the most common systemic vasculitis in children. The palpable purpura on dependent areas (buttocks, legs) distinguishes it from other causes of purpura. Renal involvement (hematuria, proteinuria) occurs in up to 50% and determines long-term prognosis.

**Q:** What ANCA subtypes are associated with granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) vs. microscopic polyangiitis (MPA)?

**A:** c-ANCA (anti-PR3/Proteinase 3): most commonly associated with GPA (Wegener's granulomatosis). p-ANCA (anti-MPO/Myeloperoxidase): most commonly associated with microscopic polyangiitis.

ANCA-associated vasculitis causes pauci-immune RPGN (crescents with no immune staining on IF). Both forms cause focal segmental necrotizing GN. Treatment is steroids + cyclophosphamide or rituximab; plasmapheresis for severe renal disease or pulmonary hemorrhage.

**Q:** What is the most common infectious association with cryoglobulinemic vasculitis?

**A:** Hepatitis C virus (HCV) — present in at least 50% of patients with cryoglobulinemia

Cryoglobulinemia presents with purpura, fever, Raynaud's phenomenon, arthralgias, and AKI. Decreased serum complement (especially C4) is characteristic. Treatment targets the underlying cause (antiviral for HCV) plus plasmapheresis, steroids, and cyclophosphamide.

**Q:** What organism most commonly causes shunt nephritis, and what is the treatment?

**A:** Staphylococcus epidermidis. Treatment: remove the infected shunt and administer appropriate antibiotics. Consider ventriculoperitoneal shunt (lowest risk for shunt nephritis).

Shunt nephritis is immune-complex mediated nephritis associated with chronically infected ventriculo-atrial shunts. It presents as acute nephritic syndrome with decreased complement and can progress to nephrotic range proteinuria. Removing the source of infection is essential.

**Q:** What are the histological and clinical features of HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN)?

**A:** Histology: collapsing FSGS with tubular cystic dilation and tubulo-reticular inclusions. Clinical: predominantly African American men, heavy proteinuria, rapid progressive renal insufficiency. Apo-L1 risk genotypes. Progresses to kidney failure within 1 year without treatment. Treatment: HAART, short-term high-dose steroids, ACEI.

HIVAN is the most common cause of ESRD in HIV+ African Americans. Apo-L1 gene variants (G1, G2) dramatically increase risk. The collapsing variant of FSGS is characteristic. HAART is the cornerstone of treatment and has markedly reduced HIVAN incidence.

**Q:** What renal manifestations are caused by Hepatitis B vs. Hepatitis C?

**A:** Hepatitis B: membranous nephropathy, membranoproliferative GN, polyarteritis nodosa. Hepatitis C: membranous nephropathy, membranoproliferative GN, cryoglobulinemia (Type II/III)

Both hepatitis B and C can cause membranous and membranoproliferative GN. HBV uniquely causes polyarteritis nodosa. HCV is the most common cause of cryoglobulinemia. These associations are tested frequently on the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN) and how does it differ from glomerular disease?

**A:** TIN involves cellular infiltrates primarily affecting the renal interstitium and tubular cells. Key feature: tubular functional defects (Fanconi syndrome, RTA, DI) are disproportionately greater than the decrease in GFR, distinguishing it from glomerular disease which prominently affects filtration.

In TIN, tubular dysfunction manifests as glycosuria, aminoaciduria, RTA, or concentrating defects depending on which tubular segment is affected. Proteinuria is typically mild and non-nephrotic. AKI from TIN is often reversible if the offending agent is removed.

**Q:** What tubular dysfunction syndromes arise from proximal vs. distal vs. collecting duct injury?

**A:** Proximal tubule: Fanconi syndrome (glycosuria, aminoaciduria, phosphaturia, hyperuricosuria), Type II RTA. Distal tubule: Type I RTA (decreased H+ secretion), Na-wasting nephropathy, Type IV RTA (from hyperkalemia). Collecting duct: urinary concentrating defect (nephrogenic DI), polyuria.

The site of tubular injury determines the clinical manifestations. Proximal tubule injury from heavy metals, myeloma, or genetic causes causes Fanconi syndrome. Distal injury from amyloidosis or obstruction impairs acid secretion and sodium handling.

**Q:** What antibiotic classes most commonly cause acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (AIN)?

**A:** Beta-lactams (most common), sulfonamides, rifampin, quinolones, cephalosporins

Drug-induced AIN is a hypersensitivity reaction occurring 7-10 days after drug exposure. Beta-lactams are the most common culprits. NSAIDs, PPIs, allopurinol, thiazides, and furosemide also cause AIN. The triad of fever, rash, and eosinophilia is classic but only seen in ~10% of cases.

**Q:** What are the urine findings in acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)?

**A:** Mild, non-nephrotic range proteinuria and microscopic hematuria, sterile pyuria, WBC casts, and eosinophiluria (not sole finding). Blood: eosinophilia, elevated Cr and urea, normal anion gap metabolic acidosis (RTA), hypophosphatemia, hypo- or hyperkalemia.

WBC casts are the hallmark of tubulointerstitial inflammation (AIN, pyelonephritis). Eosinophiluria has low sensitivity but high negative predictive value. Normal anion gap acidosis results from impaired tubular acid secretion (RTA). Gallium scan shows intense renal uptake.

**Q:** What is the clinical triad of allergic/hypersensitivity AIN?

**A:** Fever, eosinophilia, and skin rash (serum sickness-like syndrome). Drug-induced AIN typically occurs 7-10 days after drug exposure. Rifampin characteristically causes serum sickness-like syndrome.

While the classic triad of fever, rash, and eosinophilia is taught, it is only present in a minority of AIN cases. AKI occurring 7-10 days after starting a new medication should raise suspicion for AIN. Treatment is removal of the offending agent and, if immune/allergic etiology, early corticosteroids.

**Q:** What is the most important cause of chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN)?

**A:** Urinary tract obstruction (tumours, stones, bladder outlet obstruction, vesicoureteral reflux)

Chronic TIN is characterized by slowly progressive renal failure with moderate proteinuria and tubular dysfunction. Obstruction is the most important cause; others include chronic pyelonephritis, nephrotoxins (analgesics, lithium, cisplatin), heavy metals, multiple myeloma, and granulomatous disease.

**Q:** What are the nephrotoxic drugs most commonly associated with chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis?

**A:** Analgesics: NSAIDs (most common), acetaminophen. Chemotherapy: cisplatin. Immunosuppressants: cyclosporine, tacrolimus, lithium. Heavy metals: lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic. Herbal: aristolochic acid (Chinese herbs).

Analgesic nephropathy from chronic NSAID use is a common cause of CKD. Lithium causes nephrogenic DI acutely and chronic TIN with prolonged use. Aristolochic acid (found in some Chinese herbal preparations) causes rapidly progressive TIN — an important association to know.

**Q:** What are the key findings in chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN)?

**A:** Non-anion gap metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia out of proportion to renal insufficiency, polyuria, nocturia, partial or complete Fanconi syndrome, progressive renal failure with azotemia. Urine: mild proteinuria, few RBCs and WBCs, no RBC casts. Ultrasound: shrunken kidneys with irregular contours.

Shrunken, scarred kidneys on ultrasound differentiate chronic from acute TIN (which shows normal or enlarged kidneys). The absence of RBC casts (which are hallmarks of GN) and the presence of tubular dysfunction help distinguish TIN from glomerular disease.

**Q:** What are the three phases of acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and their features?

**A:** 1. Oliguric phase: decreased urine output, azotemia, uremia — lasts 10-14 days. 2. Diuretic phase: urine output >500 mL/day as retained water/solutes are excreted, but tubular damage persists. 3. Recovery phase: tubular function recovers.

During the oliguric phase, hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis are life-threatening complications. The diuretic phase can paradoxically cause electrolyte losses and dehydration despite recovering GFR. Full renal recovery may take weeks to months.

**Q:** What are the causes of acute tubular necrosis (ATN)?

**A:** Ischemic: sustained prerenal AKI (hypotension, sepsis, hemorrhage, cardiac failure). Nephrotoxic: contrast agents, aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin, heavy metals, rhabdomyolysis (myoglobin), hemolysis (hemoglobin)

ATN is the most common cause of non-prerenal AKI in hospitalized patients. Ischemic ATN results from prolonged prerenal hypoperfusion. Nephrotoxic ATN results from direct tubular cell toxicity. Rhabdomyolysis and hemolysis cause pigment nephropathy (myoglobin/hemoglobin are directly tubulotoxic).

**Q:** What are the urine findings in acute tubular necrosis (ATN)?

**A:** High FENa (>2%), pigmented granular (muddy brown) casts, urine osmolality <350 mOsm/kg, urine Na ~40 mEq/L

Pigmented granular (muddy brown) casts are the hallmark of ATN, formed from necrotic tubular epithelial cells. High FENa reflects loss of tubular sodium reabsorptive capacity. A fluid challenge will NOT correct serum creatinine in ATN, helping differentiate from prerenal AKI.

**Q:** What are the life-threatening complications of ATN?

**A:** Hyperkalemia (can cause arrhythmias — most urgent), metabolic acidosis, hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypoalbuminemia

Hyperkalemia is the most immediately dangerous complication of ATN, causing fatal arrhythmias. It requires urgent management including IV calcium gluconate, insulin/dextrose, salbutamol, sodium bicarbonate, and dialysis if refractory.

**Q:** How do you prevent contrast-induced AKI (contrast nephropathy)?

**A:** Pre- and post-procedure isotonic saline hydration. Hold diuretics, NSAIDs, ACEIs/ARBs, cyclosporine on morning of procedure. Use low-dose non-ionic, iso- or low-osmolal contrast agents. Note: IV sodium bicarbonate and N-acetylcysteine NOT shown to be beneficial (PRESERVE trial, NEJM 2018).

The PRESERVE trial (NEJM 2018) showed no benefit of IV sodium bicarbonate over IV saline, and no benefit of oral N-acetylcysteine over placebo in preventing contrast-associated AKI or composite outcomes at 90 days. Adequate hydration with isotonic saline remains the evidence-based intervention.

**Q:** What does the PRESERVE trial (NEJM 2018) show about contrast-induced AKI prevention?

**A:** IV sodium bicarbonate was NOT superior to IV 0.9% sodium chloride, and oral N-acetylcysteine was NOT superior to oral placebo in preventing contrast-associated AKI, death, need for dialysis, or persistent decline in kidney function at 90 days.

This landmark trial definitively showed that commonly used interventions (bicarb and NAC) for preventing contrast nephropathy are ineffective. Isotonic saline hydration remains the standard. The findings changed clinical practice guidelines regarding pre-procedure prophylaxis.

**Q:** What urine cast types are associated with which renal conditions?

**A:** RBC casts: glomerulonephritis (pathognomonic). WBC casts: tubulointerstitial nephritis, pyelonephritis. Pigmented granular (muddy brown) casts: ATN. Fatty casts/oval fat bodies: nephrotic syndrome. Waxy/broad casts: advanced CKD (wide casts from dilated tubules). Hyaline casts: normal or dehydration.

Urine cast identification is a high-yield MCCQE1 skill. RBC casts are pathognomonic for glomerulonephritis. WBC casts indicate tubular inflammation. Fatty casts with oval fat bodies are seen in heavy proteinuria. Cast type narrows the differential diagnosis of AKI or kidney disease.

**Q:** When is renal biopsy indicated in the workup of glomerular disease?

**A:** When proteinuria >0.5 g/d with casts and/or hematuria and specific diagnosis needed. Required for: RPGN, unexplained nephrotic syndrome in adults, suspected AIN or GN where diagnosis is uncertain, to assess prognosis (degree of fibrosis guides decision on immunosuppression). Contraindicated in solitary kidney, uncontrolled hypertension, bleeding disorders.

Renal biopsy with light microscopy, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy is essential to classify glomerular disease and guide therapy. In children with nephrotic syndrome (presumed MCD), biopsy is typically deferred and empirical steroids are given first.

**Q:** What is amyloidosis of the kidney and how does it present?

**A:** Nodular deposits of amyloid in the mesangium (usually amyloid light chain — AL type). Presents as nephrotic range proteinuria (albuminuria) with progressive renal insufficiency. Can be primary or secondary to multiple myeloma, TB, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory diseases, or malignancy.

Renal amyloidosis is an important secondary cause of glomerular disease. Congo red staining shows apple-green birefringence under polarized light. AA amyloid (secondary) is associated with chronic inflammatory conditions. AL amyloid (primary/myeloma-associated) is more common and requires treatment of the underlying plasma cell disorder.

**Q:** What is the renal-ocular syndrome (idiopathic acute TIN)?

**A:** Acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (AIN) plus uveitis (TINU syndrome). Idiopathic etiology — not caused by drugs or infections. Treated with corticosteroids.

TINU (Tubulointerstitial Nephritis and Uveitis) syndrome is an uncommon idiopathic condition predominantly affecting adolescent females. The uveitis can precede, coincide with, or follow the nephritis. Corticosteroids treat both the renal and ocular components.

**Q:** Why is edema seen in nephrotic syndrome, and what two mechanisms contribute?

**A:** 1. Primary mechanism: hypoalbuminemia reduces plasma oncotic pressure → fluid shifts to interstitium. 2. Secondary mechanism: enhanced renal tubular reabsorption of sodium and water (possibly via filtered proteins stimulating cortical collecting duct epithelial sodium channels), causing primary sodium retention even before hypoalbuminemia develops.

The 'underfill' hypothesis (hypoalbuminemia → reduced oncotic pressure → edema → compensatory sodium retention) is complemented by the 'overfill' hypothesis (primary renal sodium retention independent of oncotic pressure). Both mechanisms likely contribute to edema in nephrotic syndrome.

**Q:** What is thin basement membrane disease and how does it differ from Alport syndrome?

**A:** Thin basement membrane disease: usually autosomal dominant, benign, presents with hematuria WITHOUT proteinuria, does not progress to ESRD. Alport syndrome: X-linked Type IV collagen mutation, associated with sensorineural hearing loss and ocular abnormalities, proteinuria <2 g/d, progresses to ESRD.

Both conditions cause persistent microscopic hematuria but have very different prognoses. Thin basement membrane disease is the most common cause of benign familial hematuria. Alport syndrome requires aggressive management to slow ESRD progression. Genetics and extrarenal findings distinguish them.

**Q:** What are the indications for urology consult and possible cystoscopy in the workup of hematuria?

**A:** Age >50, risk factors for bladder cancer (smoking, occupational chemical exposure, prior pelvic radiation), or low suspicion for renal/glomerular cause of hematuria

Hematuria can be from either renal (glomerular, tubulointerstitial) or urological (bladder, ureteral, prostate) causes. In older patients or those with bladder cancer risk factors, anatomical causes must be excluded. Glomerular hematuria typically presents with RBC casts and dysmorphic RBCs.

**Q:** What systemic diseases can cause MPGN (membranoproliferative GN) as a secondary cause?

**A:** HCV (most common), HBV, SLE, malaria, leukemia, lymphoma, shunt nephritis. MPGN on the nephritic-nephrotic spectrum: has both hematuria and variable proteinuria.

MPGN is classified by immunofluorescence into immune complex-mediated (Type I, caused by HCV, HBV, SLE, cryoglobulinemia, and infections) and complement-mediated (Type II = dense deposit disease). Treatment addresses the underlying cause; immune complex MPGN may respond to ACE inhibition or immunosuppression.

**Q:** Which cancer types are most commonly associated with SIADH?

**A:** Small cell lung cancer (most classic), bronchogenic carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, Hodgkin's lymphoma, thymoma, leukemia.

Small cell lung cancer is the prototypical ectopic ADH-secreting tumour. Any malignancy can cause paraneoplastic SIADH, but small cell lung cancer is by far the most frequently tested. This is a common MCCQE1 question pairing hyponatremia with lung malignancy.

**Q:** Which drug classes are associated with SIADH?

**A:** Antidepressants (TCAs, SSRIs), antineoplastics (vincristine, cyclophosphamide), anti-epileptics (carbamazepine, barbiturates), chlorpropamide, ACE inhibitors, DDAVP, oxytocin, nicotine.

SSRIs and carbamazepine are the most commonly tested drug causes of SIADH. Carbamazepine both enhances ADH secretion and potentiates its renal effects. Cyclophosphamide at high doses causes SIADH through direct enhancement of ADH action.

**Q:** What formula estimates the change in serum [Na+] from 1 L of any IV fluid?

**A:** Change in serum [Na+] = {(IV [Na+] + [K+]) − patient's serum [Na+]} / (TBW + 1)

Where TBW = 0.6 × weight (kg) for men; 0.5 × weight (kg) for women.

This Adrogue-Madias formula is essential for calculating safe sodium correction rates. Adding 1 to TBW accounts for the litre of infused fluid added to total body water. Both the sodium AND potassium content of the IV fluid affect serum sodium, as potassium is also osmotically active.

**Q:** How is overly-rapid sodium correction managed?

**A:** 1. Give free water (IV D5W) to re-lower sodium
2. Administer DDAVP (1-4 mcg IV q8h) to stop ongoing water diuresis and prevent further sodium rise

DDAVP acts as a synthetic ADH analogue, causing water retention and re-dilution of serum sodium. This 'proactive' DDAVP strategy (giving DDAVP alongside hypertonic saline) is increasingly used to control the rate of correction and prevent ODS.

**Q:** Define hypernatremia and list its main causes.

**A:** Serum [Na+] >145 mmol/L — always a hyperosmolar state.

Causes:
• Reduced intake: elderly, infants, coma, post-surgical
• Extrarenal losses: GI (diarrhea, fistulas), insensible losses (exercise, seizures)
• Renal losses: Central DI, nephrogenic DI, osmotic diuresis (hyperglycemia, mannitol, urea, polyethylene glycol)

Hypernatremia = too little water relative to sodium. It is less common than hyponatremia because intact thirst mechanism and ADH secretion are protective. Most patients who develop hypernatremia have impaired access to water or impaired thirst.

**Q:** What fluids are used to treat hypernatremia and how much free water do they provide?

**A:** • IV D5W: ~1 L free water per litre
• IV 0.45% NS (half-normal saline): ~500 mL free water per litre
• IV 2/3 & 1/3 (3.3% dextrose + 0.3% NaCl): intermediate free water

First correct hemodynamic instability with 0.9% NS bolus if volume depleted, then switch to hypotonic solution.

D5W is isotonic in the bag but provides essentially free water after glucose is metabolized. 0.45% NS is the most commonly used solution for chronic hypernatremia correction. If the patient is hemodynamically unstable, isotonic saline takes priority over sodium correction.

**Q:** What is the water deficit formula used in hypernatremia?

**A:** H2O deficit = TBW × ([Na+]plasma / 140 − 1)

TBW = 0.6 × weight (kg) for men; 0.5 × weight (kg) for women

This formula estimates the free water deficit needed to restore serum sodium to normal (140 mmol/L). The calculated deficit must be corrected over the appropriate time frame — within 24h for acute, or at 8-10 mmol/L/24h for chronic hypernatremia — and adjusted for ongoing losses.

**Q:** Define diabetes insipidus (DI) and distinguish central from nephrogenic.

**A:** DI: collecting tubule impermeable to water due to absence of or impaired response to ADH → hypotonic polyuria.

• Central DI: deficient ADH release from posterior pituitary (neurosurgery, trauma, granulomatous disease, malignancy)
• Nephrogenic DI: renal resistance to ADH (lithium, amyloidosis, sickle cell, Sjögren, polycystic kidney, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia)

Both types present with hypotonic polyuria and hypernatremia (if access to water is limited). The dehydration test distinguishes them: after depriving water until hypernatremia develops, give DDAVP — urine concentrates (Uosm >300) in central DI but not in nephrogenic DI.

**Q:** How is the dehydration (water deprivation) test performed and interpreted?

**A:** 1. Deprive patient of water under supervision until: Uosm >300 mOsm/kg, serum [Na+] >146, OR 3% body weight loss
2. Administer DDAVP 2 mcg IV
3. Interpret:
   • If Uosm rises >300 after DDAVP → Central DI (responds to exogenous ADH)
   • If Uosm fails to rise → Nephrogenic DI (renal resistance to ADH)
   • If Uosm concentrated before DDAVP → Primary polydipsia

The test is used when the patient is not already hypernatremic at assessment. Copeptin (a stable surrogate for vasopressin) measured after hypertonic saline infusion has shown superior diagnostic accuracy compared to the water deprivation test in recent studies.

**Q:** What is the treatment of central DI vs. nephrogenic DI?

**A:** Central DI:
• DDAVP 10 mcg intranasally or 2 mcg SC/IV

Nephrogenic DI:
• Maintain fluid intake to match losses (PO water, IV D5W, IV 0.45% NS)
• Treat underlying cause
• Thiazide diuretics (paradoxically reduce urine output via volume depletion → proximal tubular reabsorption → less delivery to collecting duct)
• Low-sodium, low-protein diet

DDAVP is ineffective in nephrogenic DI because the kidney cannot respond to ADH. Thiazides work paradoxically by inducing mild volume depletion, stimulating proximal tubular sodium and water reabsorption, thereby reducing the volume of filtrate delivered to the ADH-unresponsive collecting duct.

**Q:** What are the ECG changes of hypokalemia in order of severity?

**A:** 1. U waves (most characteristic — low amplitude wave after T wave)
2. Flattened or inverted T waves
3. Depressed ST segment
4. Prolonged QT interval
5. Sinus bradycardia
6. (Severe) PR prolongation, wide QRS, arrhythmias (VF, VT)

Note: ECG changes are more predictive of clinical picture than serum [K+].

U waves are the hallmark ECG finding of hypokalemia, reflecting delayed ventricular repolarization. Hypokalemia is particularly dangerous in patients on digoxin (potentiates toxicity), those with CAD, or co-existing hypomagnesemia. ECG monitoring is essential when [K+] <3.0 mmol/L.

**Q:** How is renal vs. non-renal K+ loss distinguished in hypokalemia?

**A:** Spot urine K:Cr ratio:
• <1.5 mEq/mmol → GI loss (non-renal; e.g., diarrhea, vomiting)
• >1.5 mEq/mmol → Renal loss

If renal loss: check BP and acid-base status, plasma renin and aldosterone, serum Mg2+.

24-hour urine K+ excretion can also be used.

The spot urine K:Cr ratio is a practical bedside tool to differentiate the cause of hypokalemia. Renal K+ wasting indicates diuretic use, hyperaldosteronism, RTA, or Mg2+ deficiency. Urine K+ <20 mmol/L suggests extrarenal losses (GI or poor intake).

**Q:** What are the key principles of potassium replacement in hypokalemia?

**A:** • Oral KCl preferred (8 mmol/tablet or 20 mmol/tablet; liquid solution also available)
• IV: KCl in saline — max 40 mmol/L via peripheral vein, 60 mmol/L via central vein, max infusion rate 20 mmol/h
• Avoid dextrose solutions (insulin release worsens hypokalemia)
• Replace Mg2+ before or concurrently (can't correct K+ if Mg2+ depleted)
• Caution if renal impairment, DM, or elderly (risk of hyperkalemia)
• K+-sparing diuretics can prevent renal K+ loss

Oral replacement is safer and preferred. IV potassium must never be given as bolus — rapid infusion causes fatal arrhythmias. Hypomagnesemia causes persistent renal K+ wasting by increasing K+ secretion in the collecting duct — magnesium must be repleted first.

**Q:** Why does hypokalemia often coexist with metabolic alkalosis?

**A:** • K+ shifts from cells to ECF → H+ shifts into cells → intracellular acidosis
• Plasma [HCO3-] increases (alkalosis)
• Renal tubular cells respond to low intracellular pH by secreting H+ into lumen and reabsorbing HCO3- (perpetuating alkalosis)
• Conversely, metabolic alkalosis drives K+ into cells, worsening hypokalemia

The K+/H+ exchange across cell membranes creates a bidirectional relationship: hypokalemia causes alkalosis and alkalosis worsens hypokalemia. This cycle is maintained by renal compensation. Conditions like vomiting and diuretics cause both simultaneously.

**Q:** What are the ECG changes of hyperkalemia in order of severity?

**A:** 1. Peaked, narrow, symmetric T waves (earliest)
2. Decreased amplitude/loss of P waves
3. Prolonged PR interval
4. Widening of QRS → eventual merging with T wave (sine-wave pattern)
5. AV block
6. Ventricular fibrillation / asystole

Hyperkalemia progressively depolarizes cardiac membranes. Peaked T waves appear at [K+] ~6 mEq/L; sine-wave pattern and VF risk occur above 7-8 mEq/L. ECG changes are the primary indication for emergent treatment, not the absolute potassium level alone.

**Q:** What are the most common causes of pseudohyperkalemia?

**A:** • Sample hemolysis (most common)
• Prolonged time from venipuncture to analysis
• Sample taken from vein where IV KCl is running
• Prolonged tourniquet use
• Extreme leukocytosis or thrombocytosis

Management: repeat blood test with proper technique (no prolonged tourniquet, rapid processing).

Pseudohyperkalemia is important to exclude before treating, as unnecessary treatment of hyperkalemia can cause dangerous hypokalemia. Hemolysis releases intracellular K+ into the sample. Extreme thrombocytosis or leukocytosis releases K+ during clotting in the test tube.

**Q:** What causes hyperkalemia with a normal GFR (via hypoaldosteronism)?

**A:** Three mechanisms:
1. Decreased aldosterone stimulus (low renin, low aldosterone): diabetic nephropathy, NSAIDs, chronic interstitial nephritis, HIV, ACEi, ARBs, heparin
2. Decreased aldosterone production (normal renin, low aldosterone): Addison disease, AIDS, metastatic cancer, CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency)
3. Aldosterone resistance: K+-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone, amiloride, triamterene), renal tubulointerstitial disease

Type IV RTA (hyporenin-hypoaldosteronism) is the most common cause of hyperkalemia with normal GFR, especially in diabetic nephropathy. NSAIDs reduce renin release. ACEi/ARBs reduce angiotensin II, reducing aldosterone. Hyperkalemia here requires evaluation of the RAAS axis.

**Q:** Outline the stepwise acute treatment of life-threatening hyperkalemia.

**A:** 1. Stabilize myocardium: Calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% solution (1-2 amps) IV → onset minutes, lasts 30-60 min; no effect on serum [K+]

2. Shift K+ into cells:
   • Insulin (regular) 10 units IV + D50W 50-100 mL (monitor glucose q1h)
   • Salbutamol (Ventolin) 10-20 mg nebulized or 0.5 mg IV

3. Enhance K+ removal:
   • Via urine: furosemide 40 mg IV; fludrocortisone if aldosterone deficient
   • Via GI: patiromer (Veltassa) 8.4 g PO OD; sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC/Lokelma) 10 g TID x 48h; sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate) with caution
   • Dialysis: if renal failure or unresponsive to above

Mnemonic: BIG K → B-agonist, Insulin, Glucose, Kayexalate

Calcium gluconate does not lower K+ but protects the cardiac membrane by raising the action potential threshold. Shifts are temporary (4-6h for insulin). Definitive removal requires excretion via kidney or GI tract. Kayexalate should be used with caution due to risk of colonic necrosis, especially with sorbitol.

**Q:** Why must dextrose be given with insulin in hyperkalemia treatment, and what precaution applies?

**A:** Insulin drives K+ into cells but simultaneously lowers blood glucose → hypoglycemia risk.
• Give D50W (50 mL of 50% dextrose) with insulin 10 units IV
• Monitor capillary blood glucose q1h
• Caution: do NOT give dextrose before or without insulin if hyperkalemia is severe — hypertonic glucose raises osmolality → extracellular K+ shift → may worsen hyperkalemia and cause arrhythmia

The correct sequence is insulin first (or simultaneously with dextrose), never dextrose alone. Hypertonic glucose without insulin causes osmotic shift that temporarily raises serum K+. Regular insulin lowers K+ by ~0.8 mmol/L at 1 hour and its effect lasts 4-6 hours.

**Q:** What is the danger of sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate) in hyperkalemia treatment?

**A:** Risk of colonic necrosis and intestinal perforation, especially when used in combination with sorbitol.

Use with caution; patiromer (Veltassa) and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC/Lokelma) are newer, better-tolerated alternatives but are more expensive.

Kayexalate is a cation exchange resin that exchanges Na+ for K+ in the GI tract. The sorbitol co-administered to prevent constipation causes the dangerous intestinal injury. Patiromer and SZC are preferred when available due to better GI safety profiles, though cost limits widespread use.

**Q:** Define hyperphosphatemia and list its main causes.

**A:** Serum phosphate >1.45 mmol/L.

Causes:
• Increased phosphate load: GI intake (e.g., rectal enemas), IV phosphate, tumour lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, lactic/ketoacidosis
• Reduced renal clearance: AKI/CKD, hypoparathyroidism, acromegaly, tumour calcinosis
• Pseudohyperphosphatemia: hyperglobulinemia, hyperlipidemia, hyperbilirubinemia

In CKD, hyperphosphatemia is nearly universal in advanced stages due to reduced phosphate excretion. Phosphate binds calcium → hypocalcemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism. Phosphate also deposits in soft tissues and vessels causing calciphylaxis and cardiovascular disease.

**Q:** How is hyperphosphatemia treated?

**A:** • Acute: IV saline; hemodialysis if symptomatic
• Chronic:
  - Low phosphate diet
  - Phosphate binders taken WITH meals: calcium carbonate (CaCO3), lanthanum carbonate, sevelamer

Phosphate binders must be taken with meals to bind dietary phosphate in the GI tract before absorption. Calcium-based binders can worsen vascular calcification in CKD patients. Non-calcium binders (sevelamer, lanthanum) are preferred in CKD with vascular calcification or hypercalcemia.

**Q:** What are the causes of hypophosphatemia?

**A:** • Inadequate intake: starvation, malabsorption, antacid use (bind phosphate), alcoholism
• Renal losses: hyperparathyroidism, diuretics, Fanconi syndrome, X-linked/autosomal dominant hypophosphatemic rickets, multiple myeloma, early post-renal transplant
• Excessive skeletal mineralization: osteoblastic metastases, hungry bone syndrome (post-parathyroidectomy)
• Shift into cells: recovery from metabolic acidosis, respiratory alkalosis, refeeding (insulin-stimulated), DKA treatment

Refeeding syndrome is a classic MCCQE1 scenario: severely malnourished patients given carbohydrates release insulin → phosphate shifts into cells → severe hypophosphatemia causing respiratory failure, arrhythmias, and hemolysis. Hungry bone syndrome occurs after parathyroidectomy when suddenly avid bone reabsorbs calcium and phosphate.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of severe hypophosphatemia (<0.32 mmol/L)?

**A:** • Muscle weakness, respiratory depression (ventilatory failure)
• Low cardiac output / CHF (weakened cardiac muscle)
• Neurological: irritability, encephalopathy, seizures, coma
• Hemolytic anemia
• Decreased O2 delivery (low 2,3-DPG, O2 stuck to hemoglobin)
• Impaired leukocyte and platelet function (infections, bleeding)

All manifestations stem from depleted intracellular ATP and 2,3-DPG. Symptoms typically arise only when phosphate <0.32 mmol/L. Respiratory muscle weakness causing ventilatory failure is a life-threatening complication of severe hypophosphatemia, especially in ICU patients being weaned from ventilator.

**Q:** When and how is hypophosphatemia treated?

**A:** • Initiate treatment when serum [PO4] <0.64 mmol/L
• PO therapy: if asymptomatic OR symptomatic with [PO4] >0.32 mmol/L
  - 2-4 g/day divided BID-QID (start 1 g/day to minimize diarrhea), encourage phosphate-rich diet
• IV therapy: if symptomatic AND [PO4] <0.32 mmol/L

Oral phosphate is preferred when tolerated, but diarrhea limits the dose. IV phosphate is reserved for severe symptomatic cases (respiratory failure, severe hemolysis). Monitor calcium during replacement as phosphate can bind serum calcium causing hypocalcemia.

**Q:** Define hypermagnesemia, its causes, and treatment.

**A:** Serum magnesium >1.05 mmol/L.

Causes: AKI/CKD, Mg2+-containing antacids or enemas, IV magnesium sulphate (e.g., eclampsia treatment).

Features: drowsiness, hyporeflexia (loss of patellar reflex is early warning), respiratory depression, heart block, cardiac arrest, hypotension.

Treatment:
• Discontinue Mg2+-containing products
• Calcium gluconate 10% 10-20 mL IV (antagonizes Mg2+)
• Hemodialysis if renal failure

Hypermagnesemia is rare with normal renal function because kidneys efficiently excrete magnesium. Loss of deep tendon reflexes (patellar reflex) is an early clinical sign and is used to monitor for toxicity in obstetric patients receiving IV MgSO4 for eclampsia. Calcium gluconate reverses magnesium toxicity acutely.

**Q:** Define hypomagnesemia, its causes, and why it matters clinically.

**A:** Serum magnesium <0.70 mmol/L.

Causes:
• GI losses: starvation, vomiting/diarrhea, alcoholism, acute pancreatitis, malabsorption
• Renal losses: hyperaldosteronism (cirrhosis, CHF), hyperglycemia, loop/thiazide diuretics, nephrotoxic drugs, PPIs, early post-renal transplant

Clinical importance: Hypomagnesemia prevents correction of hypokalemia and hypocalcemia — must replete Mg2+ first.

Magnesium is required for proper Na/K-ATPase function and for PTH secretion and action. Without adequate Mg2+, K+ continues to be wasted renally and PTH cannot mobilize calcium. PPIs are an increasingly recognized cause of chronic hypomagnesemia in clinical practice.

**Q:** What are the clinical features and ECG changes of hypomagnesemia?

**A:** Clinical:
• Tremors, nausea/vomiting, lethargy/weakness
• Seizures, paresis
• Chvostek and Trousseau signs (secondary hypocalcemia)

ECG:
• Widened QRS
• Prolonged PR interval
• T-wave abnormalities
• Torsades de Pointes (TdP) — potentially fatal ventricular arrhythmia

Torsades de Pointes is the most feared arrhythmia of hypomagnesemia — magnesium IV is the treatment of choice for TdP regardless of serum magnesium levels. Chvostek and Trousseau signs reflect secondary hypocalcemia from PTH dysfunction.

**Q:** What is the anion gap (AG) formula, its normal range, and how does albumin affect it?

**A:** AG = [Na+] − ([Cl−] + [HCO3−])
Normal range: 10-14 mEq/L (baseline ~12)

Albumin correction: For each 10 g/L fall in serum albumin below normal (40 g/L), lower expected AG by 3 mEq/L.
• Example: albumin = 20 g/L → expected AG = 6 mEq/L (not 12)

Albumin is a negatively charged protein — the main unmeasured anion in normal plasma. Hypoalbuminemia lowers the normal AG, so a 'normal' measured AG in a hypoalbuminemic patient may actually represent a high AG metabolic acidosis. Always correct AG for albumin in critically ill patients.

**Q:** What is the osmolar gap, how is it calculated, and what does an elevated value suggest?

**A:** Osmolar gap = Measured serum osmolality − Calculated serum osmolality
Normal: <10 mOsm/kg

Calculated osmolality = 2[Na+] + [Urea] + [Glucose] (all in mmol/L)
Mnemonic: 'Two Salts and a Sticky BUN'

Elevated OG (>10): suggests presence of unmeasured osmoles:
• Toxic alcohols: methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropyl alcohol, ethanol
• Mannitol
• Post-TURP (sorbitol or glycine irrigation)

In toxic alcohol ingestion, the osmolar gap is elevated early (before metabolism) while the anion gap becomes elevated later (as alcohols are metabolized to acids). Measuring both simultaneously helps identify the stage of toxicity and guide urgency of treatment (fomepizole, dialysis).

**Q:** List the causes of high anion gap metabolic acidosis using the MUDPILES CAT mnemonic.

**A:** M — Methanol
U — Uremia (advanced renal failure)
D — Diabetic ketoacidosis (also starvation and alcoholic ketoacidosis)
P — Paraldehyde
I — Isopropyl alcohol / Iron / Ibuprofen / Indomethacin
L — Lactic acidosis (Type A: hypoperfusion; Type B: metformin, liver disease, thiamine deficiency, antiretrovirals)
E — Ethylene glycol
S — Salicylates

CAT:
C — Cyanide / Carbon monoxide
A — Alcoholic ketoacidosis
T — Toluene (glue sniffing)

Lactic acidosis (especially Type A from shock) and DKA are the most common causes of high AG metabolic acidosis in clinical practice. Methanol and ethylene glycol are classic toxicology causes. Metformin causes Type B lactic acidosis by inhibiting the electron transport chain.

**Q:** Distinguish Type A from Type B lactic acidosis.

**A:** Type A lactic acidosis (hypoxic):
• Due to tissue hypoperfusion → anaerobic metabolism
• Causes: any cause of shock, ischemic bowel, profound hypoxemia

Type B lactic acidosis (non-hypoxic):
• Failure to metabolize normally produced lactic acid
• Most common: severe liver disease
• Others: excessive alcohol, thiamine deficiency, metformin (ETC inhibition), certain antiretrovirals, large tumours, mitochondrial myopathies, cyanide poisoning

Note: Lactic acidosis and ketoacidosis generally do NOT cause hyperkalemia because lactate/ketoacid anions enter cells along with H+, preventing the usual K+/H+ exchange. This distinguishes them from mineral acid acidoses (e.g., HCl) which do cause hyperkalemia.

**Q:** List the causes of normal AG (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis using HARDUPSAY.

**A:** H — Hyperalimentation
A — Acetazolamide / Addison's disease
R — RTA (Type I, II, IV)
D — Diarrhea (most common)
U — Ureteroenteric fistula / Ureterosigmoidostomy
P — Pancreaticoduodenal fistula
S — Saline (high volume IV normal saline)
A — A component of DKA
Y — (various other)

Key: HCO3- is lost or replaced by Cl−

In normal AG metabolic acidosis, the 'missing' bicarbonate is replaced by chloride (hence hyperchloremia). Diarrhea causes direct HCO3- loss from the GI tract. High-volume normal saline causes 'dilutional' or 'hyperchloremic' acidosis by diluting bicarbonate and providing excess Cl-.

**Q:** How does the urine anion gap help distinguish causes of normal AG metabolic acidosis?

**A:** Urine AG = (Urine Na+ + Urine K+) − Urine Cl−

• Urine AG <0 (negative): adequate NH4+ excretion → non-renal cause (e.g., diarrhea)
• Urine AG >0 (positive): inadequate NH4+ excretion → renal cause (e.g., distal RTA Type I)

NH4+ (unmeasured cation) is excreted with Cl−; high NH4+ excretion → high urine Cl− → negative UAG.

In diarrhea, the kidney appropriately increases NH4+ excretion to compensate for HCO3- loss, leading to high urine Cl- and negative UAG. In distal RTA, the kidney cannot secrete H+ → cannot produce NH4+ → urine Cl- is low → positive UAG. This is the key test to distinguish these two common causes.

**Q:** Compare the four types of renal tubular acidosis (RTA).

**A:** Type I (Distal RTA):
• Defect: inability to secrete H+ in collecting duct
• Result: impaired NH4+ excretion, very low urine pH (<5.5 cannot be achieved)
• Causes: Sjögren, lupus, amphotericin B, obstructive uropathy

Type II (Proximal RTA):
• Defect: impaired HCO3- reabsorption in proximal tubule
• Result: urinary HCO3- loss until new lower threshold reached
• Associated with Fanconi syndrome

Type III: Combination of I and II — extremely rare

Type IV (Hyperkalemic RTA):
• Defect: reduced ammoniagenesis due to hyperkalemia (from aldosterone deficiency/resistance)
• Most common RTA; associated with diabetic nephropathy, hyporenin-hypoaldosteronism

Type IV is the most clinically common, presenting as hyperkalemia + metabolic acidosis with normal GFR (e.g., diabetic nephropathy on ACEi). Type I is associated with nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis (alkaline urine promotes calcium phosphate stone formation). Type II is associated with Fanconi syndrome (global proximal tubule dysfunction).

**Q:** What are the specific treatments for toxic alcohol poisoning (methanol and ethylene glycol)?

**A:** Both:
• Fomepizole (alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor) — blocks conversion to toxic metabolites
• Dialysis (often required, especially if metabolic acidosis or symptoms of toxicity present)

Methanol:
• Folate supplementation (enhances formate metabolism)
• Metabolized to formic acid → toxic to optic nerve (blindness) and brain

Ethylene glycol:
• Pyridoxine + thiamine supplementation (enhance oxalate metabolism)
• Metabolized to oxalic acid → oxalate crystals in urine (envelope-shaped), nephrotoxicity

Fomepizole is the antidote of choice for both because it blocks alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing formation of toxic metabolites (formate from methanol; oxalate and other acids from ethylene glycol). Ethanol was the historical antidote but fomepizole is preferred. Calcium oxalate crystals in urine are pathognomonic for ethylene glycol.

**Q:** When is sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) indicated in metabolic acidosis and what are its risks?

**A:** Indications:
• Severe reduction in [HCO3-] (e.g., <8 mmol/L) especially with pH <7.0
• No metabolizable anion (salicylate, formate, oxalate, sulphate) — note: lactate and ketoacid anions can be metabolized to HCO3-

Risks:
• Hypokalemia (K+ shifts into cells — correct K+ first)
• ECF volume overload (Na+ load → pulmonary edema)
• Overshoot alkalosis (partial conversion of organic anions to HCO3- + persisting hyperventilation)

NaHCO3 is NOT routinely given for lactic acidosis or DKA because as the underlying cause is treated, lactate and ketone anions are metabolized to HCO3-, often causing overshoot alkalosis. NaHCO3 is most appropriate when there is no metabolizable anion (e.g., uremic acidosis, RTA, salicylate poisoning via alkaline diuresis).

**Q:** What characterizes metabolic alkalosis and what are its main causes?

**A:** Metabolic alkalosis: pH >7.45 + increased [HCO3-]

Requires: precipitating event AND maintenance factors.

Precipitating causes (CLEVERPD):
• Chloride loss (vomiting, NG suction)
• Licorice (chronic ingestion)
• Endocrine: Cushing's/Conn's syndrome, Bartter's syndrome
• Volume contraction (contraction alkalosis)
• Exogenous alkali (NaHCO3, milk-alkali syndrome)
• Refeeding
• Post-hypercapnia
• Diuretics (loop/thiazide)

Maintenance factors: volume depletion, hypokalemia, hyperaldosteronism

The kidney is capable of excreting large amounts of bicarbonate, so persistent alkalosis requires a maintenance factor that impairs HCO3- excretion. Volume depletion drives proximal HCO3- reabsorption. Hypokalemia stimulates ammoniagenesis and HCO3- generation. Without these, alkalosis self-corrects.

**Q:** How is metabolic alkalosis classified by urine chloride, and how does this guide treatment?

**A:** Saline-sensitive (most common):
• Urine Cl− <20 mEq/L
• Features: ECF volume contraction, hypokalemia
• Causes: vomiting, NG suction, diuretics (remote use)
• Treatment: IV 0.9% NaCl + replenish K+/Mg2+; carbonic anhydrase inhibitor (acetazolamide) to increase urinary HCO3- loss (ensure K+ is normal first)

Saline-resistant:
• Urine Cl− >20 mEq/L
• Features: ECF expansion, hypertension, increased mineralocorticoids
• Causes: primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's), Cushing's, Bartter's syndrome
• Treatment: remove source of aldosterone/glucocorticoid + spironolactone

Urine chloride <20 mEq/L indicates the kidney is avidly reabsorbing chloride (and bicarbonate) due to volume depletion — responds to saline. Urine Cl- >20 in the setting of alkalosis indicates an ongoing mineralocorticoid excess — saline will not help and may worsen hypertension.

**Q:** What is the expected respiratory compensation for metabolic alkalosis?

**A:** Hypoventilation to retain CO2.

Expected: arterial pCO2 rises by 0.7 mmHg for each 1 mmol/L rise in [HCO3-]

Important limitation: an upper limit to compensation exists — the brain's hypoxic drive prevents complete apnea, so pCO2 rarely rises above ~55-60 mmHg in compensation.

If pCO2 does not rise appropriately, a concurrent respiratory alkalosis is present. If pCO2 rises more than expected, concurrent respiratory acidosis exists. The upper limit of compensation prevents life-threatening hypoxemia from hypoventilation.

**Q:** Define polyuria and distinguish water diuresis from solute diuresis.

**A:** Polyuria: urine output >3 L/day in adults (or >2 L/m2 in children).
Distinguish from urinary frequency (multiple voids but total volume <3 L/day).

Water diuresis:
• Diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic)
• Primary polydipsia
• Drugs (diuretics)

Solute diuresis:
• Glucose (uncontrolled diabetes mellitus)
• Salt (high sodium intake, IV fluids)
• Urea (resolution of azotemia, high protein intake, tissue breakdown)

The distinction between water and solute diuresis guides workup. Urine osmolality helps: in water diuresis, urine is dilute (Uosm <300 mOsm/kg); in solute diuresis, urine osmolality may be isotonic (~300 mOsm/kg). Glucosuria in uncontrolled DM is the most common cause of solute diuresis.

**Q:** What is the delta-delta ratio in metabolic acidosis and how is it interpreted?

**A:** Delta-delta = (Increase in AG above normal) / (Decrease in HCO3- below normal)

• If increase in AG < decrease in HCO3-: co-existing normal AG metabolic acidosis
• If increase in AG > decrease in HCO3-: co-existing metabolic alkalosis
• If equal: pure high AG metabolic acidosis

In a pure high AG metabolic acidosis, each mEq/L rise in AG should correspond to a 1 mEq/L fall in HCO3- (1:1 ratio). If HCO3- is lower than expected by the AG rise alone, there is an additional acid source without an anion (normal AG acidosis). If HCO3- is higher than expected, something is also generating alkali (metabolic alkalosis).

**Q:** What is the approach to acid-base analysis (5 steps)?

**A:** 1. Identify primary disturbance (pH, HCO3-, pCO2)
2. Evaluate compensation (check if appropriate — inappropriate = second disorder)
3. Calculate plasma anion gap = [Na+] − ([Cl−] + [HCO3−]); correct for albumin
4. Calculate osmolar gap = measured − calculated osmolality (if AG elevated, consider toxic alcohols)
5. If AG elevated, calculate delta-delta: compare rise in AG vs. fall in HCO3- to identify co-existing disorders

A systematic approach prevents missing mixed disorders. Compensation always occurs in the same direction as the primary disturbance (e.g., metabolic acidosis → low pCO2; metabolic alkalosis → high pCO2). Overcompensation never occurs — if pH normalizes, a mixed disorder exists.

**Q:** What are the factors that increase renal potassium excretion?

**A:** 1. Hyperkalemia itself
2. Increased distal tubular urine flow rate and Na+ delivery (thiazides and loop diuretics increase K+ loss)
3. Elevated aldosterone → activates ENaC in cortical collecting duct → Na+ reabsorption → K+ excretion
4. Metabolic alkalosis (increases K+ secretion)
5. Hypomagnesemia (promotes renal K+ wasting)
6. Increased non-resorbable anions in tubule lumen (HCO3-, penicillin, salicylate) → increased tubular flow → more K+ secretion

Understanding these factors explains why vomiting (metabolic alkalosis + volume depletion activating RAAS), loop diuretics, and hypomagnesemia all cause renal K+ wasting. Non-resorbable anions act like diuretics by increasing luminal electronegativity and flow rate, driving K+ secretion.

**Q:** What are the three key steps in treating asymptomatic hyponatremia?

**A:** 1. Water restriction to <1 L/day
2. Consider IV 0.9% NS + furosemide (reduces urine osmolality and augments free water excretion)
3. Consider NaCl tablets as oral sodium source

For refractory cases:
• Furosemide + oral salt tablets
• Oral urea (osmotic aquaresis)
• Vasopressin receptor 2 antagonists (vaptans, e.g., tolvaptan) — most effective for SIADH

Note: If patient is volume-expanded, avoid NaCl; if volume-depleted, avoid furosemide.

Tolvaptan (a V2 receptor antagonist/vaptan) is the most specific treatment for SIADH, causing electrolyte-free water excretion (aquaresis). However, it carries a risk of overly rapid sodium correction if fluid restriction is maintained, and has hepatotoxicity concerns limiting use to specialist settings.

**Q:** How does salicylate (ASA) overdose cause a mixed acid-base disorder?

**A:** Salicylate causes BOTH:
1. Respiratory alkalosis: direct stimulation of respiratory center → hyperventilation
2. High AG metabolic acidosis:
   - Salicylic acid itself contributes to AG
   - Lactic acidosis (salicylate impairs electron transport chain at toxic levels)
   - Ketoacidosis (salicylate activates fat breakdown)

Net result: often pH close to normal (mixed disorder), but both disorders present simultaneously.

Treatment: alkaline diuresis (sodium bicarbonate IV to alkalinize urine and trap ionized salicylate) + dialysis for severe cases.

Salicylate poisoning is a classic MCCQE1 scenario for mixed acid-base disorders. In adults, metabolic acidosis predominates; in children, respiratory alkalosis may predominate early. Alkalinization of urine to pH >7.5 significantly increases renal clearance of salicylate (ion trapping).

**Q:** What are the insulin, B2-agonist, and calcium gluconate mechanisms in treating hyperkalemia?

**A:** Calcium gluconate:
• Antagonizes hyperkalemia-induced membrane depolarization
• Raises action potential threshold → protects cardiac conduction
• Does NOT change serum [K+]; onset: minutes; duration: 30-60 min

Insulin (regular 10 units IV + D50W):
• Stimulates Na/K-ATPase → drives K+ into cells
• Lowers K+ by ~0.8 mmol/L at 1h; onset: 15-30 min; duration: 4-6h

Salbutamol (B2-agonist, 10-20 mg nebulized):
• Stimulates Na/K-ATPase → drives K+ into cells
• Onset: 30-90 min; additive to insulin effect

Calcium gluconate is the first-line emergency agent when ECG changes are present, given its rapid cardiac protection. Insulin and B2-agonists provide temporary cellular shift. All three are temporizing — definitive K+ removal requires furosemide, cation exchangers, or dialysis.

**Q:** What symptoms are associated with hypocalcemia (secondary to hyperphosphatemia)?

**A:** • Tetany
• Seizures
• Hypotension
• QT prolongation (cardiac arrhythmias)
• Papilledema
• Psychiatric manifestations

Classic signs: Chvostek sign (facial twitch with tapping CN VII) and Trousseau sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation).

Hyperphosphatemia causes hypocalcemia by: 1) direct calcium phosphate precipitation in blood/tissues, and 2) stimulating PTH secretion (secondary hyperparathyroidism in CKD). QT prolongation from hypocalcemia can cause life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. Papilledema is a rare but MCCQE1-testable manifestation.

**Q:** What are the causes of transcellular K+ shift causing hyperkalemia?

**A:** • Intravascular hemolysis
• Rhabdomyolysis
• Tumour lysis syndrome
• Insulin deficiency (insulin normally drives K+ into cells)
• Hyperglycemia (osmotic shift of water out of cells carries K+)
• Metabolic acidosis due to mineral acids (H+ enters cells, K+ exits)
• Beta-blockers (block β2-mediated Na/K-ATPase stimulation)
• Digitalis overdose (blocks Na/K-ATPase)
• Succinylcholine (depolarizing NMJ blocker → massive K+ release from muscle)

Note: Lactic and ketoacidosis generally do NOT cause hyperkalemia.

Succinylcholine is a particularly important cause of hyperkalemia in certain clinical settings (burns, denervation injuries, prolonged immobility) where it can cause massive K+ release and cardiac arrest. Digitalis toxicity blocks the Na/K-ATPase, preventing K+ re-entry into cells.

**Q:** What are the absolute indications for initiating dialysis in chronic kidney disease?

**A:** Volume overload (refractory), hyperkalemia (refractory), severe metabolic acidosis (refractory), neurologic signs/symptoms of uremia (encephalopathy, neuropathy, seizures), uremic pericarditis, refractory accelerated HTN, clinically significant bleeding diathesis, persistent severe nausea/vomiting.

Per KDIGO 2024 guidelines, absolute indications reflect life-threatening complications of uremia that cannot be managed medically. These are distinguished from relative indications (anorexia, fatigue, pruritus, restless legs, cognitive decline) which guide individualized decision-making. Dialysis is generally initiated when GFR reaches <10 mL/min.

**Q:** What are the relative (vs. absolute) indications for initiating dialysis?

**A:** Relative indications: anorexia and dysgeusia, decreased cognitive functioning, profound fatigue and weakness, severe anemia unresponsive to erythropoietin, persistent severe pruritus, restless leg syndrome, progressive deterioration in nutritional status refractory to dietary intervention.

Relative indications guide individualized decision-making alongside eGFR trajectory and patient preferences. KDIGO 2024 emphasizes symptom-driven initiation rather than a fixed eGFR threshold, though dialysis is generally started when GFR is approximately <10 mL/min.

**Q:** A patient with CKD stage 5 has refractory hyperkalemia and uremic encephalopathy. Using the AEIOU mnemonic, list the indications for emergency dialysis.

**A:** AEIOU: Acidosis (severe metabolic), Electrolyte imbalance (refractory hyperkalemia), Intoxication (dialyzable toxins), Overload (volume — refractory pulmonary edema), Uremia (pericarditis, encephalopathy; urea >35–50 mM).

The AEIOU mnemonic provides a bedside framework for identifying when urgent/emergent dialysis is required. In this scenario, both hyperkalemia (E) and encephalopathy/uremia (U) are present. Uremic pericarditis is a classic absolute indication given the risk of tamponade.

**Q:** At what eGFR should patients with CKD be referred for vascular access surgery for AV fistula creation?

**A:** When eGFR is <15–20 mL/min, or within 1 year of anticipated need for hemodialysis.

AV fistulas (surgically connecting artery to vein) are the preferred vascular access for hemodialysis because they have lower infection and thrombosis rates compared to AV grafts or central venous catheters. Early referral allows maturation time (typically several months) before dialysis is needed.

**Q:** Compare peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis regarding rate, mechanism of ultrafiltration, and membrane used.

**A:** PD: slow rate; osmotic pressure drives ultrafiltration; peritoneum is the membrane. HD: fast rate; hydrostatic pressure drives ultrafiltration; semi-permeable artificial membrane (e.g., polysulfone).

Both modalities use concentration gradient and convection for solute removal. PD uses an indwelling peritoneal catheter and glucose-based dialysate; HD uses extracorporeal blood circuit through a dialysis machine. PD allows greater independence and is preferred when residual renal function is present.

**Q:** What are the major complications of peritoneal dialysis compared to hemodialysis?

**A:** PD complications: bacterial peritonitis, infection at catheter site, metabolic effects of glucose absorption, encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (rare). HD complications: central venous stenosis/thrombosis, bacteremia (catheter-related), bleeding (due to heparin), hemodynamic stress of extracorporeal circuit, disequilibrium syndrome.

Disequilibrium syndrome (headache, cerebral edema, hypotension, nausea, muscle cramps) occurs during early HD sessions due to rapid solute/water flux. Bacterial peritonitis is the most feared complication of PD and is treated with intraperitoneal antibiotics. PD is preferred in patients with hemodynamic instability or residual renal function.

**Q:** What are the two available forms of peritoneal dialysis and how do they differ?

**A:** CAPD (continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis): 2–5 manual exchanges per day, performed by patient. Automated PD (APD): uses a cycler machine at night while patient sleeps.

Both CAPD and APD allow dialysis at home, offering greater patient independence than in-centre HD. Advantages of PD include fewer dietary restrictions, better rehabilitation rates, and preservation of residual renal function. Home hemodialysis is also possible and allows nocturnal dialysis (3–7 nights/week, 4–8 hours/session).

**Q:** When is peritoneal dialysis preferred over hemodialysis, and when is hemodialysis preferred over PD?

**A:** PD preferred: residual renal function present, patient preference for home therapy. HD preferred: intraperitoneal adhesions present, home conditions non-conducive, hemodynamic instability.

Residual renal function (RRF) is better preserved with PD than HD. Intraperitoneal adhesions from prior surgery or peritonitis contraindicate PD. Hemodynamically unstable patients (e.g., sepsis, heart failure) may not tolerate PD adequacy due to impaired peritoneal perfusion.

**Q:** What is the preferred renal replacement therapy for ESRD, and what immunosuppression protocol is used post-transplant?

**A:** Renal transplantation is preferred for all suitable patients. Immunosuppression: induction with IV thymoglobulin or basiliximab, followed by maintenance oral regimen (corticosteroids + calcineurin inhibitor [cyclosporine or tacrolimus] + anti-metabolite [mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine]).

Renal transplant provides maximum GFR replacement and best reverses uremic signs/symptoms. The 1-year allograft survival is ≥90%. Living donor transplants have better short- and long-term outcomes than deceased donor transplants. Native kidneys are usually left in situ; the transplanted kidney is placed in the iliac fossa with renal artery anastomosed to the external iliac artery.

**Q:** What is the leading cause of mortality in renal transplant recipients, and what are the major long-term complications?

**A:** Leading cause of mortality: cardiovascular disease. Major complications: infections (bacterial, viral, fungal, opportunistic — especially CMV, PJP between 1–6 months post-transplant), new-onset DM (from prednisone/tacrolimus), graft rejection, BK virus nephropathy, malignancy (skin cancer, Kaposi's sarcoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma), cyclosporine/tacrolimus nephropathy.

Immunosuppression increases infection and malignancy risk. CMV and PJP prophylaxis is standard in the first 6 months. BK virus (polyomavirus) reactivates under over-immunosuppression and can cause graft loss. Late allograft failure is primarily due to interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy and death with a functioning graft.

**Q:** A renal transplant recipient presents 3 months post-transplant with rising creatinine, low-grade fever, and graft site tenderness. What is the most likely diagnosis and what causes late allograft loss?

**A:** Most likely: acute rejection (cellular or humoral). Note: symptoms are often absent/subtle. Late allograft loss is caused by: interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy, death with functioning graft, and factors including HTN, hyperlipidemia, donor age, new-onset DM, and chronic antibody-mediated injury (transplant glomerulopathy).

Acute rejection presents with rising Cr, fever, hematuria, and graft tenderness, though many cases are asymptomatic and detected on protocol biopsy. Early allograft damage from acute rejection and peritransplant injuries contributes to chronic allograft nephropathy. Long-term monitoring of calcineurin inhibitor levels (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) is essential.

**Q:** What are the mechanisms of action and major adverse effects of loop diuretics?

**A:** Mechanism: inhibit Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ transporter in thick ascending limb of Loop of Henle; cause renal and peripheral vasodilation. Major adverse effects: hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, hypercalciuria (with stone formation), volume depletion with metabolic alkalosis, gout precipitation, allergy in sulfa-sensitive individuals.

Examples: furosemide, bumetanide, ethacrynic acid, torsemide. IV furosemide is equivalent to 2× the oral dose. Used for edema (CHF, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis), SIADH-induced hyponatremia (free water clearance), and HTN. Less effective for HTN due to short action. Ethacrynic acid is the only loop diuretic safe in sulfa allergy.

**Q:** What are the clinical indications, mechanism, and key adverse effects of thiazide diuretics?

**A:** Mechanism: inhibit Na⁺/Cl⁻ transporter in distal convoluted tubule. Indications: first-line essential HTN, edema, idiopathic hypercalciuria/nephrolithiasis, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Adverse effects: hypokalemia, elevated serum urate/gout precipitation, hypercalcemia, elevated lipids, glucose intolerance.

Thiazides cause Ca²⁺ retention (unlike loop diuretics), making them useful for hypercalciuria and calcium stones. They paradoxically reduce urine volume in nephrogenic DI by causing mild volume depletion, increasing proximal tubular reabsorption. Examples: HCTZ, chlorthalidone (longer-acting, preferred for CV risk reduction), indapamide, metolazone.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of potassium-sparing diuretics, and what is their major adverse effect?

**A:** Mechanism: spironolactone is an aldosterone antagonist; triamterene and amiloride block Na⁺ channels in cortical collecting duct. Major adverse effect: hyperkalemia (caution with ACEIs). Additional: triamterene can cause nephrotoxicity and nephrolithiasis; spironolactone causes gynecomastia (estrogenic effect).

Potassium-sparing diuretics reduce K⁺ loss caused by other diuretics. Spironolactone is specifically used for severe CHF, ascites, and hyperaldosteronism. Amiloride is used in cystic fibrosis to decrease viscosity of secretions. Combining with ACEIs/ARBs significantly increases hyperkalemia risk.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of osmotic diuretics and what are their main indications?

**A:** Mechanism: non-reabsorbable solutes (mannitol, glycerol, urea) increase osmotic pressure of glomerular filtrate in renal tubules, inhibiting water reabsorption and increasing urinary excretion of toxic material. Indications: reduce intracranial or intraocular pressure, mobilize excess fluid in renal failure or edematous states.

Mannitol (0.25–2 g/kg IV over 30–60 min) is the prototypical osmotic diuretic used in neurosurgery and traumatic brain injury for ICP management. A transient volume expansion occurs before diuresis, which is a key adverse effect. Electrolyte abnormalities (↓Na, ↓K) also occur.

**Q:** What are the renal indications, mechanism, and major adverse effects of ACE inhibitors in nephrology?

**A:** Indications: HTN, renoprotection (diabetic and non-diabetic nephropathy), cardioprotection. Mechanism: inhibit ACE → prevent angiotensin II formation → vasodilation + reduced aldosterone → ↓Na/H₂O retention → ↓BP. Also reduce fibrosis and atherogenesis. Adverse effects: cough (most common), angioedema, hyperkalemia, AKI, teratogenicity, agranulocytosis (captopril).

ACEIs reduce efferent arteriolar resistance, lowering intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria — independent of BP lowering. BENEDICT trial: ACEIs (trandolapril) prevent microalbuminuria in T2DM + HTN. REIN trial: ramipril is renoprotective in non-diabetic nephropathy with non-nephrotic proteinuria. ACEIs are teratogenic (contraindicated in pregnancy).

**Q:** How do ARBs compare to ACEIs in mechanism and clinical use in nephrology, and what are their key adverse effects?

**A:** ARBs: competitive antagonists at angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor. Similar indications to ACEIs: HTN, renoprotection, cardioprotection. Key advantage: no ACE-inhibitor cough. Adverse effects: hyperkalemia, AKI, teratogenicity; reduce dose in hepatic impairment. Examples: losartan, candesartan, irbesartan, valsartan, telmisartan.

DETAIL trial: ARB telmisartan = ACEI enalapril in slowing renal deterioration in T2DM. IDNT trial: irbesartan reduced ESRD by 23% vs. placebo and amlodipine in T2DM nephropathy independently of BP. RENAAL trial: losartan reduced ESRD by 28%. VA-NEPHRON-D showed ACEI + ARB combination increases hyperkalemia and AKI without added benefit — combination is contraindicated.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of SGLT2 inhibitors and what are their renal and cardiovascular benefits supported by landmark trials?

**A:** Mechanism: inhibit SGLT2 in proximal tubules → increased glucose and sodium excretion in urine → osmotic diuresis, natriuresis, reduced BP, weight loss. Benefits: renoprotection and cardioprotection in T2DM and CKD. Key trials: CREDENCE (canagliflozin — 30% reduction in composite renal endpoint in T2DM CKD), DAPA-CKD (dapagliflozin — safe in CKD with and without T2DM), EMPA-KIDNEY (empagliflozin — reduced kidney disease progression in broad CKD population).

SGLT2i reduce intraglomerular hypertension by causing afferent arteriolar constriction via tubuloglomerular feedback (increased NaCl delivery to macula densa). Adverse effects: GU infections, euglycemic DKA (hold perioperatively), nausea, constipation. Canagliflozin has increased risk of lower limb amputation and fractures.

**Q:** What did the CREDENCE trial demonstrate regarding SGLT2 inhibitors in diabetic nephropathy?

**A:** Canagliflozin 100 mg daily reduced the composite primary endpoint (ESRD, doubling of serum Cr, or death from renal/CV causes) by 30% (HR 0.70) in T2DM patients with albuminuric CKD (eGFR 30–90, ACR >300–5000) on RAAS blockade. Also reduced CV death/MI/stroke (HR 0.85).

CREDENCE was the first dedicated renal outcomes trial for an SGLT2i and was stopped early due to efficacy. All patients were on background RAAS blockade (ACEI or ARB), confirming additive renoprotection. This established canagliflozin as a standard of care in T2DM with CKD.

**Q:** What did the DAPA-CKD trial demonstrate and why was it significant?

**A:** Dapagliflozin (10 mg daily) reduced the composite of ≥50% eGFR reduction, ESRD, or death from cardiorenal causes by 39% (HR 0.61) in patients with CKD (eGFR 25–75, ACR 200–5000), both with and without T2DM. Trial was stopped early for efficacy.

DAPA-CKD was significant because it extended SGLT2i benefits to non-diabetic CKD patients, establishing dapagliflozin as renoprotective regardless of diabetic status. NNT to prevent one primary outcome event was 19. This supports the use of SGLT2i in proteinuric CKD broadly.

**Q:** What did the EMPA-KIDNEY trial show about empagliflozin in CKD?

**A:** Empagliflozin 10 mg daily reduced kidney disease progression or cardiovascular death (13.1% vs. 16.9%) in a broad CKD population (eGFR as low as ~20 mL/min/1.73m², with or without T2DM). Fewer hospitalizations; no significant effect on heart failure or cardiovascular death individually. Serious adverse event rates were similar.

EMPA-KIDNEY enrolled the broadest CKD population of the SGLT2i trials, including patients with lower eGFR values and non-diabetic etiologies. This trial further solidified SGLT2i as a cornerstone therapy in CKD management, complementing RAAS blockade.

**Q:** What did the FLOW trial show about semaglutide in CKD with T2DM?

**A:** Semaglutide 1 mg weekly reduced major kidney disease events (kidney failure, significant eGFR reduction, or death from kidney/CV causes) by 24% in patients with T2DM and CKD. Also reduced major CV events and all-cause mortality. Serious adverse events less common with semaglutide (49.6% vs. 53.8%).

FLOW was a landmark trial establishing GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide) as renoprotective in T2DM + CKD, independent of glycemic control. This expands the treatment toolkit for diabetic nephropathy alongside RAAS blockade and SGLT2i.

**Q:** What did the FIDELIO-DKD trial show about finerenone in CKD + T2DM?

**A:** Finerenone (non-steroidal MRA) reduced the composite of kidney failure, ≥40% reduction in GFR, and death from renal causes by 18% (HR 0.82) vs. placebo in patients with T2DM and CKD. Hyperkalemia-related treatment discontinuation was more frequent with finerenone.

Finerenone is a selective, non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist with less hyperkalemia than spironolactone/eplerenone. FIDELIO-DKD established kidney and cardiovascular benefits in T2DM + CKD on background RAAS blockade, with finerenone now part of evidence-based CKD management.

**Q:** What was the key finding of the VA-NEPHRON-D trial regarding combination RAAS blockade?

**A:** Combining ACEIs (lisinopril) + ARBs (losartan) in T2DM with proteinuric nephropathy provided no significant benefit over ARB monotherapy on primary endpoint (eGFR decline, ESRD, or death), and significantly increased risk of hyperkalemia and AKI. The trial was stopped early for safety.

The VA-NEPHRON-D trial definitively showed that dual RAAS blockade (ACEI + ARB) is harmful — higher adverse events without additional renoprotective benefit. This is now a contraindicated combination in CKD management. Patients should receive ACEI or ARB monotherapy, not both.

**Q:** What were the conclusions of the IDEAL trial regarding timing of dialysis initiation in CKD?

**A:** Early initiation (eGFR 10–14) vs. late initiation (eGFR 5–7) of dialysis in progressive CKD showed no significant difference in all-cause mortality (37.6% vs. 36.6%; HR 1.04, P=0.75). No differences in frequency of adverse events.

IDEAL confirmed that symptom-driven, rather than eGFR-threshold-driven, dialysis initiation is appropriate. Earlier dialysis initiation did not improve survival or clinical outcomes, supporting the current KDIGO recommendation to initiate dialysis based on symptoms and clinical indicators rather than a fixed eGFR cutoff.

**Q:** What did the STARRT-AKI trial show about timing of renal replacement therapy in AKI?

**A:** Accelerated (early) vs. standard initiation of RRT in critically ill patients with severe AKI showed no significant difference in 90-day all-cause mortality. The accelerated strategy group had higher rates of RRT and adverse events, and survivors had greater dependence on RRT.

STARRT-AKI mirrors the IDEAL trial findings for CKD — earlier is not necessarily better for dialysis initiation. Unnecessary early RRT may expose patients to procedure-related risks and may impede native kidney recovery. Standard (watchful waiting) approach is preferred in AKI unless absolute indications are met.

**Q:** What did the CONVINCE trial show about hemodiafiltration vs. hemodialysis?

**A:** High-dose hemodiafiltration reduced all-cause mortality compared to conventional high-flux hemodialysis (17.3% vs. 21.9%) in patients with kidney failure over a median 30-month follow-up.

Hemodiafiltration (HDF) combines diffusion (like HD) with high-volume convection, removing a broader range of middle molecules. The CONVINCE trial suggests high-dose HDF provides a survival benefit over standard HD, potentially making HDF the preferred modality in eligible patients. The mean convection volume was 25.3 L/session.

**Q:** What did the FHN trial demonstrate about hemodialysis frequency?

**A:** Frequent hemodialysis (6×/week) vs. conventional (3×/week) was associated with significant benefits in left ventricular mass reduction and physical health scores (primary outcomes), and improved control of HTN and hyperphosphatemia.

The FHN trial supports the benefit of increased HD frequency over conventional thrice-weekly dialysis for cardiovascular and physical outcomes. More frequent dialysis removes more fluid and solutes, reducing hemodynamic burden on the heart. Home nocturnal HD is one way to achieve this frequency.

**Q:** What did the HEMO trial show about dialysis dose and membrane flux?

**A:** High-dose dialysis vs. standard-dose dialysis, and high-flux vs. low-flux dialyzer membranes in thrice-weekly hemodialysis showed no significant differences in all-cause mortality. Neither higher dose nor higher flux improved survival.

The HEMO trial established that for conventional 3×/week HD, increasing dose (Kt/V) or switching to high-flux membranes beyond standard parameters does not further reduce mortality. This contrasts with the FHN trial which showed frequency (6×/week) does matter.

**Q:** What were the findings of the CHOIR and CREATE trials regarding hemoglobin targets in CKD anemia?

**A:** CHOIR: targeting Hb 13.5 g/dL (higher) vs. 11.3 g/dL with epoetin alfa resulted in MORE primary events (death, MI, CHF hospitalization, stroke; HR 1.34) — higher target is harmful. CREATE: complete Hb correction to 13–15 g/dL vs. 10.5–11.5 g/dL did not reduce CV events.

Both trials establish that targeting normal hemoglobin in CKD anemia with ESAs is harmful. Current practice targets Hb 10–12 g/dL (avoiding levels >13 g/dL with ESAs). The TREAT trial further showed darbepoetin alfa did not reduce death/CV/ESRD events and increased stroke risk. ESA use should be minimized and iron optimized first.

**Q:** What did the SHARP trial demonstrate about lipid-lowering therapy in CKD?

**A:** Simvastatin 20 mg + ezetimibe 10 mg daily vs. placebo in CKD patients with no prior MI reduced first major atherosclerotic events by 17% (11.3% vs. 13.4%), with significant reductions in non-hemorrhagic strokes and arterial revascularization.

SHARP was a landmark trial establishing that LDL lowering with statin/ezetimibe combination is beneficial in CKD. Statins do not improve survival in dialysis patients (AURORA trial — rosuvastatin showed no benefit in HD patients), but benefit pre-dialysis CKD patients. ALERT trial showed fluvastatin in renal transplant recipients reduced cardiac deaths/non-fatal MI but not overall major adverse cardiac events.

**Q:** What did the SPRINT trial demonstrate about blood pressure targets in high-risk non-diabetic patients?

**A:** Intensive BP target (<120 mmHg systolic) vs. standard (<140 mmHg) in high-risk non-diabetic patients reduced the composite of CV events and all-cause mortality, but the intensive group experienced higher rates of certain serious adverse events (hypotension, syncope, AKI, electrolyte abnormalities). Trial stopped early for benefit.

SPRINT enrolled 9,361 patients without DM. Results support intensive BP control in high-risk patients; however, this must be balanced against adverse effects. In CKD, KDIGO 2021 guidelines recommend BP target <120 mmHg systolic when tolerated, using standardized office measurement. The benefit must be weighed against risk of AKI and electrolyte disturbances.

**Q:** What did the TEMPO 3:4 and REPRISE trials demonstrate about tolvaptan in ADPKD?

**A:** TEMPO 3:4: tolvaptan slowed total kidney volume increase (2.8% vs. 5.5%/year) and improved composite clinical progression endpoints in ADPKD patients aged 18–50 with relatively preserved function. REPRISE: tolvaptan in later-stage ADPKD (eGFR 25–44) also showed slower eGFR decline (-2.34 vs. -3.61 mL/min/1.73m²) over 12 months. Both had higher discontinuation rates due to aquaresis and hepatic adverse events.

Tolvaptan is a vasopressin V2-receptor antagonist that reduces cyst growth by decreasing cAMP-driven fluid secretion. It is the only approved disease-modifying therapy for ADPKD in Canada. Monitoring for hepatotoxicity is required. It is contraindicated in patients who cannot sense or respond to thirst.

**Q:** What did the MAINRITSAN trial show about maintenance therapy in ANCA-associated vasculitis?

**A:** Rituximab (500 mg q6 months) was significantly superior to daily azathioprine for maintaining remission in ANCA-associated vasculitis at 28 months (major relapse: 5% vs. 29%; HR 6.61). Serious adverse events were comparable.

ANCA-associated vasculitis (GPA, MPA) causes rapidly progressive GN and requires aggressive initial induction (cyclophosphamide or rituximab + steroids) followed by maintenance therapy. MAINRITSAN established rituximab as the preferred maintenance agent. PEXIVAS showed plasma exchange does NOT reduce death or ESRD in severe ANCA vasculitis, and reduced-dose glucocorticoids are non-inferior to standard dose.

**Q:** What did the PEXIVAS trial show about plasma exchange and glucocorticoid dosing in ANCA-associated vasculitis?

**A:** Plasma exchange did NOT reduce death or ESRD compared to no plasma exchange in severe ANCA-associated vasculitis (28.4% vs. 31.0%; no significant difference). Reduced-dose glucocorticoid regimen was non-inferior to standard dose and resulted in fewer serious infections.

PEXIVAS definitively ended routine use of plasma exchange for severe ANCA vasculitis, which was previously a common practice. Additionally, reduced-dose glucocorticoids achieve equivalent disease control with less infectious morbidity. These results have substantially changed treatment protocols for ANCA vasculitis.

**Q:** What did the AURORA1 and BLISS-LN trials demonstrate about novel agents for lupus nephritis?

**A:** AURORA1: voclosporin (calcineurin inhibitor) + standard therapy achieved complete renal response in 41% vs. 23% with placebo (OR 2.65) at 52 weeks. BLISS-LN: belimumab (anti-BLyS biologic) + standard therapy achieved primary efficacy renal response in 43% vs. 32% with placebo at 104 weeks, with lower risk of renal events or death (HR 0.51).

Both trials used standard therapy (MMF or cyclophosphamide-azathioprine) as background. Voclosporin is a novel calcineurin inhibitor with favorable dosing (no TDM required). Belimumab targets B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS), reducing autoantibody production. These agents represent advances in lupus nephritis management beyond traditional immunosuppression.

**Q:** What did the CORAL trial show about renal artery stenting for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis?

**A:** Renal artery stenting + medical therapy vs. medical therapy alone for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis showed NO significant difference in prevention of adverse CV or renal events (35.1% vs. 35.8%; HR 0.94, P=0.58). Stenting provided no additional benefit.

CORAL definitively showed that renal artery stenting does not add benefit over optimal medical therapy in atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis. Medical management (RAAS blockade, statins, antiplatelets, smoking cessation) remains the standard of care. Revascularization is reserved for select cases: rapidly worsening renal function, flash pulmonary edema, or bilateral RAS.

**Q:** What did the BICAR-ICU trial show about sodium bicarbonate therapy in severe metabolic acidosis?

**A:** Sodium bicarbonate infusion in critically ill ICU patients with severe metabolic acidemia had no effect on the primary outcome (death + organ failure at day 28 in the overall population), but benefited those with acute kidney injury. Adverse events were more common in the bicarbonate group.

BICAR-ICU showed bicarbonate may benefit a specific subgroup (AKI patients with severe acidemia) but is not beneficial for all critically ill patients with metabolic acidosis. This informs individualized use of bicarbonate therapy in the ICU, rather than routine administration.

**Q:** What did the SALT trial show about tolvaptan for hyponatremia?

**A:** Tolvaptan (oral vasopressin V2-receptor antagonist) significantly increased serum Na⁺ concentrations in patients with euvolemic or hypervolemic hyponatremia during days 1–4 and at 30 days (P<0.001 for both). Side effects: dry mouth, thirst, increased urination.

Tolvaptan is a vaptane/aquaretic agent that selectively blocks ADH V2 receptors, causing free water excretion without sodium loss. It is indicated for SIADH-induced euvolemic hyponatremia and hypervolemic hyponatremia (CHF, cirrhosis). Rapid correction risk: overshoot correction can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome — must monitor carefully.

**Q:** What did the SMART trial demonstrate about IV fluid choice in the emergency department?

**A:** Balanced crystalloids (lactated Ringer's/Plasma-Lyte) vs. normal saline in non-critically ill adults: hospital-free days were similar, but incidence of major adverse kidney events within 30 days was LOWER with balanced crystalloids.

Normal saline causes hyperchloremic non-anion gap metabolic acidosis and may worsen renal outcomes due to renal vasoconstriction. Balanced crystalloids (lower chloride content) are now preferred for IV fluid therapy. The SMART trial was the non-critically ill complement to the SMART-ICU trial, both favoring balanced crystalloids.

**Q:** What did the OPAL-HK trial show about patiromer for hyperkalemia in CKD on RAAS inhibitors?

**A:** Patiromer (potassium binder) in CKD patients on RAAS inhibitors with serum K⁺ 5.1–6.5 mmol/L significantly reduced serum K⁺ over 4 weeks. In the randomized withdrawal phase, recurrence of hyperkalemia occurred in 60% of placebo vs. 15% of patiromer patients.

Patiromer is an oral cation exchange polymer that binds potassium in the GI tract. It enables continuation of RAAS inhibitors (which cause hyperkalemia but are renoprotective) in patients with CKD. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC) is another approved K⁺ binder. Both are now guideline-recommended for chronic hyperkalemia management in CKD on RAAS therapy.

**Q:** What did the BENEDICT trial demonstrate about prevention of microalbuminuria in T2DM?

**A:** In T2DM with HTN and normoalbuminuria, trandolapril (ACEI) alone or combined with verapamil (non-DHP CCB) significantly reduced development of persistent microalbuminuria (6% trandolapril, 5.7% combination) vs. placebo (10%) and verapamil alone (11.9%). ACEIs are superior to non-DHP CCBs alone.

BENEDICT established ACEIs as renoprotective in T2DM even before microalbuminuria develops, supporting their use for primary prevention of diabetic nephropathy in high-risk patients. Non-DHP CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem) have some antiproteinuric effects but are less effective than ACEIs. DHP CCBs (amlodipine) increase proteinuria and are not renoprotective.

**Q:** What are the CNS, hematologic, and endocrine complications of chronic kidney disease/uremia?

**A:** CNS: encephalopathy, decreased LOC, stupor, seizure. Hematologic: normocytic anemia (EPO deficiency), bleeding tendency (platelet dysfunction), increased infection risk. Endocrine: decreased testosterone/estrogen/progesterone, increased FSH/LH, insulin resistance (decreased insulin requirements), hypertriglyceridemia, accelerated atherogenesis.

Uremic toxins cause platelet dysfunction (adhesion/aggregation impaired), explaining the bleeding tendency in ESRD despite normal platelet counts. Decreased insulin requirements in diabetic patients approaching ESRD is an important clinical clue — insulin is renally cleared. Hypertriglyceridemia in CKD accelerates cardiovascular disease.

**Q:** What is renal osteodystrophy and how does CKD lead to secondary hyperparathyroidism?

**A:** CKD → decreased GFR → decreased Ca²⁺, increased PO₄³⁻, decreased 1,25-OH vitamin D3 (active) → secondary hyperparathyroidism → osteitis fibrosa cystica. Also: calciphylaxis (vascular Ca²⁺ deposition causing skin necrosis).

In CKD, the failing kidney cannot activate vitamin D (1α-hydroxylation) and cannot excrete phosphate. Low Ca²⁺ and low active vitamin D stimulate PTH secretion. Elevated PTH causes bone resorption (osteitis fibrosa cystica), pathological fractures, and cardiovascular calcification. Treatment includes phosphate binders, active vitamin D analogs, and calcimimetics (cinacalcet).

**Q:** What are the cardiovascular and dermatologic complications of CKD/ESRD?

**A:** Cardiovascular: cardiomyopathy, CHF, arrhythmia, pericarditis, accelerated atherosclerosis. Dermatologic: pruritus, ecchymosis, hematoma, calciphylaxis (vascular calcium deposition causing skin and subcutaneous tissue necrosis).

Uremic pericarditis is an absolute indication for dialysis (BUN typically >35–50 mM). Calciphylaxis is a severe complication of CKD-related calcium-phosphate dysregulation causing painful ischemic skin ulcers, typically on the thighs, abdomen, or buttocks. It carries high mortality. Treatment includes sodium thiosulfate, wound care, and correction of calcium-phosphate product.

**Q:** What did the EVOLVE trial show about cinacalcet in dialysis patients with secondary hyperparathyroidism?

**A:** Cinacalcet (calcimimetic) vs. placebo in hemodialysis patients with moderate-severe secondary hyperparathyroidism did NOT significantly reduce the composite of death, MI, hospitalization for unstable angina/HF, or peripheral vascular events (48.3% vs. 49.2%; HR 0.93, P=0.11).

Despite cinacalcet effectively lowering PTH and calcium-phosphate product, it did not translate to improved cardiovascular outcomes in the EVOLVE trial. Cinacalcet remains used for management of secondary hyperparathyroidism in dialysis (reducing PTH, Ca, PO₄), but its CV benefit is unproven. It can cause hypocalcemia and nausea.

**Q:** What did the CLiCK trial show about chlorthalidone in advanced CKD with poorly controlled HTN?

**A:** Chlorthalidone (thiazide-like diuretic) vs. placebo in stage 4 CKD with poorly controlled HTN reduced systolic BP by 11.0 vs. 0.5 mmHg at 12 weeks (P<0.001). Adverse events (hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, dizziness, hyperuricemia) were more frequent with chlorthalidone.

CLiCK challenged the dogma that thiazide diuretics are ineffective in advanced CKD (eGFR <30). Chlorthalidone retained antihypertensive efficacy even in stage 4 CKD, expanding treatment options for this difficult population. Adverse effects must be monitored closely. This supports the role of thiazide-like diuretics as adjunctive BP agents in advanced CKD.

**Q:** What does the Kidney Failure Risk Equation provide and when is it applied?

**A:** The Kidney Failure Risk Equation provides the 2-year and 5-year probability of treated kidney failure for patients with CKD stage 3–5. It helps guide discussions about dialysis planning and referral for vascular access surgery.

The Kidney Failure Risk Equation uses variables such as age, sex, eGFR, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio to predict risk of ESRD. It identifies patients who would benefit from early referral to nephrology, vascular access creation (AV fistula when eGFR <15–20), and renal transplant evaluation.

**Q:** What did the AASK trial show about ACE inhibitors in hypertensive nephrosclerosis?

**A:** Ramipril (ACEI) caused a 36% slower mean GFR decline vs. amlodipine (DHP CCB) in hypertensive renal disease (P=0.006). Ramipril also slowed proteinuria progression. No significant differences vs. metoprolol (beta-blocker).

AASK established ACEI as preferred antihypertensive agents in Black patients with hypertensive nephrosclerosis, who are at high risk of CKD progression. DHP CCBs (amlodipine) worsen proteinuria relative to ACEIs in this population, making them less preferred as first-line agents when proteinuria is present.

**Q:** What did the REIN trial show about ACE inhibitors in non-diabetic nephropathy?

**A:** Ramipril (ACEI) vs. conventional antihypertensive therapy in non-diabetic nephropathy with non-nephrotic proteinuria: ramipril significantly reduced progression to ESRD (RR 2.72) and to overt proteinuria (RR 2.40), independent of BP effects.

REIN (Ramipril Efficacy in Nephropathy) established renoprotective effects of ACEIs in non-diabetic proteinuric nephropathy. REIN-2 showed that intensified BP control (diastolic <130 vs. <90) with addition of a CCB to ACEI provided no further benefit for ESRD prevention in non-diabetic CKD on ACEI. Both trials support ACEI as cornerstone therapy in proteinuric CKD.

**Q:** What is the ELITE-SYMPHONY trial and what is its implication for renal transplant immunosuppression?

**A:** ELITE-SYMPHONY compared immunosuppressive regimens in renal transplant recipients: low-dose tacrolimus (with daclizumab induction + MMF + corticosteroids) achieved the highest mean GFR at 12 months (65.4 mL/min/1.73m²) compared to standard cyclosporine or low-dose cyclosporine/sirolimus regimens. Low-dose sirolimus had the most serious adverse events.

ELITE-SYMPHONY established low-dose tacrolimus-based immunosuppression as the preferred maintenance regimen post-renal transplant. Tacrolimus is more potent but more nephrotoxic than cyclosporine at standard doses; low-dose tacrolimus preserves renal function better. Sirolimus (mTOR inhibitor) avoids nephrotoxicity but has worse outcomes in this setting.

**Q:** What is disequilibrium syndrome in hemodialysis and when does it occur?

**A:** Disequilibrium syndrome: headache, cerebral edema, hypotension, nausea, muscle cramps related to solute/water flux — typically occurs during first few hemodialysis sessions at the start of ESRD treatment.

Rapid removal of urea and solutes from the blood creates an osmotic gradient where the brain temporarily has higher solute concentration, drawing water in and causing cerebral edema. Prevention includes shorter, slower initial HD sessions, using a higher sodium dialysate, and administering mannitol. It is distinct from MELAS or other neurologic emergencies.

**Q:** What is the preferred vascular access for hemodialysis and how is it created?

**A:** Preferred: AV fistula (native). Created by surgically connecting an artery (usually radial) to an adjacent vein (usually cephalic), which increases vein diameter and wall thickness, allowing easy cannulation. Hierarchy: AV fistula > AV graft > central venous catheter (last resort due to infection/thrombosis risk).

AV fistulas have the lowest complication rates (infection, stenosis, thrombosis) and longest longevity. They require 6–8 weeks to mature before use. AV grafts use synthetic material and mature faster but have higher thrombosis rates. Central venous catheters carry the highest infection risk (bacteremia) and cause central venous stenosis.

## Nephrology Vascular

**Q:** What are the classic signs and symptoms of acute renal artery occlusion (renal infarction)?

**A:** Fever, nausea/vomiting, flank pain; leukocytosis; markedly elevated LDH (>4x upper limit of normal with minimal AST/ALT elevation — strongly suggestive); acute onset HTN or sudden worsening of chronic HTN; renal dysfunction (elevated Cr if bilateral or solitary functioning kidney).

Renal infarction is an important, potentially reversible cause of renal failure. LDH elevation disproportionate to AST/ALT is a hallmark. HTN results from RAAS activation due to ischemia of the kidney.

**Q:** Which laboratory finding is 'strongly suggestive' of renal infarction?

**A:** LDH >4x the upper limit of normal with minimal elevations in AST/ALT.

The disproportionate rise in LDH compared to AST/ALT is characteristic of renal infarction because LDH is abundant in renal tissue. This pattern helps distinguish renal infarction from hepatic causes of enzyme elevation.

**Q:** What are the two main causes of renal artery stenosis and how do they differ?

**A:** 1. Atherosclerotic plaques (90%): affect proximal 1/3 of renal artery; males >55 yr, smokers. 2. Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD, 10%): affects distal 2/3 of renal artery or segmental branches; young females (typical onset <30 yr).

Atherosclerosis is by far the most common cause and is associated with cardiovascular risk factors. FMD is the classic cause in young women and typically involves the distal renal artery. Treatment strategies differ — FMD patients with confirmed disease should be considered for revascularization.

**Q:** A 65-year-old male smoker with known diffuse atherosclerosis presents with refractory hypertension and spontaneous hypokalemia. His creatinine rises after starting an ACEI. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Renal artery stenosis (ischemic renal disease / renovascular HTN) — likely atherosclerotic in etiology.

Classic features of renovascular HTN include severe/refractory HTN, spontaneous hypokalemia (from renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation), and paradoxical rise in Cr with ACEI/ARB (due to loss of efferent arteriolar constriction that was maintaining GFR). In a male >55 yr with atherosclerotic risk factors, atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis is the leading cause.

**Q:** What is the gold standard investigation for renal artery stenosis and what is the best non-invasive screening test?

**A:** Gold standard: renal arteriography (risk of contrast nephropathy). Best non-invasive screening: duplex Doppler ultrasound (assesses kidney size and blood flow; operator dependent). CT or MR angiography are effective non-invasive alternatives; avoid gadolinium if GFR <30 mL/min (risk of systemic dermal fibrosis).

Digital subtraction angiography and CT/MR angiography are effective to establish stenosis. For MR angiography, gadolinium contrast is avoided when GFR <30 mL/min due to risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis. The STAR trial showed stenting provided no benefit over medical therapy for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis.

**Q:** According to the STAR trial (NEJM 2014), what is the recommended initial treatment for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis with HTN or CKD?

**A:** Medical management is recommended as first-line. Percutaneous renal artery angioplasty and stenting offers no significant benefit over comprehensive medical therapy alone for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis.

The STAR (Stenting and Medical Therapy for Atherosclerotic Renal Artery Stenosis) trial showed no significant difference in cardiovascular events, renal outcomes, or mortality between stenting plus medical therapy vs. medical therapy alone. Stenting may be considered in select cases: uncontrolled HTN resistant to maximal pharmacotherapy, progressive renal function loss, or recurrent acute pulmonary edema with significant stenosis.

**Q:** What are the three major categories of causes of renal vein thrombosis?

**A:** 1. Endothelial damage: blunt trauma, tumour infiltration (RCC), vasculitis, renal transplant rejection. 2. Stasis: severe volume loss (GI fluid loss, hemorrhage, dehydration), renal vein compression. 3. Hypercoagulability: nephrotic syndrome (especially membranous nephropathy), sepsis, oral contraceptives, disseminated malignancy, intrinsic hypercoagulability, sickle cell disease.

Nephrotic syndrome — especially membranous nephropathy — is the classic association with renal vein thrombosis due to urinary loss of anticoagulant proteins (antithrombin III, protein C, protein S) and increased hepatic synthesis of procoagulant factors.

**Q:** What is the classic first presenting symptom of chronic renal vein thrombosis?

**A:** Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the typical first presenting symptom of chronic renal vein thrombosis, along with increasing proteinuria and/or tubular dysfunction.

Chronic renal vein thrombosis develops collateral circulation which prevents the acute signs (flank pain, hematuria, rising creatinine). Instead, the thrombus propagates and dislodges causing PE. Acute renal vein thrombosis presents with N/V, flank pain, hematuria, elevated LDH, rising Cr, and sudden increase in proteinuria.

**Q:** What is the treatment for renal vein thrombosis?

**A:** Anticoagulation therapy: initial IV heparin, followed by warfarin (target INR 2.5) within 3–10 days, continued for minimum 1 year. Select cases suitable for thrombectomy or thrombolysis (streptokinase, urokinase, tPA).

Anticoagulation aids recanalisation, improves renal function, and reduces thromboembolism risk. The minimum 1-year duration reflects the underlying thrombophilic state (often nephrotic syndrome). Thrombolysis is reserved for cases not responding to anticoagulation.

**Q:** What is atheroembolic renal disease and what are its characteristic laboratory/biopsy findings?

**A:** Progressive renal insufficiency from embolic obstruction of small- and medium-sized renal vessels by atheromatous emboli. Lab: eosinophilia, eosinophiluria, hypocomplementemia. Biopsy: needle-shaped cholesterol clefts (from tissue-processing artifacts) with surrounding tissue reaction in small/medium vessels.

Atheroembolic disease can occur spontaneously or following arterial manipulation (angiography, surgery, angioplasty). Anticoagulants and thrombolytics can worsen disease by interfering with ulcerated plaque healing. Prognosis is poor — at least one-third develop ESRD. There is no effective specific treatment.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of how renal artery stenosis causes secondary hypertension?

**A:** Decreased renal perfusion → increased renin release → increased angiotensin II production → 1) generalized arteriolar constriction (immediate BP rise) and 2) aldosterone release → Na⁺ and water retention (sustained BP elevation). GFR is maintained by angiotensin II-induced efferent arteriolar constriction which raises filtration fraction (GFR/RBF). Elevated BP leads to further kidney damage and worsening HTN.

This is renovascular HTN — the most common cause of secondary HTN (1-2% of all hypertensive patients). ACEI/ARB effectively lower BP by blocking angiotensin II and efferent arteriolar constriction. However, in bilateral RAS or unilateral RAS in a solitary kidney, ACEI/ARB can precipitate acute renal failure by removing the efferent tone that was maintaining GFR.

## Nephrology Tma

**Q:** What enzyme is reduced in TTP and what autoantibodies help diagnose it?

**A:** ADAMTS13 (a metalloprotease that cleaves von Willebrand factor multimers) is reduced in TTP. ADAMTS13 autoantibodies are useful for diagnosing TTP.

In TTP, autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 prevent cleavage of ultra-large vWF multimers, leading to platelet microthrombi. HUS is more commonly associated with Shiga toxin-producing E. coli and features more renal involvement (fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillary loops and arterioles), while TTP is more neurologically predominant.

**Q:** What is the treatment for TTP-HUS and which agent targets complement-mediated HUS?

**A:** TTP-HUS: plasma exchange plus corticosteroids. Complement-mediated HUS (atypical HUS): eculizumab (monoclonal antibody against complement C5). Avoid platelet transfusions and ASA in TTP-HUS.

Plasma exchange removes ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and replaces ADAMTS13 enzyme in TTP. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement activation and is used specifically for complement-mediated (atypical) HUS. Platelet transfusions are contraindicated in TTP as they 'fuel the fire' of microthrombus formation.

## Nephrology Transplant

**Q:** What are the two mechanisms of calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine/tacrolimus) nephrotoxicity?

**A:** 1. Acute (reversible): afferent and efferent arteriolar vasoconstriction → decreased GFR → prerenal azotemia + tubular vacuolization. Treatment: calcium channel blockers or prostaglandin analogs, reduce/switch immunosuppressive drug. 2. Chronic (largely irreversible): obliterative arteriolopathy → interstitial nephritis and CKD (striped fibrosis pattern).

Calcineurin inhibitors are a major cause of kidney failure in other solid organ transplants (e.g., heart). The ELITE-Symphony trial showed tacrolimus-based regimens had superior renal outcomes vs. cyclosporine or sirolimus regimens. Low-dose tacrolimus is now preferred for renal transplantation.

## Nephrology Analgesic

**Q:** What is the mechanism of NSAID-induced vasomotor AKI and which populations are most at risk?

**A:** NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase, preventing prostaglandin synthesis. Normally, prostaglandins vasodilate the afferent arteriole to maintain renal blood flow. Without prostaglandins, afferent vasoconstriction causes renal ischemia → prerenal azotemia. High-risk: elderly, underlying renal disease, hypovolemia (diuretics, CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome).

In states of reduced renal perfusion, the kidney relies on prostaglandin-mediated afferent dilation to maintain GFR. NSAIDs remove this compensatory mechanism, precipitating AKI. Patients typically develop prerenal azotemia within days of NSAID initiation. Treatment is discontinuation of the NSAID; dialysis is rarely needed.

**Q:** What is analgesic chronic interstitial nephritis and what are its classic features?

**A:** Chronic interstitial nephritis due to excessive consumption of antipyretics (phenacetin or acetaminophen) combined with NSAIDs. Features: papillary necrosis (gross hematuria, flank pain, declining renal function), calyceal filling defect on IVP ('ring sign'), increased risk of transitional cell carcinoma of the renal pelvis. Good prognosis if analgesics discontinued.

Phenacetin is the classic analgesic associated with nephropathy and urothelial malignancy. The 'ring sign' on IVP represents a sloughed papilla still in its calyx. These patients often have emotional stress, psychiatric symptoms, and GI disturbance. The combination of an analgesic with an NSAID is more nephrotoxic than either alone.

**Q:** What are the metabolic effects of NSAIDs on the kidney beyond AKI?

**A:** 1. Sodium retention (secondary to reduced GFR). 2. Hyperkalemia and HTN (secondary to hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism). 3. Excess water retention (loss of prostaglandin-mediated antagonism of ADH action).

NSAIDs reduce renin secretion by inhibiting prostaglandins, causing hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism and hyperkalemia. This is clinically important in patients already at risk for hyperkalemia (CKD, on ACEIs/ARBs, diabetic). NSAIDs also blunt the natriuretic and diuretic effects of loop diuretics.

## Nephrology Diabetic

**Q:** What is the most common cause of end-stage renal disease in North America?

**A:** Diabetic nephropathy (diabetic progressive glomerulosclerosis) is the most common cause of ESRD in North America.

Approximately 50% of patients with diabetes will develop nephropathy. Diabetic nephropathy is a slow, progressive increase in albuminuria followed later by a decrease in eGFR. It is associated with HTN and retinopathy (especially type 1 DM) and neuropathy (especially type 2 DM). Indigenous Canadians with diabetes have 2.59 times higher risk of ESRD than non-Indigenous people with diabetes.

**Q:** What are the four stages of diabetic progressive glomerulosclerosis (Mogensen stages)?

**A:** Stage 1: Increased GFR (120-150%, compensatory hyperfiltration), normal albumin. Stage 2: No detectable albuminuria, slight increase in mesangial matrix. Stage 3 (incipient nephropathy): A2 albuminuria (30-300 mg/24h; ACR 2.0-20 mg/mmol), normal GFR. Stage 4 (overt nephropathy): A3 albuminuria (>300 mg/24h; ACR >20 mg/mmol), declining GFR, sclerosed glomeruli (<20% of filtration surface area).

Once A3 albuminuria is established, 50% of patients reach ESRD within 7-10 years. The classic pathological lesion is Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodular glomerulosclerosis (15-20%); more common is diffuse glomerulosclerosis with uniform increase in mesangial matrix.

**Q:** What features in a patient with diabetes suggest a non-diabetic cause of renal disease?

**A:** 1. Rising creatinine with little/no proteinuria. 2. Lack of retinopathy or neuropathy (retinopathy precedes nephropathy in ~80% type 1 DM, ~50% type 2 DM). 3. Persistent hematuria (microscopic or macroscopic). 4. Signs/symptoms of other systemic disease. 5. Time course disproportionate to diabetes duration (e.g., rapidly rising Cr in short-duration DM). 6. Family history of non-diabetic renal disease (e.g., PKD, Alport's).

These red flags prompt investigation for alternative diagnoses such as IgA nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, lupus nephritis, or hereditary nephropathy. Retinopathy almost always precedes nephropathy in type 1 DM, so nephropathy without retinopathy strongly suggests a non-diabetic etiology.

**Q:** What is the first-line treatment for hypertension in diabetic nephropathy and what is the blood pressure target?

**A:** First-line: ACEI or ARB (provide renoprotection even in normotensive diabetic nephropathy). Second-line: dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers. Target BP: <130/80 mmHg. Combination of ACEI and ARB is NOT recommended.

ACEI/ARBs reduce intraglomerular pressure by dilating the efferent arteriole, decreasing proteinuria and slowing nephropathy progression independent of BP lowering. Serum Cr can safely rise up to 30% with initiation — usually stabilizes after 2-4 weeks. If >30% rise or hyperkalemia develops, discontinue and consider second-line agent. Monitor K+ and Cr within 1 week of initiation.

**Q:** What SGLT2 inhibitors are indicated for renoprotection in diabetic nephropathy and what is their mechanism?

**A:** Canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and empagliflozin provide renoprotection independent of glycemic effects. Indicated in type 2 DM with CKD and eGFR ≥20 mL/min/1.73m², and in CKD with ACR ≥20 mg/mmol or heart failure irrespective of albuminuria level. Mechanism: reduce intraglomerular pressure via tubuloglomerular feedback (reduced glucose/sodium reabsorption → increased macula densa NaCl → afferent arteriolar constriction).

The CREDENCE trial demonstrated canagliflozin reduces renal outcomes in type 2 DM with diabetic nephropathy. SGLT2i also reduce risk of heart failure hospitalization and have independent cardiovascular benefits. They should be continued unless not tolerated or kidney replacement therapy is initiated.

**Q:** What are the four renal complications of diabetes?

**A:** 1. Progressive glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodular and diffuse). 2. Accelerated atherosclerosis (decreased GFR, increased ATN risk with contrast). 3. Autonomic neuropathy (atonic bladder → functional obstruction, urinary retention, infection, obstructive nephropathy). 4. Papillary necrosis (ischemic necrosis of medullary papillae in type 1 DM; sloughed papillae may obstruct ureter, present as renal colic).

Diabetic papillary necrosis occurs due to ischemia of the vasa recta that supply the papillae. Sloughed papillae can obstruct the ureter causing renal colic or hydronephrosis. Autonomic neuropathy leading to atonic bladder promotes stasis and recurrent UTIs, causing obstructive nephropathy.

**Q:** What referral criteria to a nephrologist are recommended for patients with CKD and diabetes?

**A:** Refer if: ACR >60 mg/mmol, eGFR <30 mL/min, progressive loss of kidney function, inability to achieve BP targets, or inability to stay on ACEI or ARB.

These criteria reflect high-risk features requiring specialist involvement for optimization of CKD management, consideration of dialysis preparation, and management of complications beyond primary care scope. Early referral (eGFR <30 rather than waiting for ESRD) allows for timely dialysis access planning and transplant evaluation.

**Q:** What is the GLP-1 receptor agonist's role in type 2 DM with CKD?

**A:** A long-acting GLP-1 RA should be used in adults with type 2 DM and CKD who have not achieved glycemic targets despite metformin and SGLT2 inhibitor, or who are unable to use these agents. Prioritize GLP-1 RA agents with documented CV benefits (e.g., semaglutide, liraglutide).

GLP-1 RAs provide weight loss, glucose lowering, and cardiovascular protection. They are third-line after metformin and SGLT2i in type 2 DM with CKD. Their renal benefits are partly via BP reduction, weight loss, and direct anti-inflammatory/anti-fibrotic effects. Dose adjustment may be needed in advanced CKD.

**Q:** What is the finerenone indication in CKD?

**A:** Finerenone (a non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist) provides renoprotection on top of RAAS blockade in CKD, particularly in type 2 DM with diabetic nephropathy.

Finerenone differs from spironolactone/eplerenone in being non-steroidal with greater selectivity for the mineralocorticoid receptor, reducing off-target sex-hormone side effects. It reduces proteinuria, slows CKD progression, and reduces cardiovascular events. It is used on top of ACEI or ARB, not as a replacement.

## Nephrology Systemic

**Q:** How does multiple myeloma cause renal damage?

**A:** Multiple mechanisms: 1. Light chain cast nephropathy ('myeloma kidney') — large tubular casts (light chains + Tamm-Horsfall protein). 2. Hypercalcemia. 3. Hyperuricemia. 4. Infection. 5. AL amyloidosis (proteinuria ± nephrotic syndrome). 6. Monoclonal Ig deposition disease (nodular glomerulosclerosis similar to DN, or MPGN). 7. Diffuse tubular obstruction.

Bence-Jones proteins (monoclonal light chains) are filtered at the glomerulus but NOT detected on standard urine dipstick — they require urine protein immunoelectrophoresis. Light chain cast nephropathy can progress rapidly to kidney failure. Myeloma patients are poor candidates for kidney transplantation.

**Q:** Why can Bence-Jones proteinuria be missed on urine dipstick?

**A:** Urine dipstick detects albumin, not light chains (Bence-Jones proteins). Monoclonal light chains must be detected by urine protein immunoelectrophoresis or urine protein electrophoresis.

This is a classic MCCQE1 trap: a multiple myeloma patient may have heavy proteinuria from light chains that is completely missed on dipstick, which only reacts with albumin. Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and urine protein electrophoresis (UPEP) with immunofixation are the appropriate investigations.

**Q:** What renal manifestations are associated with tumour lysis syndrome?

**A:** Hyperuricemia → diffuse tubular obstruction, AKI; hyperkalemia; hyperphosphatemia; hypocalcemia; elevated LDH; lactic acidosis.

Tumour lysis syndrome occurs when massive cell death (spontaneous or treatment-induced) releases intracellular contents. Uric acid and calcium-phosphate crystals precipitate in renal tubules causing AKI. Prevention includes aggressive IV hydration and allopurinol/rasburicase prior to chemotherapy.

## Nephrology Ckd

**Q:** How is Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) defined?

**A:** Abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for a minimum of 3 months, with implications for health.

CKD is classified based on cause, GFR category (G1-G5), and albuminuria category (A1-A3). A duration of ≥3 months distinguishes CKD from acute processes. Many patients are asymptomatic until later stages. The KDIGO 2024 guidelines updated the classification system.

**Q:** What are the KDIGO 2024 GFR categories for CKD staging?

**A:** G1: ≥90 mL/min/1.73m² (normal or high). G2: 60-89 (mildly decreased). G3a: 45-59 (mildly to moderately decreased). G3b: 30-44 (moderately to severely decreased). G4: 15-29 (severely decreased). G5: <15 (kidney failure). 'D' is added to G5 for patients requiring dialysis.

CKD classification incorporates cause (C), GFR category (G), and albuminuria category (A) — the CGA system. Risk of progression and complications is determined by cause, GFR, and degree of albuminuria. Green = low risk; Yellow = moderately increased; Orange = high risk; Red = very high risk.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of CKD by organ system?

**A:** Cardiovascular: HTN, CHF, pericarditis (uremia). GI: N/V, anorexia, dysgeusia (typically only in CKD G5). Neurologic: lethargy, confusion, neuropathy, seizures, asterixis, hyperreflexia, restless leg syndrome, encephalopathy. Hematologic: normocytic normochromic anemia (reduced EPO), platelet dysfunction (uremic bleeding). Endocrine/Metabolic: hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, reduced 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D. Reproductive: hypothalamic-pituitary disturbances, infertility. Other: pruritus.

Uremic syndrome results from retention of uremic toxins and hormone deficiencies (EPO, activated vitamin D). GI symptoms (anorexia, N/V, dysgeusia) typically only manifest in CKD G5. Uremic pericarditis is an indication for urgent dialysis.

**Q:** What are the strongest predictor of progression to ESRD?

**A:** The amount of daily proteinuria (strongest predictor). Other risk factors: hypertension, age, history of chronic renal insufficiency, DM, tobacco use, analgesic use, ethnicity, lower socioeconomic status, obesity, hyperuricemia, and family history of kidney disease.

Proteinuria causes direct tubular toxicity through activation of complement and inflammatory cascades, as well as contributing to interstitial fibrosis. Reducing proteinuria with ACEI/ARB is a primary renoprotective strategy in CKD management.

**Q:** What dietary and lifestyle modifications are recommended in CKD management?

**A:** Physical activity ≥150 min/wk. Low-protein diet: 0.8 g/kg/d in adults with CKD G3-G5 (not in children — protein needed for growth). Sodium restriction: <2 g/d if HTN, CHF, or oliguria. Potassium restriction: 40-60 mmol/d for hyperkalemia. Phosphate restriction: 1 g/d for hyperphosphatemia. Avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, COX2 inhibitors, IV contrast if eGFR <60). Stop tobacco products.

Low-protein diet reduces urea generation and may slow CKD progression, though evidence in some populations is conflicted. Sodium restriction helps control hypertension and volume overload. Phosphate binders (calcium carbonate, sevelamer, lanthanum) are used when dietary restriction is insufficient.

**Q:** How is anemia of CKD managed?

**A:** IV vs PO iron if TSAT <20% or ferritin <200 AND an increase in Hb or decrease in ESA dose is desired (avoid IV iron in active systemic infections). Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents (ESAs): initiate if Hb <100 g/L, target Hb 95-110 g/L. ESAs: epoetin-alfa or darbepoetin. Contraindications to ESAs: active malignancy, history of stroke, history of malignancy.

Anemia in CKD is normocytic normochromic, primarily due to reduced EPO production. Iron deficiency is common due to poor intake and dialysis-related losses. Overtreatment of anemia with ESAs (targeting higher Hb levels) is associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Platelet dysfunction causing uremic bleeding is treated with DDAVP (desmopressin) for clinical bleeding or invasive procedures.

**Q:** How is hyperphosphatemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism managed in CKD?

**A:** Phosphate binders: calcium carbonate (if not hypercalcemic); sevelamer or lanthanum salts if BOTH hypercalcemic AND hyperphosphatemic. Vitamin D and calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D) for secondary hyperparathyroidism. For tertiary hyperparathyroidism: parathyroidectomy or cinacalcet (sensitizes parathyroid gland to Ca²⁺, decreasing PTH secretion).

In CKD, reduced renal activation of vitamin D → hypocalcemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism. Phosphate retention further suppresses calcitriol and directly stimulates PTH. Calcium carbonate is avoided when hypercalcemic as it worsens hypercalcemia. Sevelamer is a non-calcium, non-aluminum phosphate binder. Cinacalcet is a calcimimetic used for tertiary (autonomous) hyperparathyroidism.

**Q:** What SGLT2 inhibitor indications exist in non-diabetic CKD?

**A:** SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) are indicated in CKD with eGFR ≥20 mL/min/1.73m² AND urine ACR ≥20 mg/mmol, OR in patients with heart failure (irrespective of albuminuria level), regardless of diabetic status.

SGLT2i reduce intraglomerular pressure via tubuloglomerular feedback, independent of glucose lowering. Evidence from trials like DAPA-CKD shows benefit in non-diabetic CKD patients. They should be continued until kidney replacement therapy is initiated unless not tolerated.

**Q:** What is the blood pressure target for CKD patients without kidney transplant vs. those with kidney transplant?

**A:** Without transplant: ACEI/ARB, target SBP <120 mmHg. With kidney transplant: CCB/ARB, target BP <130/80 mmHg.

Intensive BP control (SBP <120 mmHg) is supported by evidence in non-transplant CKD. In transplant recipients, calcineurin inhibitors (which cause vasoconstriction) and immunosuppressive regimens complicate BP management; CCBs are particularly effective as they counteract CNI-induced vasoconstriction. Statin therapy is recommended for CKD patients not on dialysis (target LDL <2 mmol/L); initiation is not recommended for dialysis-dependent CKD.

## Nephrology Hypertension

**Q:** What is hypertensive nephrosclerosis and how does chronic differ from malignant hypertensive nephrosclerosis?

**A:** Chronic nephrosclerosis: slow vascular sclerosis with ischemic changes of intralobular and afferent arterioles; associated with Black race (ApoL1-mediated), underlying CKD; mild to severe proteinuria, normal urine sediment; target BP <120/80; can progress to renal failure if HTN poorly controlled. Malignant nephrosclerosis: fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles, disruption of vascular endothelium; acute BP elevation (dBP >120 mmHg), HTN encephalopathy, acute end-organ damage; proteinuria and hematuria with RBC casts; lower dBP to 100-110 mmHg within 6-24h.

Malignant HTN causes end-organ damage (papilledema, stroke, ACS, AKI) and requires urgent but controlled BP reduction. Lowering dBP too aggressively can cause ischemic events (autoregulation is reset at a higher BP). ApoL1 gene variants explain the higher prevalence of hypertensive nephrosclerosis in Black patients.

**Q:** In the management of malignant hypertension, what is the target blood pressure reduction in the first 6-24 hours and why is more aggressive treatment dangerous?

**A:** Lower diastolic BP to 100-110 mmHg within 6-24 hours. More aggressive treatment can cause ischemic events because cerebral autoregulation is reset at a higher BP in chronic hypertensive patients — sudden normalization causes cerebral hypoperfusion.

Malignant hypertension (dBP >120 mmHg) requires urgent but controlled BP reduction using IV agents (labetalol, nicardipine, sodium nitroprusside). The 25% rule: reduce MAP by no more than 25% in the first hour, then target 100-110 mmHg dBP over next 6-24h. Chronic hypertensives have a rightward shift of the cerebral autoregulation curve, making them vulnerable to ischemia at 'normal' BPs.

## Nephrology Cystic

**Q:** What are the genetic features and extrarenal manifestations of Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD)?

**A:** Autosomal dominant; two genes: PKD1 (chr 16p, 1:400) and PKD2 (chr 4q, 1:1000). PKD2 accounts for ~10% of renal failure cases. Patients are heterozygous but accumulate 'somatic hits' precipitating cysts. Extrarenal manifestations: hepatic cysts (33%, most common), mitral valve prolapse (25%), intracranial arterial aneurysms (10%), diverticulosis, abdominal/inguinal hernias. Less common: cysts in pancreas, spleen, thyroid, ovary, seminal vesicles, aorta.

The 'two-hit' model explains why heterozygous carriers develop cysts. PKD1 mutations are more severe (earlier ESRD). Intracranial aneurysms are clinically important — screen if family history of aneurysmal hemorrhage. Polycystic liver disease rarely causes liver failure but can cause mass effect leading to malnutrition.

**Q:** What are the signs and symptoms of ADPKD?

**A:** Often asymptomatic; discovered incidentally or by screening FHx. Symptoms: acute abdominal/flank pain or dull lumbar back pain (from cyst infection, hemorrhage, or stones); hematuria (frequently initial sign); nocturia (urinary concentrating defect); extrarenal: ruptured berry aneurysm, diverticulitis, MVP. HTN in 60-75% (from renin release due to focal intrarenal arterial compression by cysts). Bilateral palpable kidneys.

Hematuria is often the first presenting sign (microscopic or gross). Hypertension develops from activation of RAAS due to intrarenal cyst compression — management with ACEI targeting <110/75 mmHg. Clinical manifestations are rare before age 20-25; kidneys are normal at birth but may enlarge to 10x normal size.

**Q:** What is the investigation of choice for confirming ADPKD and how is it confirmed?

**A:** Ultrasound is confirmatory (enlarged kidneys, multiple cysts throughout renal parenchyma, increased cortical thickness, splaying of renal calyces). CT abdomen with contrast for equivocal cases. MRI for kidney volume measurement. Gene linkage analysis for PKD1 for asymptomatic carriers. Cr, BUN, urine R&M to assess hematuria.

Ultrasound is the first-line investigation due to its availability and lack of radiation. The number of cysts required for diagnosis varies by age. MRI is the gold standard for total kidney volume (TKV) measurement, which is used to risk-stratify patients for tolvaptan therapy.

**Q:** What is tolvaptan and when is it used in ADPKD?

**A:** Tolvaptan is a vasopressin V2 receptor (V2R) antagonist that slows decline of renal function in ADPKD. Indicated in patients aged 18-50 yr with eGFR >45 mL/min and total kidney volume (TKV) >750 mL. Use is limited by side effects (aquaresis, hepatotoxicity).

ADH (vasopressin) promotes cyst growth via V2 receptors on cyst epithelial cells (cAMP-mediated). Tolvaptan blocks this, slowing cyst growth. The TEMPO 3:4 trial (NEJM 2017) showed tolvaptan slowed TKV growth and eGFR decline vs. placebo. Encouraging free water intake >2-3 L/d also reduces endogenous ADH, supporting a similar mechanism.

**Q:** Which antibiotics can penetrate ADPKD cyst walls and treat cyst infections?

**A:** TMP/SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) and ciprofloxacin are able to penetrate cyst walls and achieve therapeutic levels for treating cyst infections.

Most antibiotics cannot penetrate cyst walls effectively. Lipophilic antibiotics like fluoroquinolones and TMP/SMX achieve adequate cyst concentrations. Cyst infections present with fever, flank pain, and elevated inflammatory markers; they are differentiated from nephrolithiasis by leukocytosis and elevated CRP without stone on CT.

**Q:** What is Autosomal Recessive Polycystic Kidney Disease (ARPKD) and how does it differ from ADPKD?

**A:** ARPKD: incidence 1:20,000 (vs. ADPKD 1:400-1:1000). Prenatal diagnosis by enlarged kidneys (cystic dilatation of collecting ducts); significant in utero involvement causes Potter sequence. Perinatal death from respiratory failure. Associated with hepatic fibrosis. Survivors develop CHF, HTN, CKD, portal hypertension. Treatment: dialysis, kidney ± liver transplant.

Potter sequence (pulmonary hypoplasia, limb deformities, characteristic facies) results from severe oligohydramnios due to absent fetal urine production. ARPKD involves collecting duct dilatation (vs. ADPKD which involves all nephron segments). Hepatic fibrosis is a consistent feature of ARPKD, distinguishing it from ADPKD (which has hepatic cysts, not fibrosis).

**Q:** What are the features of Medullary Sponge Kidney?

**A:** Common; autosomal dominant; usually diagnosed in 4th-5th decades. Multiple cystic dilatations in collecting ducts of medulla. Features: renal stones, hematuria, recurrent UTIs. 10% of patients presenting with renal stones have medullary sponge kidney. Nephrocalcinosis on abdominal X-ray in 50% (often asymptomatic). Diagnosis: contrast-filled medullary cysts on IVP → 'bouquet of flowers' radial pattern; 'Swiss cheese' appearance on histology. Does NOT result in renal failure.

The 'bouquet of flowers' appearance on IVP is pathognomonic. Medullary sponge kidney is distinguished from other cystic diseases by its medullary-only involvement, lack of progression to ESRD, and characteristic IVP appearance. Treatment is symptomatic — treat UTIs and stone formation.

**Q:** A patient with ADPKD develops a fever and flank pain. Which antibiotics should be chosen for a suspected cyst infection and why?

**A:** TMP/SMX or ciprofloxacin — both are lipophilic and penetrate cyst walls to achieve therapeutic levels within cysts. Standard beta-lactams and aminoglycosides cannot penetrate cyst walls effectively.

Cyst infections in ADPKD are difficult to treat because most antibiotics cannot reach therapeutic concentrations within cyst fluid. Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) and TMP/SMX are lipophilic, allowing cyst penetration. Blood cultures, urine culture, and CT scan are part of the workup. Prolonged courses (4-6 weeks) are typically required.

## Nephrology Esrd

**Q:** What are the three components of the presentation of End Stage Renal Disease (ESRD)?

**A:** 1. Volume overload: weight gain from fluid retention, HTN, pulmonary/peripheral edema, orthopnea (due to increased total body Na⁺). 2. Electrolyte abnormalities: high K⁺, high PO₄³⁻; low Ca²⁺ (decreased vitamin D activation + hyperphosphatemia + hypoalbuminemia), low HCO₃⁻ (metabolic acidosis). 3. Uremic syndrome: retention of uremic toxins + hormone deficiencies → multisystem manifestations.

Volume overload results from Na⁺ retention by the failing kidneys. The electrolyte picture is characteristic: hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, metabolic acidosis. Uremic syndrome affects all organ systems and includes uremic pericarditis, encephalopathy, asterixis, and platelet dysfunction.

**Q:** What is the GFR definition of ESRD and what is the strongest predictor of progression to ESRD?

**A:** ESRD: irreversible decline in kidney function requiring renal replacement therapy; GFR <15 mL/min/1.73m². Strongest predictor of progression to ESRD: amount of daily proteinuria.

A GFR <15 corresponds to CKD G5. While GFR <15 is the definition, renal replacement therapy (dialysis or transplant) may be initiated based on symptoms (uremic symptoms, volume overload, electrolyte abnormalities) rather than GFR alone. Proteinuria is the strongest predictor because it directly causes tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis.
