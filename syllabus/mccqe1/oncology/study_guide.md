# Oncology

## General

**Q:** What infectious agent is required for Kaposi Sarcoma development?

**A:** Human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8)

Also known as Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), HHV-8 is necessary but not sufficient for KS development. It is present in all clinical variants of the disease.

**Q:** What infectious agent is REQUIRED for the development of Kaposi Sarcoma?

**A:** Human Herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8), also known as Kaposi Sarcoma-associated Herpesvirus (KSHV)

HHV-8 is present in all forms of Kaposi sarcoma and is the defining pathogenic agent, required for malignant transformation of endothelial cells.

**Q:** Which cancers commonly present with paraneoplastic syndromes?

**A:** Lung, breast, ovaries, or lymphatic system

These tumors are most frequently associated with paraneoplastic phenomena.

**Q:** What cancers commonly present with paraneoplastic syndromes?

**A:** Lung, breast, ovaries, and lymphatic system malignancies

These tumors frequently produce ectopic hormones or trigger autoimmune responses.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of ectopic ACTH syndrome?

**A:** Weight gain in trunk, face, and between shoulders (typical Cushingoid features)

Hypercortisolism from ectopic ACTH produces classic Cushing's syndrome signs.

**Q:** Which malignancies are commonly associated with paraneoplastic endocrine syndromes?

**A:** Lung, breast, ovaries, or lymphatic system.

These tumors commonly produce ectopic hormones or trigger immune cross-reactions.

## Oncology

**Q:** What are the characteristics of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) vs. non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)?

**A:** SCLC (10-15%): central location, strong smoking correlation, neuroendocrine origin, disseminated at presentation, treated with chemo + radiation (surgery rare). NSCLC (85-90%): includes adenocarcinoma (most common, peripheral), SCC (central), large cell (peripheral).

Adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype in nonsmokers. SCLC has the worst prognosis. Paraneoplastic syndromes (SIADH, Cushing's, Lambert-Eaton) are most often associated with SCLC. PD-L1 testing guides immunotherapy.

**Q:** What paraneoplastic syndromes are associated with lung cancer?

**A:** SCLC: SIADH, Cushing's (ACTH), Lambert-Eaton syndrome. SCC: hypercalcemia (PTHrP). NSCLC: clubbing, HPOA, Trousseau's syndrome (migratory thrombophlebitis). Bronchial carcinoid: carcinoid syndrome.

Lung cancer is the most common cause of cancer mortality worldwide in both men and women. 2/3 of primary lung cancers metastasize to the lower lung due to increased blood flow to the base.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for esophageal SCC vs. adenocarcinoma?

**A:** SCC: smoking, alcohol, hot liquids, achalasia, strictures, diverticula; more common in Black/Asian populations. Adenocarcinoma: Barrett's esophagus (most important), GERD, obesity, smoking; more common in White populations.

Adenocarcinoma is most common in Western countries (lower 1/3). SCC is most common worldwide (upper 2/3). Both present with progressive dysphagia (solids then liquids). Prognosis is usually poor due to late presentation.

**Q:** What are the two greatest risk factors for breast cancer?

**A:** Female sex (99% of cases) and age (83% diagnosed >50 years old).

Other risk factors include personal/family history, estrogen exposure (nulliparity, early menarche, late menopause, HRT >5 years), high breast density, BRCA1/2 mutations, and radiation exposure. 1 in 8 Canadian women will be diagnosed.

**Q:** What is the triple test for diagnosis of breast cancer?

**A:** (1) Clinical breast exam, (2) Imaging (mammography + U/S if ≥30; U/S alone if <30/pregnant/lactating), (3) Pathology (U/S or mammography-guided core needle biopsy).

A normal mammogram does not rule out cancer based on clinical findings. MRI is used for patients with ≥25% lifetime risk. Any palpable dominant breast mass requires further investigation regardless of age.

**Q:** What is the key principle regarding breast-conserving surgery (BCS) vs. mastectomy?

**A:** There is no survival benefit of mastectomy over BCS plus radiation for stage I and II disease. BCS + radiation achieves equivalent survival to mastectomy when local control is achieved.

BCS contraindications include inflammatory breast cancer, multifocal disease, failure to obtain clear margins, and inability to receive radiation. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is performed in clinically node-negative disease.

**Q:** What are the hormonal therapy options for ER-positive breast cancer?

**A:** Premenopausal: tamoxifen (SERM) for 5-10 years. Postmenopausal: aromatase inhibitors (e.g., anastrozole). Other options: ovarian ablation (GnRH agonist, oophorectomy).

Women receiving tamoxifen need regular gynecologic follow-up due to increased risk of endometrial cancer. For HER2-positive breast cancer, add trastuzumab + pertuzumab to chemotherapy. Triple-negative disease (ER/PR/HER2-negative) has worse prognosis.

**Q:** What is DCIS and how is it treated?

**A:** Ductal carcinoma in situ: malignant cells contained within breast ducts, 80% non-palpable, detected by screening mammogram as microcalcifications. Treatment: lumpectomy + radiation (5-10% invasive cancer risk) or mastectomy if large/multifocal (risk reduced to 1%). 99% 5-year survival.

DCIS carries a 35% risk of invasive ductal carcinoma in the same breast over 10 years if untreated. LCIS is different — it's a risk factor for invasive carcinoma (~1%/year) in either breast and is managed with surveillance.
