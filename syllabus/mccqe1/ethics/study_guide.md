# Ethics

## Informed Consent & Capacity

**Q:** A 78-year-old with early dementia wants to refuse amputation for a gangrenous foot. How do you assess capacity for this decision?

**A:** Capacity assessment: patient must demonstrate UNDERSTANDING the information, APPRECIATION of how it applies to their situation, REASONING through the options, and COMMUNICATION of a choice (4 criteria — Aid MacIAT/ACE tests)

Capacity is decision-specific and may fluctuate. CMPA guidelines: capacity must be assessed for each decision. Presume capacity unless evidence suggests otherwise. Cognitive tests (MMSE, MoCA) do not replace formal capacity assessment. If lacking capacity: substitute decision-maker uses prior wishes or best interests.

**Q:** What are the elements of informed consent?

**A:** Patient must be: competent (has capacity), informed (understands diagnosis, proposed treatment, alternatives, risks, benefits, consequences of refusing), and voluntary (no coercion) — documented in medical record

CMPA guidelines: informed consent is a process, not just a signature. Tarasoff standard: disclose material risks (those a reasonable person would want to know, plus specific risks this patient would want to know). Risk should be disclosed in terms of probability AND severity.

**Q:** Who acts as substitute decision-maker (SDM) when a patient lacks capacity in Ontario?

**A:** SDM hierarchy in Ontario (Health Care Consent Act): 1) Appointed attorney for personal care (POA), 2) Spouse/partner, 3) Parent/child (18+), 4) Sibling, 5) Any other relative, 6) Public Guardian and Trustee

SDM hierarchy varies by province. SDM must use incapable person's prior expressed wishes and values (if known), or in their best interests if wishes unknown. SDM cannot authorize medical assistance in dying (MAID). Consent and Capacity Board (CCB) in Ontario adjudicates disputes.

**Q:** What is the difference between capacity and competence?

**A:** Capacity: clinical assessment by a healthcare provider of the patient's ability to make a specific healthcare decision; Competence: legal determination by a court that a person is or is not capable of managing their affairs

In practice, "capacity" is used in clinical settings for healthcare decisions. "Competence" is the legal term for broader decision-making ability (finances, legal decisions). Physicians assess capacity; courts determine competence. All adults are presumed capable unless assessed otherwise.

**Q:** A competent patient refuses a blood transfusion as a Jehovah's Witness. What is the physician's obligation?

**A:** Respect the patient's autonomy and informed refusal — do not administer blood products; document the refusal; offer alternative treatments (cell salvage, erythropoietin, volume expanders); explore patient's values and wishes in advance

CMPA guidelines: a competent adult has the right to refuse ANY medical treatment, even life-saving treatment. Physician must ensure the refusal is informed (patient understands consequences). Document thoroughly. Ethics consultation helpful. Court applications for children of Jehovah's Witnesses are different.

**Q:** What is an advance directive and what types exist in Canada?

**A:** Advance directive: written instructions for future healthcare when patient lacks capacity; Types: (1) Living will (instructional — specific wishes), (2) Power of Attorney for Personal Care (POAPC — designates a substitute decision-maker)

Provincial legislation governs advance directives. Living wills are instructional and tell SDMs what the patient would want. POAPC is the most practical form. Healthcare teams must honor valid advance directives. All patients should be encouraged to complete advance directives while capable.

**Q:** How should informed consent be obtained for a research study involving patients?

**A:** Research ethics board (REB) approval required; written informed consent with full disclosure of: purpose, methods, risks and benefits, confidentiality, voluntary participation, right to withdraw without consequences; research consent is separate from clinical consent

TCPS2 (Tri-Council Policy Statement) governs research involving humans in Canada. Vulnerable populations (cognitively impaired, prisoners, children) require additional protections. Waiver of consent allowed if research involves minimal risk and consent is impractical.

**Q:** A 15-year-old presents alone requesting contraception. Can consent be obtained without parental involvement?

**A:** Yes — mature minor doctrine: adolescents who can demonstrate capacity can consent to healthcare independently; most provinces recognize the mature minor doctrine for healthcare decisions

Provincial legislation varies. In most Canadian provinces, adolescents can consent to their own healthcare (including contraception, STI testing/treatment, mental health) if they demonstrate capacity. Physician assesses capacity — explain information about contraception, confirm understanding, check for coercion.

**Q:** What is therapeutic privilege and is it ethically defensible?

**A:** Therapeutic privilege: withholding information from patients to prevent psychological harm — NOT generally accepted in Canada; physicians cannot withhold information they believe the patient would want

CMPA guidelines strongly discourage therapeutic privilege. Exceptions: when disclosure would cause direct, immediate harm (rare). Physicians may choose timing and framing of difficult information, but cannot withhold material information. Cultural differences in information preferences should be explored and respected.

**Q:** What is the process for obtaining consent for an incapable person from their SDM?

**A:** SDM must use: (1) prior capable wishes if known (including advance directives); (2) if no prior wishes: known values and beliefs of the incapable person; (3) if unknown: best interests (considering well-being, consistent values, and avoid unnecessary suffering)

Ontario Health Care Consent Act: SDM is bound by these principles in hierarchical order. Best interests is the last resort. SDM cannot impose their own values on the incapable person. Consent and Capacity Board can review SDM decisions that appear contrary to the patient's best interests.

**Q:** A patient with schizophrenia is refusing hospitalization. He has paranoid delusions that are causing him to not eat. What are the options?

**A:** If patient lacks capacity: seek consent from SDM or apply for Community Treatment Order (CTO) or involuntary admission under provincial Mental Health Act; if capacity retained: respect refusal, maximize community support

Mental health capacity is assessed using the same criteria. Psychotic patients may retain capacity for healthcare decisions even with active psychosis if they can understand and appreciate. Involuntary hospitalization requires: mental disorder + serious bodily harm to self or others + hospitalization needed. Form 1 in Ontario.

**Q:** What is a do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order and how should it be established?

**A:** DNR order: documents patient's wish to not receive CPR in the event of cardiac/respiratory arrest — should be discussed proactively with all patients with serious illness; NOT a general "do not treat" order

CMPA guidelines: DNR orders require informed consent discussion (goals of care conversation), documented in chart, and regularly reviewed. DNR order does not mean "comfort care only" — all other treatments continue unless separately addressed. POLST/MOST forms document broader goals of care.

**Q:** What are the rights of patients who are involuntarily admitted under a Mental Health Act?

**A:** Rights: (1) right to be told reasons for admission, (2) right to retain legal counsel, (3) right to have admission reviewed (mental health tribunal/board), (4) right to refuse treatment even when involuntary (capacity still governs treatment consent), (5) right to have substitute decision-maker for treatment decisions

Being involuntarily hospitalized does NOT remove a patient's right to refuse treatment. Separate capacity assessment and SDM involvement required for treatment decisions. Rights adviser visits involuntary patients in Ontario to explain rights. Review board/tribunal can discharge if admission criteria not met.

**Q:** What is the Capable Consent to Treatment and how does it differ from MAID consent?

**A:** Regular treatment consent: capable patient, informed of options, voluntary decision; MAID consent: additional safeguards — two assessments by independent practitioners, 10-day reflection period (waivable for imminent loss of capacity), written request, witness requirements

MAID (Medical Assistance in Dying): Bill C-7 expanded eligibility beyond end of life. Additional protections: final consent can be waived in advance for loss of capacity if suffering from serious neurocognitive illness. Bill C-7 consultation period for mental illness as sole condition.

**Q:** What is the duty of a physician when a capable patient consents to a procedure but then becomes uncapable during the procedure?

**A:** If patient gave prior capable consent and becomes incapable during the procedure (e.g., anesthesia), physician can continue the procedure the patient consented to — do NOT need to stop and get SDM consent

This applies to the specific procedure consented to. Any expansion of the procedure beyond what was consented to requires SDM consent unless it is an emergency. If the patient had an advance directive addressing this situation, it applies. CMPA guidance on intraoperative consent issues.

**Q:** A 67-year-old man with moderate Alzheimer's disease presents to sign consent for an elective hip replacement. He can state the nature of the procedure but cannot explain what would happen if he declined surgery. He is accompanied by his adult son who holds a Continuing Power of Attorney for Property (but not Personal Care). What is the most appropriate next step?

A) Proceed with consent from the son, as he holds POA
B) Assess the patient's capacity formally and, if incapacitated, identify a substitute decision-maker following provincial hierarchy
C) Accept the patient's stated wish to proceed as his expressed preference
D) Delay surgery until a court-appointed guardian is obtained
E) Contact the provincial capacity board immediately

**A:** B) Assess the patient's capacity formally and, if incapacitated, identify a substitute decision-maker following provincial hierarchy

POA for Property does NOT authorize healthcare decisions. POA for Personal Care (or its equivalent) is required. The son cannot consent here. The physician must formally assess capacity using the ability to understand information AND appreciate consequences. If the patient lacks capacity, the provincial SDM hierarchy applies (spouse/partner → child → parent → sibling…). A court order is only needed if no SDM is available or there is disagreement.

**Q:** During consent for a laparoscopic cholecystectomy, a surgeon informs a patient of a 0.3% risk of common bile duct injury. The patient undergoes the procedure and suffers a bile duct injury. She later sues, claiming she was not warned. In Canadian law (following Reibl v. Hughes and the modified objective test), which statement is MOST accurate?

A) The surgeon is not liable because disclosure of 0.3% risk meets the reasonable physician standard
B) Liability depends on whether a reasonable person in the patient's circumstances would have declined surgery had they known of the risk
C) The surgeon is liable only if the injury resulted from negligence in technique
D) Disclosure of any risk below 1% is not legally required
E) The patient must prove the surgeon acted below the standard of care in performing the procedure

**A:** B) Liability depends on whether a reasonable person in the patient's circumstances would have declined surgery had they known of the risk

Canadian law (Reibl v. Hughes, 1980) uses a MODIFIED OBJECTIVE test: would a reasonable person in the patient's specific circumstances have refused surgery if properly warned? This departs from both the purely subjective test (what would THIS patient have done) and purely objective test (what would ANY patient do). Technical negligence is a separate inquiry. Threshold percentages do not determine disclosure — materiality to the patient does.

**Q:** A 15-year-old presents alone requesting oral contraceptives. She demonstrates full understanding of the risks, benefits, and alternatives, and her reasons are consistent and not influenced by a third party. Her parents are unaware of her request. Under the mature minor doctrine in most Canadian provinces, what is the correct approach?

A) Refuse treatment and require parental consent as she is under 16
B) Prescribe and maintain confidentiality, as she meets criteria for mature minor status
C) Prescribe but notify parents within 24 hours as per mandatory reporting
D) Refer to psychiatry to assess maturity before prescribing
E) Prescribe only with written consent from one parent

**A:** B) Prescribe and maintain confidentiality, as she meets criteria for mature minor status

The mature minor doctrine (recognized across Canadian common law provinces) allows a minor with decision-making capacity to consent to treatment without parental involvement. Capacity criteria: understands the nature of the treatment and its consequences. There is no minimum age threshold — it is a functional test. Disclosing to parents without her consent would violate confidentiality unless there is a specific mandatory reporting obligation (which does not apply here).

**Q:** During an emergency appendectomy under general anesthesia, the surgeon incidentally discovers a 4 cm ovarian cyst. The patient has previously expressed no preference about this scenario. Which action is MOST ethically and legally appropriate?

A) Remove the cyst since it is clinically significant and the patient is already under anesthesia
B) Remove the cyst only if it appears malignant on gross inspection
C) Leave the cyst, close the abdomen, and discuss findings with the patient postoperatively
D) Consult gynecology intraoperatively for a decision
E) Proceed with removal given the principle of implied consent for emergencies

**A:** C) Leave the cyst, close the abdomen, and discuss findings with the patient postoperatively

The 'extension doctrine' allows intraoperative extension of a procedure ONLY when: (1) it is immediately life-threatening, AND (2) delaying would cause serious harm. An asymptomatic ovarian cyst does not meet this threshold. Implied consent covers the emergency that brought the patient to the OR (appendectomy), not elective additional procedures. The correct approach is to close and discuss. Delaying a cyst management decision does not cause immediate harm.

**Q:** A 78-year-old with early vascular dementia refuses a blood transfusion for a GI bleed. His Hgb is 58. He states: 'I don't want a blood transfusion because I am a Jehovah's Witness and it goes against my faith.' He cannot name the hospital or the date, but consistently articulates his religious objection and the consequence (he may die). The treating physician assesses him. What is the most legally sound conclusion?

A) He lacks capacity because he cannot name the hospital or date, suggesting global cognitive impairment
B) He has capacity for THIS specific decision despite cognitive deficits, as he understands and appreciates its consequences in the context of his values
C) He lacks capacity because refusal in this context is irrational
D) His SDM should be consulted because dementia always negates capacity
E) A capacity board must be convened before any treatment decisions can be made

**A:** B) He has capacity for THIS specific decision despite cognitive deficits, as he understands and appreciates its consequences in the context of his values

Capacity is DECISION-SPECIFIC, not global. Inability to recall the date or orientation details does not automatically negate capacity for a specific healthcare decision. The test is: can he understand the relevant information AND appreciate how it applies to his situation? He clearly does — he understands he may die and links that to his religious values. This is a classic MCCQE1 trap: dementia ≠ incapacity. Courts have repeatedly upheld such refusals (Malette v. Shulman analogy). Treat capacity as a sliding scale — higher stakes require more rigorous assessment.

**Q:** A surgeon plans to operate on a patient's LEFT knee. In the operating room, the patient is draped and anesthetized. The circulating nurse notices the consent form says 'right knee.' The surgeon states he reviewed imaging and is certain the left knee is the correct site. What is the most appropriate action?

A) Proceed with left knee surgery as the surgeon's clinical judgment supersedes an administrative error
B) Wake the patient and clarify the correct site before proceeding
C) Proceed and have the patient re-sign corrected consent in the recovery room
D) Continue if two team members verbally confirm the correct site
E) Proceed based on the imaging report which overrides the written consent

**A:** B) Wake the patient and clarify the correct site before proceeding

Wrong-site surgery is a never event and a serious patient safety issue. Regardless of clinical certainty, surgery must STOP and the patient must be woken to confirm. The consent form is a legal document — discrepancies cannot be resolved intraoperatively by team consensus. This aligns with the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist and provincial patient safety frameworks. 'Surgeon's certainty' does not substitute for confirmed written consent.

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman is intubated and sedated in the ICU following a massive stroke. Her husband (her SDM) refuses a recommended PEG tube, stating she 'would have wanted everything done.' The ICU team believes PEG insertion aligns with her current medical interests. Her husband, however, has no documentation of her wishes. Under the substituted judgment standard, what is the correct framework for the SDM's decision?

A) The SDM must consent to any treatment the medical team considers beneficial
B) The SDM must make the decision the incapacitated person would have made based on prior expressed values, wishes, and beliefs
C) The SDM's personal beliefs take precedence when there is no advance directive
D) The SDM should choose the option least likely to result in death
E) A second SDM should be appointed given the conflict

**A:** B) The SDM must make the decision the incapacitated person would have made based on prior expressed values, wishes, and beliefs

The gold standard for SDM decision-making in Canada is SUBSTITUTED JUDGMENT: what would the patient have decided? This is NOT about what the SDM wants or what is medically 'best.' The SDM must act on prior expressed wishes (verbal or written), known values, and cultural/religious beliefs. If no prior wishes are known, the SDM defaults to 'best interests.' The husband's statement 'would have wanted everything done' is a substituted judgment claim and must be evaluated against known evidence of her wishes. Conflict resolution resources (ethics consult, capacity board) are appropriate if needed.

**Q:** A physician obtains verbal consent for a lumbar puncture, performs the procedure without incident, but forgets to document the consent discussion in the chart. The patient later develops post-LP headache and claims consent was never obtained. In a medicolegal context, what is the most accurate statement?

A) Without written documentation, consent cannot be legally proven, and the physician is liable
B) Documentation of consent is important evidence, but verbal consent can still be valid if independently corroborated
C) Verbal consent is always sufficient for minor invasive procedures and documentation is optional
D) Undocumented consent constitutes battery regardless of whether the patient was actually informed
E) The burden of proof shifts entirely to the physician to prove consent occurred

**A:** B) Documentation of consent is important evidence, but verbal consent can still be valid if independently corroborated

Consent itself is a PROCESS, not a piece of paper. Written documentation is not legally required for consent to be valid — but it is critical evidence. Verbal consent CAN be valid and enforceable if supported by contemporaneous notes, witnesses, or clinical records. However, the CMPA strongly recommends documentation. Lack of written consent does not automatically constitute battery (which requires absence of consent, not absence of documentation). The burden of proof in civil litigation typically rests with the plaintiff (patient).

**Q:** During a psychiatric assessment, a physician uses the MacCAT-T to evaluate a patient's decision-making capacity. The patient scores well on 'Understanding' and 'Reasoning' but poorly on 'Appreciation.' Which conclusion is MOST clinically accurate?

A) The patient has full capacity because understanding and reasoning are the most critical domains
B) The patient likely lacks capacity; inability to appreciate how information applies to their own situation is a fundamental incapacity criterion
C) The MacCAT-T is not validated for psychiatric patients and cannot guide capacity determination
D) Poor appreciation alone is insufficient to declare incapacity
E) The patient should be reassessed in 48 hours as appreciation scores fluctuate

**A:** B) The patient likely lacks capacity; inability to appreciate how information applies to their own situation is a fundamental incapacity criterion

The MacCAT-T assesses four domains: Understanding, Appreciation, Reasoning, and Expressing a Choice. APPRECIATION is arguably the most clinically significant — it tests whether the patient can acknowledge how the information applies to THEIR OWN situation. A patient with a delusion that they don't actually have cancer may 'understand' that cancer is serious but fail appreciation. Poor appreciation strongly suggests lack of capacity and is a key differentiator in psychiatric populations. A formal capacity determination still requires clinical judgment beyond the score.

**Q:** A family physician who has been treating a patient for 10 years is approached by the now-discharged (2 years ago) patient for a romantic relationship. The patient is clearly the initiator and the physician has done nothing to encourage the relationship. Under CPSO guidelines, which statement is MOST accurate?

A) Sexual relationships with former patients are permitted after a 2-year gap if the patient initiates
B) Sexual or romantic relationships with former patients are prohibited indefinitely if the physician-patient relationship was of a significant nature, as the power imbalance may persist indefinitely
C) A 1-year waiting period applies after formal discharge from care
D) The prohibition applies only during active physician-patient relationships
E) Consent from the former patient removes all ethical barriers

**A:** B) Sexual or romantic relationships with former patients are prohibited indefinitely if the physician-patient relationship was of a significant nature, as the power imbalance may persist indefinitely

CPSO and CMA guidelines prohibit sexual relationships with former patients when the physician-patient relationship was of a significant, ongoing, or trust-based nature (e.g., 10 years of family medicine). The prohibition may be INDEFINITE, not time-limited, because the power imbalance, transference, and trust established in long-term care can persist beyond formal discharge. Patient initiation does NOT eliminate the ethical responsibility — the professional is held to a higher standard. Short encounters (e.g., a single ER visit years ago) may be treated differently, but this case clearly warrants prohibition.

**Q:** A physician witnesses a colleague who appears intoxicated while preparing to perform an elective procedure. The colleague waves off concerns and proceeds to the OR. What is the MOST appropriate immediate action?

A) Document the observation and report to the department head after the procedure is complete
B) Physically prevent the colleague from entering the OR and immediately inform the department chief/most responsible physician and/or hospital administration
C) Inform the patient and allow the patient to decide whether to proceed
D) Contact the CPSO immediately before any other action
E) Follow the colleague into the OR to monitor the situation

**A:** B) Physically prevent the colleague from entering the OR and immediately inform the department chief/most responsible physician and/or hospital administration

This is an IMMEDIATE patient safety emergency. The physician has a duty to prevent imminent harm. The steps are: (1) IMMEDIATELY inform a superior (department chief, chief of staff, or on-call administrator) who can intervene, (2) the colleague must be removed from the OR before the procedure begins, (3) arrange alternative surgical coverage. Waiting until after the procedure, 'monitoring' the situation from inside the OR, or contacting the CPSO first (which is for longer-term reporting) are all incorrect. Patient safety takes absolute precedence over collegial loyalty.

**Q:** A surgeon operating in a private clinic refers patients to a diagnostic imaging clinic in which she holds a 15% ownership stake. She does not disclose this financial relationship to her patients. This scenario MOST directly violates which ethical/legal principle?

A) Beneficence — the referrals may not be in patients' best interests
B) Conflict of interest and the duty to disclose material information that may affect patient decision-making and physician objectivity
C) Fiduciary duty only if a patient suffers actual financial harm
D) Non-maleficence — the referrals expose patients to unnecessary radiation
E) Justice — the referrals are unfair to competing imaging centres

**A:** B) Conflict of interest and the duty to disclose material information that may affect patient decision-making and physician objectivity

A physician owning interest in a facility to which they refer patients creates a CONFLICT OF INTEREST — the physician's financial interests may influence clinical decisions. Physicians have a duty to: (1) disclose the relationship to patients, (2) provide alternatives, and (3) ensure the referral is clinically justified. Most provincial medical practice acts and CMA guidelines require disclosure of such relationships. Non-disclosure constitutes a breach of the fiduciary duty (which is underpinned by conflict of interest principles) and may constitute professional misconduct. Self-referral rules vary by province.

**Q:** A physician begins treating a mutual friend who was referred to them by a colleague. Over several months, the physician finds the relationship becoming personally intimate due to the prior friendship. No sexual contact has occurred, but the physician finds themselves routinely sharing personal information about other patients, giving the friend preferential scheduling, and disclosing personal information during appointments. This situation MOST reflects:

A) Normal behaviour in small communities where dual relationships are unavoidable
B) Boundary erosion — the blurring of professional and personal roles that constitutes a precursor to more serious boundary violations
C) Only a problem if the patient reports feeling uncomfortable
D) Only a reportable concern if it progresses to sexual contact
E) A CMPA matter rather than a CPSO concern

**A:** B) Boundary erosion — the blurring of professional and personal roles that constitutes a precursor to more serious boundary violations

This is a classic BOUNDARY EROSION scenario — the progressive blurring of professional limits that precedes more serious violations. Warning signs include: preferential scheduling, sharing personal information about other patients (confidentiality breach), and excessive self-disclosure. These represent a failure of professional role maintenance. The correct action for the physician is to recognize the erosion, seek peer or professional consultation, and consider transferring care to avoid harm. Boundary violations do not require sexual contact to be serious or reportable.

**Q:** A physician receives a free flight and hotel accommodation worth $3,000 from a pharmaceutical company to attend a medical conference where the company's new diabetes drug is prominently featured. The CME sessions are peer-reviewed and accredited. Which statement is MOST accurate?

A) This is acceptable as the CME content is peer-reviewed
B) Accepting industry-funded travel and accommodation creates a conflict of interest regardless of content quality, and contravenes CMA and provincial guidelines
C) This is acceptable if the physician discloses the sponsorship to patients who are subsequently prescribed the drug
D) Travel below $5,000 is exempt from conflict of interest regulations
E) This is only problematic if the physician was directly involved in promotional activities

**A:** B) Accepting industry-funded travel and accommodation creates a conflict of interest regardless of content quality, and contravenes CMA and provincial guidelines

CMA guidelines and most provincial college policies prohibit physicians from accepting industry gifts, including funded travel, that could be seen as influencing prescribing. The 'no quid pro quo' defense is insufficient — the concern is PERCEIVED or UNCONSCIOUS influence. Multiple studies show pharma-sponsored meals and travel measurably shift prescribing patterns. Accredited content does not cleanse the conflict. Physicians must pay their own way to industry events. If they accept corporate sponsorship, they must declare it and recuse themselves from related prescribing decisions.

**Q:** A researcher wants to conduct a retrospective chart review of 5,000 patients to study outcomes of a new antibiotic. Individual patient consent would be impractical and would introduce selection bias. The data is identifiable. Under the Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS2), can the REB waive consent?

A) No — identifiable data always requires individual consent regardless of practicality
B) Yes — the REB may waive consent if: the research cannot be practicably conducted without the waiver, it involves no more than minimal risk, waiving will not adversely affect participants' welfare, and participants would not object if asked
C) Yes — retrospective studies are categorically exempt from consent requirements
D) No — only anonymized data can be used without consent
E) Yes, but only if a patient representative group approves the waiver

**A:** B) Yes — the REB may waive consent if: the research cannot be practicably conducted without the waiver, it involves no more than minimal risk, waiving will not adversely affect participants' welfare, and participants would not object if asked

TCPS2 Article 3.7A permits REB waiver of consent for research involving identifiable information when: (1) the research cannot practically be conducted with consent, (2) the research involves no more than minimal risk, (3) privacy is protected to the extent possible, (4) waiving consent will not adversely affect welfare, and (5) it is impractical to contact participants (e.g., they are deceased, records are too old). All conditions must be met. This is a frequently tested TCPS2 provision.

**Q:** A Phase II RCT of a new cancer drug is ongoing. An interim analysis reveals the experimental arm has a statistically significant and clinically meaningful survival benefit over placebo. The trial is designed to run for 2 more years. What ethical principle requires consideration of early termination?

A) Equipoise — if evidence no longer supports uncertainty about superiority, continuing the trial is unethical
B) Non-maleficence — the drug has unknown long-term side effects
C) Justice — placebo arm participants deserve equal access to the experimental drug
D) Beneficence — researchers must maximize the benefit to future patients by completing the trial
E) Autonomy — participants consented to the full trial duration and must be respected

**A:** A) Equipoise — if evidence no longer supports uncertainty about superiority, continuing the trial is unethical

CLINICAL EQUIPOISE is the ethical foundation for RCTs — there must be genuine uncertainty about which arm is superior. When compelling interim data demonstrates clear superiority, equipoise is broken, and continuing the trial to administer placebo to participants becomes unethical (withholding a known superior treatment). Data Safety Monitoring Boards (DSMBs) exist precisely to monitor for this. Early stopping rules are pre-specified. Beneficence to future patients does NOT override duty to current participants.

**Q:** A researcher studying cognitive decline in elderly patients wishes to enroll participants who can no longer provide independent consent. She plans to obtain consent from their SDMs. Which statement under TCPS2 is MOST accurate?

A) Research on incapable adults is categorically prohibited in Canada
B) SDM consent is permissible if the research offers direct benefit OR, for minimal-risk research, if the SDM gives consent and the research cannot be conducted with capable participants
C) The research must be approved by a court before enrollment can proceed
D) Research involving incapable adults always requires the research to have been enrolled when the participant was still capable
E) Only observational research (no interventions) may be conducted on incapable adults

**A:** B) SDM consent is permissible if the research offers direct benefit OR, for minimal-risk research, if the SDM gives consent and the research cannot be conducted with capable participants

TCPS2 permits research on incapable adults when: (1) the research cannot be conducted solely with capable individuals, (2) SDM provides authorized third-party consent, (3) the research offers direct benefit to the participant OR involves minimal risk and generalizable knowledge, and (4) the participant's assent is sought and objection is respected. Canada does not require court approval for SDM-consented research. The 'enrolled when capable' requirement (prospective assent) is one but not the only pathway.

**Q:** A researcher publishes a study showing a new surgical technique reduces complication rates by 40%. Six months later, data fabrication is discovered — the statistical results were manipulated. The journal retracts the paper. Surgeons who adopted the technique based on the published findings have already performed 200 cases. What is the PRIMARY obligation of the researcher following discovery of the fabrication?

A) Retract the paper and resign from the institution
B) Notify the journal, the institution, and regulatory bodies; take all steps to prevent continued use of the fraudulent findings, including notification of the surgical community
C) Sue the whistleblower who reported the fabrication
D) Publish a correction (not retraction) acknowledging statistical errors
E) Only the institution (not the individual researcher) bears responsibility for notification

**A:** B) Notify the journal, the institution, and regulatory bodies; take all steps to prevent continued use of the fraudulent findings, including notification of the surgical community

Research misconduct (fabrication, falsification, plagiarism) triggers multiple obligations: (1) immediate notification and cooperation with institutional investigation, (2) retraction of the publication, (3) notification of parties who may have acted on the findings (surgical societies, health authorities), and (4) possible notification to Health Canada if the research involved approved devices or drugs. The researcher bears PRIMARY personal responsibility alongside institutional obligations. Retraction alone is insufficient if practice has already changed.

**Q:** During a clinical trial, a participant develops a serious adverse event potentially related to the investigational drug. The principal investigator is uncertain whether it is drug-related. What is the MOST appropriate immediate action?

A) Wait until the end of the trial to report, as single events cannot be attributed to causation
B) Report the serious adverse event to Health Canada within the required timeframe (15 calendar days for unexpected serious adverse drug reactions) regardless of certainty about causality
C) Consult with the pharmaceutical sponsor first to determine if reporting is necessary
D) Exclude the participant from the analysis and continue the trial
E) Notify the REB only if the event is fatal

**A:** B) Report the serious adverse event to Health Canada within the required timeframe (15 calendar days for unexpected serious adverse drug reactions) regardless of certainty about causality

Under Health Canada's regulations (Food and Drug Regulations, C.05.012), all UNEXPECTED serious adverse drug reactions must be reported to Health Canada within 15 calendar days regardless of whether causality is established. 'Unexpected' means not described in the current investigator's brochure. Fatal/life-threatening unexpected reactions require expedited 7-day reporting. The standard is precautionary — causal certainty is not required for reporting. The REB must also be notified.

**Q:** A physician is served with a subpoena to produce a patient's medical records for civil litigation between the patient and a third party. The patient has NOT consented to the disclosure. What is the most appropriate response?

A) Comply immediately with the subpoena — it is a court order that supersedes privacy legislation
B) Notify the patient immediately, seek legal counsel (and CMPA assistance), and potentially file a motion to quash or limit the subpoena pending patient consent or a court order specifically directing disclosure
C) Refuse categorically and destroy the records before the court date
D) Disclose only demographic information and withhold clinical content
E) Notify the CPSO before taking any other action

**A:** B) Notify the patient immediately, seek legal counsel (and CMPA assistance), and potentially file a motion to quash or limit the subpoena pending patient consent or a court order specifically directing disclosure

A subpoena is a DEMAND to produce, not automatically a court ORDER permitting breach of privilege. Ontario courts recognize physician-patient privilege in civil proceedings. The correct steps: (1) notify the patient immediately so they can instruct their own counsel, (2) seek CMPA guidance, (3) potentially bring a motion to quash/modify the subpoena or require a formal court order before disclosure. Simply complying without this process may breach PHIPA and violate physician-patient confidentiality. Only an explicit court ORDER (not merely a subpoena) compels production.

**Q:** A physician is asked to complete a disability insurance form for a long-term patient and write that the patient is 'unable to work in any capacity' when, in the physician's clinical opinion, the patient is able to perform sedentary work. The patient insists this framing is necessary to obtain benefits. What is the most appropriate response?

A) Complete the form as requested — patient advocacy is a core physician role
B) Decline to complete the form and discharge the patient from care

Failure to disclose may also violate the hospital's bylaws and the physician's professional obligations.

**Q:** Under the Ontario Health Care Consent Act (HCCA), which statement about the hierarchy of substitute decision-makers is MOST accurate when a patient lacks both a designated POA for Personal Care and a court-appointed guardian?

A) The attending physician assumes SDM authority
B) The SDM hierarchy proceeds as: spouse/partner → child (16+) → parent → sibling → other relative → public guardian — the highest ranked available and willing person who meets the qualifications acts as SDM
C) Any adult family member may act as SDM without ranking
D) The SDM hierarchy only applies to palliative care decisions
E) Provincial courts must appoint a guardian before any treatment decisions can be made

**A:** B) The SDM hierarchy proceeds as: spouse/partner → child (16+) → parent → sibling → other relative → public guardian — the highest ranked available and willing person who meets the qualifications acts as SDM

Ontario's Health Care Consent Act (HCCA) s.20 establishes a specific SDM HIERARCHY for patients without a POA for Personal Care or court-appointed guardian: (1) guardian of the person, (2) attorney for personal care, (3) representative appointed by CCB, (4) SPOUSE or PARTNER, (5) child (16+) or parent, (6) parent with right of access, (7) sibling, (8) any other relative, (9) Office of the Public Guardian and Trustee. Qualifications for SDMs: must be capable, 16+, not prohibited by court, willing to assume role. The OPGT acts as last resort. **Q:** A physician treats a patient who is a sex worker. The physician internally holds a moral judgment about the patient's profession. The patient presents for routine STI screening. Which principle guides the physician's obligations?

A) The physician may refuse care based on personal moral objections to the patient's profession
B) Non-judgmental care is a foundational principle: the physician must provide equitable, respectful healthcare to all patients regardless of personal moral views about their profession, lifestyle, or choices
C) The physician should refer to another provider to avoid personal discomfort
D) The physician may disclose the patient's profession to public health authorities
E) The physician may charge higher fees to offset the increased STI risk

**A:** B) Non-judgmental care is a foundational principle: the physician must provide equitable, respectful healthcare to all patients regardless of personal moral views about their profession, lifestyle, or choices

Physicians have a professional obligation to provide NON-DISCRIMINATORY, non-judgmental care to all patients regardless of personal moral views. The Canadian Human Rights Act prohibits discrimination on various grounds, and professional codes explicitly prohibit withholding care based on moral disapproval of a patient's lawful occupation. In fact, sex workers are a vulnerable population who already face significant barriers to healthcare — stigmatizing attitudes by healthcare providers compound these barriers. The physician must recognize their own bias, manage it, and provide the same quality of care they would provide to any patient. Disclosure of occupation to public health authorities is not appropriate without clinical justification.

**Q:** A physician is asked by a patient for a medical procedure the physician believes is not indicated but carries low risk and the patient desires for personal reasons. The patient is fully informed and has decision-making capacity. Examples might include a low-risk elective genetic test or a low-risk screening test outside guidelines. Which framework applies?

A) The physician must always refuse non-indicated procedures
B) The physician must always comply with all patient requests
C) The physician must balance patient autonomy with professional integrity — they may decline procedures they believe are not clinically indicated, but must explain the reasoning, not dismiss the request, and ensure the patient can access the procedure through another provider if they wish
D) The physician should charge the patient for non-insured services and comply
E) Only procedures covered by provincial insurance may be offered

**A:** C) The physician must balance patient autonomy with professional integrity — they may decline procedures they believe are not clinically indicated, but must explain the reasoning, not dismiss the request, and ensure the patient can access the procedure through another provider if they wish

Patients may request interventions not in standard guidelines. The physician's obligations: (1) engage seriously with the request, (2) provide an honest professional assessment, (3) explain why the procedure is not recommended if they believe it's not indicated, (4) respect that the patient may still want it, (5) either proceed if they can do so with professional integrity, OR decline and facilitate access to another provider. Physicians are NOT obligated to provide procedures they believe are not indicated — but they cannot abandon the patient. This is the autonomy-beneficence-non-maleficence triangle in routine practice.

**Q:** A first-generation Canadian patient who speaks limited English requires complex surgical consent. A bilingual family member offers to interpret. The hospital has professional medical interpreter services available. What is the MOST appropriate approach?

A) Use the family member as interpreter as it is more time-efficient
B) Use the family member if the patient prefers this approach exclusively
C) Strongly recommend and provide professional medical interpreter services — family member interpretation risks omissions, emotional filtering, distorted disclosure, and confidentiality breach; it may invalidate informed consent if material information is not accurately conveyed
D) Use the bilingual family member and have them sign a form confirming accurate interpretation
E) No interpreter is needed if consent forms are translated into the patient's language

**A:** C) Strongly recommend and provide professional medical interpreter services — family member interpretation risks omissions, emotional filtering, distorted disclosure, and confidentiality breach; it may invalidate informed consent if material information is not accurately conveyed

Professional medical interpretation is the STANDARD OF CARE for informed consent with language-discordant patients. Risks of family member interpretation: (1) patient may withhold sensitive information from family, (2) family member may editorialize, filter, or simplify information based on emotional investment, (3) family members lack medical terminology competence, (4) confidentiality may be breached. Patient preference for a family member interpreter should be explored but professional services must be offered and made available. Use of an untrained family interpreter for complex consent is a medicolegal risk and may constitute a barrier to equitable care. CPSO guidelines support this.

**Q:** TRC Call to Action #22 calls for recognition of the value of what practices within the Canadian healthcare system?

**A:** Aboriginal healing practices, and the use of these practices in the treatment of Aboriginal patients, in collaboration with Aboriginal healers and Elders, where requested by Aboriginal patients.

Call to Action #22 mandates that the Canadian healthcare system integrate Traditional healing when patients request it. This requires physicians to respect and facilitate access to Traditional healers, ceremonies, and medicines alongside Western medical treatments. Several Canadian hospitals, including Sioux Lookout Meno Ya Win Health Centre, have implemented healing rooms.

**Q:** TRC Call to Action #23 addresses healthcare professional education. What does it require medical and nursing schools to do?

**A:** Require all students to take a course on Aboriginal health issues, including the history and legacy of residential schools, the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), Treaties and Aboriginal rights, and Indigenous teachings and practices.

Call to Action #23 has prompted Canadian medical schools to integrate Indigenous health curricula. The Association of Faculties of Medicine of Canada (AFMC) has developed competency frameworks to support this. It ensures future physicians understand the historical context driving Indigenous health disparities.

**Q:** Define cultural safety and explain how it differs from cultural competence in the context of Canadian Indigenous healthcare.

**A:** Cultural safety is defined by the recipient of care — the patient determines whether their encounter was culturally safe. It requires practitioners to examine their own biases, power imbalances, and institutional racism. Cultural competence focuses on the provider learning about other cultures, which can lead to stereotyping. Cultural safety shifts the focus from knowledge about cultures to self-reflection and systemic change.

Cultural safety originated from Maori nursing scholar Irihapeti Ramsden in New Zealand and has been adopted by Canadian organizations including the First Nations Health Authority (FNHA) in BC. The Indigenous Physicians Association of Canada emphasizes that cultural safety goes beyond awareness or sensitivity by addressing power differentials and institutional structures that perpetuate health inequities.

**Q:** A physician dismisses an Indigenous patient's request for a Traditional healer visit during their hospitalization, stating 'we practice evidence-based medicine here.' Which concept does this scenario best illustrate, and what is the appropriate response?

**A:** This illustrates a culturally unsafe practice and Indigenous-specific racism. The appropriate response is to facilitate the Traditional healer visit per TRC Call to Action #22 and the patient's right to culturally safe care. Traditional healing and Western medicine are not mutually exclusive.

Dismissing Traditional healing reflects epistemic racism — the assumption that Western biomedical knowledge is the only valid knowledge system. Canadian healthcare institutions are increasingly required to accommodate Traditional healing practices. The FNHA's cultural safety framework explicitly requires healthcare providers to respect Indigenous healing traditions.

**Q:** A 62-year-old residential school survivor presents with chronic pain, insomnia, hypervigilance, and avoidance of healthcare settings. What approach to care is most appropriate?

**A:** Trauma-informed care: recognize the impact of residential school trauma, establish safety and trust, offer patient control over the clinical encounter, avoid retraumatization (e.g., explain all procedures, ask permission for physical examination), screen for PTSD, and offer culturally appropriate mental health supports including access to the Indian Residential Schools Resolution Health Support Program.

Over 150,000 Indigenous children attended residential schools in Canada, and survivors have elevated rates of PTSD, depression, substance use disorders, and chronic pain. The Indian Residential Schools Resolution Health Support Program (1-866-925-4419) provides crisis counselling for survivors. Trauma-informed care principles — safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment — are essential.

**Q:** How should a physician approach integrating Traditional Indigenous healing practices with Western medical treatment?

**A:** Respectfully ask the patient if they use Traditional medicines or healing practices; do not assume based on appearance. If yes, document with permission, check for potential interactions with prescribed treatments, facilitate access to Traditional healers if requested, and never dismiss or discourage Traditional healing. Obtain guidance from community Elders or cultural liaisons when uncertain.

TRC Call to Action #22 mandates recognition of Traditional healing in the Canadian healthcare system. Many hospitals in Canada now have cultural liaison workers and healing rooms. Traditional practices may include smudging (burning sage, sweetgrass, cedar, or tobacco), sweat lodge ceremonies, and herbal medicines. Some Traditional medicines may interact with pharmaceuticals, so respectful inquiry is clinically important.

**Q:** What is the Canadian government's duty to consult, and how does UNDRIP (United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples) relate to healthcare?

**A:** The duty to consult is a constitutional obligation (Section 35, Constitution Act 1982) requiring the Crown to consult and accommodate Indigenous peoples when government actions may affect their Aboriginal or Treaty rights. UNDRIP, adopted into Canadian law via the UNDRIP Act (2021), affirms Indigenous peoples' right to their traditional medicines, health practices, and the highest attainable standard of health.

UNDRIP Article 24 specifically addresses the right to traditional medicines and health practices. Canada's UNDRIP Act (Bill C-15, 2021) requires federal laws to be consistent with UNDRIP. For healthcare, this means Indigenous communities must be meaningfully consulted on health policies affecting them, and their right to self-determination in health governance must be respected.

**Q:** The National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (MMIWG) concluded that the violence amounts to what, and what is a key healthcare-related finding?

**A:** The National Inquiry concluded that the violence constitutes genocide. Key healthcare-related findings include that Indigenous women and girls face racism and discrimination when accessing healthcare, are dismissed or undertreated for pain and acute conditions, face barriers to reporting violence, and experience disproportionate rates of forced or coerced sterilization.

The 2019 MMIWG Final Report documented systemic causes of violence including colonial structures, institutional racism in healthcare, and failures of the justice system. The Report's 231 Calls for Justice include healthcare-specific recommendations. The forced sterilization of Indigenous women, documented as recently as 2018 in Saskatchewan, exemplifies how healthcare systems perpetuate colonial violence.

**Q:** What is Indigenous-specific racism in healthcare, and what Canadian case exemplifies its lethal consequences?

**A:** Indigenous-specific racism in healthcare includes stereotyping (assuming substance use), dismissal of symptoms, undertreating pain, providing substandard care, and making racist comments. The death of Joyce Echaquan in 2020, a Atikamekw woman who livestreamed staff making racist and derogatory comments as she was dying in a Joliette, Quebec hospital, is a prominent example.

Joyce Echaquan's death led to the creation of Joyce's Principle, which demands the right to equitable access to health and social services without discrimination, and the right to the highest attainable standard of health. The coroner's inquiry ruled that racism contributed to her death. The In Plain Sight report (2020, BC) also documented widespread Indigenous-specific racism in the BC healthcare system.

**Q:** What are key principles of culturally safe communication when providing care to Indigenous patients in Canada?

**A:** Key principles include: acknowledge the historical context affecting trust in healthcare; avoid assumptions about identity, culture, or lifestyle; use respectful, non-judgmental language; allow adequate time (do not rush encounters); recognize that silence may indicate thoughtfulness, not disengagement; involve family/community as the patient wishes; ask about preferred name and pronouns; and ask permission before physical examination.

The FNHA and IPAC (Indigenous Physicians Association of Canada) emphasize that culturally safe communication requires ongoing self-reflection about one's own biases and power. The San'yas Indigenous Cultural Safety Training program, developed in BC, is widely used across Canadian health authorities. Communication must be individualized — not all Indigenous patients share the same cultural practices or preferences.

**Q:** A medical student says, 'I learned about Indigenous cultural practices so I am now culturally competent to treat Indigenous patients.' What is the critical flaw in this reasoning, and what framework should replace it?

**A:** The critical flaw is that cultural competence implies a finite endpoint of knowledge acquisition and risks stereotyping by assuming all Indigenous patients share the same practices. Cultural safety is the appropriate framework — it is ongoing, requires self-reflection on one's own biases and privilege, examines power imbalances in the clinical encounter, and is ultimately defined by the patient, not the provider.

Cultural safety evolved through a continuum: cultural awareness → cultural sensitivity → cultural competence → cultural safety. The key distinction is that cultural safety addresses systemic and institutional power dynamics, not just individual knowledge. The RCPSC (Royal College) and CFPC (College of Family Physicians of Canada) now embed cultural safety in CanMEDS and competency frameworks for Canadian physicians.

**Q:** What is the SPIKES protocol for breaking bad news?

**A:** Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions, Strategy/Summary

Evidence-based framework. Assess patient understanding first, ask permission to share information, respond to emotions with empathy before discussing plan.

**Q:** What is the duty of candour after a medical error?

**A:** Obligation to disclose harmful incidents to patients/families honestly and promptly. Includes: what happened, consequences, what is being done to mitigate harm, systemic changes to prevent recurrence

CPSO policy. Apology legislation in Canada protects apologies from being used as admission of liability. Disclosure is ethical obligation regardless of legal implications.

**Q:** What is shared decision-making and when is it most important?

**A:** Collaborative process where clinician shares evidence on options/outcomes and patient shares values/preferences to reach a joint decision. Critical for: screening, preference-sensitive decisions, goals of care

Decision aids improve patient knowledge and reduce decisional conflict. Examples: PSA screening, cancer treatment options, end-of-life care. Not appropriate for emergencies requiring immediate action.

**Q:** A patient files a complaint against you with the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario (CPSO). What is the first step in the CPSO complaints process?

**A:** The complaint is acknowledged and forwarded to the physician, who must provide a written response within 30 days. The Inquiries, Complaints and Reports Committee (ICRC) then reviews the complaint and response to determine next steps.

In Ontario, the Regulated Health Professions Act (RHPA) governs the complaints process through the ICRC. Possible outcomes range from no action, to a caution, to referral to the Discipline Committee. Physicians should contact the CMPA immediately upon receiving a complaint for medico-legal support.

**Q:** Under Canadian law, which conditions require mandatory reporting by a physician, even if it means breaching patient confidentiality?

**A:** Mandatory reporting obligations include: child abuse or neglect (to Children's Aid Society), certain communicable diseases (to public health), patients unfit to drive (varies by province; mandatory in some like Ontario), gunshot wounds (in most provinces), and suspected elder abuse (varies by jurisdiction).

Mandatory reporting duties override physician-patient confidentiality under specific provincial and federal statutes. In Ontario, the Child, Youth and Family Services Act mandates reporting suspected child maltreatment. Failure to report can result in regulatory discipline and, in the case of child abuse, potential criminal liability.

**Q:** You suspect a colleague is practising medicine while impaired by alcohol. What is your professional obligation in Canada?

**A:** You have a duty to report the colleague to the appropriate regulatory body (e.g., provincial College of Physicians and Surgeons). Most provincial Colleges also have Physician Health Programs that provide confidential support. The duty to report exists regardless of your relationship with the colleague.

The CMA Code of Ethics and provincial regulatory frameworks mandate that physicians report colleagues whose fitness to practise may be impaired. Provincial Physician Health Programs (e.g., the Ontario Physician Health Program) offer non-punitive pathways for impaired physicians to receive treatment while protecting patient safety.

**Q:** A grateful patient offers you a $200 gift card after a successful surgery. How should you handle this situation according to Canadian professional guidelines?

**A:** You should generally decline the gift. Accepting gifts of significant monetary value can create a sense of obligation and blur professional boundaries. If declining would harm the therapeutic relationship, a token gift of minimal value may be acceptable but should be documented.

The CMA Code of Ethics and CPSO Professional Obligations and Human Rights policy advise physicians to avoid accepting gifts that could influence clinical judgment or create perceived obligations. The CMPA recommends documenting any gift interaction. Cultural sensitivity is important, as some patients offer gifts as cultural practice.

**Q:** A physician posts a de-identified but recognizable clinical case on social media for educational purposes. What are the professional and legal risks?

**A:** Even de-identified cases may be recognizable and violate patient privacy under PIPEDA and provincial health privacy legislation (e.g., PHIPA in Ontario). Risks include regulatory complaints, civil liability, loss of patient trust, and potential College discipline.

The CMA Social Media Guidelines and CMPA resources emphasize that social media posts can breach privacy even when direct identifiers are removed. Combinations of clinical details may render a patient identifiable. Physicians should obtain explicit written consent before sharing any clinical information online.

**Q:** After a medical error, what does the 'duty of candour' require of a Canadian physician?

**A:** The duty of candour requires the physician to promptly disclose the error to the patient, explain what happened, describe the actual and potential consequences, outline steps being taken to mitigate harm, and offer an apology. Many provinces have Apology Acts that protect apologies from being used as admissions of liability.

The CMPA, CPSI (now Healthcare Excellence Canada), and CMA all endorse transparent disclosure of medical errors. Provincial Apology Acts (e.g., Ontario's Apology Act, 2009) protect expressions of sympathy from being used as evidence of liability, encouraging open disclosure and maintaining trust.

**Q:** What is 'just culture' in the context of healthcare, and how does it differ from a blame culture?

**A:** Just culture distinguishes between human error (consoled), at-risk behaviour (coached), and reckless behaviour (disciplined). It focuses on system-level factors contributing to errors rather than solely blaming individuals, while still holding individuals accountable for intentional reckless conduct.

Healthcare Excellence Canada (formerly CPSI) promotes just culture as fundamental to patient safety. Unlike a blame culture that punishes all errors regardless of intent, just culture recognizes that most medical errors arise from system failures. This approach encourages error reporting, enabling organizations to identify and fix systemic vulnerabilities.

**Q:** A pharmaceutical company invites you to an all-expenses-paid conference at a resort. What are the conflict of interest concerns under Canadian guidelines?

**A:** Accepting may create a perceived or real conflict of interest. The CMA Guidelines for Physicians in Interactions with Industry state that physicians should not accept personal gifts or hospitality of more than minimal value, and educational events should be in appropriate settings with transparent funding.

The CMA's Guidelines for Physicians in Interactions with Industry and Innovative Medicines Canada's Code of Ethical Practices restrict pharmaceutical company hospitality. Accepting lavish hospitality can create implicit reciprocity obligations that bias prescribing. Continuing medical education should be needs-based and accredited independently of industry influence.

**Q:** What is the role of the CMPA (Canadian Medical Protective Association) in the Canadian medical regulatory system?

**A:** The CMPA provides medico-legal protection and liability coverage to its physician members. It assists with legal defence in malpractice suits, provides guidance on complaints processes, offers risk management education, and compensates patients when negligence is established. It is a mutual defence organization, not an insurance company.

The CMPA is unique globally as a physician-owned mutual defence organization rather than a commercial insurer. It covers over 100,000 Canadian physicians and is funded by member fees, which are partially reimbursed by provincial governments. Understanding the CMPA's role is essential for navigating the Canadian medico-legal landscape.

**Q:** How does professional self-regulation work in Canadian medicine, and what is the 'social contract' underlying it?

**A:** In Canada, each province/territory has a College of Physicians and Surgeons that regulates physicians. The social contract grants the profession autonomy to set standards, credential members, and discipline practitioners, in exchange for prioritizing public safety and acting in the public interest rather than professional self-interest.

Self-regulation is a privilege granted by provincial legislatures through acts such as Ontario's RHPA. Recent controversies have led to governance reforms in several provinces, including increased public representation on College boards. The overriding principle is that the College's mandate is public protection, not advocacy for physicians.

**Q:** You must inform a patient that their biopsy shows metastatic cancer. Using the SPIKES protocol, what are the six steps for breaking bad news?

**A:** S – Setting: arrange privacy, sit down, involve significant others per patient's wishes. P – Perception: ask what the patient already knows. I – Invitation: ask how much information they want. K – Knowledge: deliver information in plain language, with warning shots. E – Emotions: respond empathetically to the patient's reaction. S – Strategy/Summary: outline next steps and plan for follow-up.

The SPIKES protocol (Buckman, 2005) is the most widely taught framework for breaking bad news in Canadian medical education. The MCCQE1 frequently tests the principle that you should assess the patient's understanding before delivering information (P step) and respond to emotion before discussing management (E step).

**Q:** What are the four core principles of motivational interviewing (MI)?

**A:** The four principles are: (1) Express empathy through reflective listening, (2) Develop discrepancy between current behaviour and desired goals, (3) Roll with resistance rather than confronting it, and (4) Support self-efficacy by affirming the patient's ability to change.

Motivational interviewing (Miller and Rollnick) is recommended by Canadian clinical practice guidelines for behaviour change counselling, including smoking cessation and alcohol reduction. The OARS technique (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, Summarizing) operationalizes MI in clinical encounters.

**Q:** A patient with newly diagnosed breast cancer asks you what treatment she should get. How do you apply the shared decision-making framework?

**A:** Shared decision-making involves: (1) Recognizing a decision needs to be made, (2) Presenting all reasonable treatment options with their risks, benefits, and evidence, (3) Exploring the patient's values, preferences, and concerns, (4) Reaching a decision together that aligns with the patient's informed preferences. Decision aids can help facilitate this process.

Shared decision-making is a core component of the CanMEDS Communicator role and is distinguished from both paternalistic medicine (doctor decides) and informed choice (patient decides alone). The Ottawa Decision Support Framework, developed at the Ottawa Hospital Research Institute, provides evidence-based patient decision aids used across Canada.

**Q:** When working with a professional medical interpreter, what communication practices should a physician follow?

**A:** Speak directly to the patient (not to the interpreter), use short sentences, avoid jargon and idioms, pause frequently to allow interpretation, arrange seating so interpreter is beside or slightly behind the patient, brief the interpreter beforehand on the topic, and use a professional interpreter rather than family members for sensitive or complex discussions.

Canadian healthcare accreditation standards require access to professional interpreter services. Using family members as interpreters is discouraged because of confidentiality concerns, potential for omission of sensitive information, and role conflicts (especially with children interpreting for parents). Telephone and video interpretation services are increasingly available across Canadian health systems.

**Q:** What are the key elements of culturally safe communication, and how does cultural safety differ from cultural competence?

**A:** Cultural competence focuses on learning about other cultures (knowledge-based). Cultural safety shifts the focus to the practitioner's self-reflection on their own power, privilege, and biases, and how these affect the therapeutic relationship. The patient defines whether the encounter was culturally safe, not the provider.

Cultural safety originated in New Zealand Maori nursing and is now central to Indigenous health education in Canada. The First Nations Health Authority in BC and the AFMC have embedded cultural safety into medical curricula. It requires physicians to acknowledge colonial legacies, systemic racism, and power imbalances in healthcare.

**Q:** What are the medico-legal documentation standards for a clinical encounter in Canada?

**A:** Documentation should include: date and time, patient identification, relevant history and findings, clinical reasoning, diagnosis or differential, management plan, informed consent discussions, patient education provided, and follow-up arrangements. Entries should be legible, contemporaneous, objective, and avoid derogatory language. Late entries should be clearly identified as such.

The CMPA emphasizes that 'if it wasn't documented, it wasn't done' in medico-legal proceedings. Provincial Colleges set documentation standards, and the CMPA reports that inadequate documentation is a contributing factor in a significant proportion of medico-legal cases. Electronic medical records must meet provincial privacy legislation requirements (e.g., PHIPA).

**Q:** A patient requests their complete medical record. What are their rights under Canadian privacy legislation?

**A:** Under PIPEDA (federal) and provincial health privacy acts (e.g., PHIPA in Ontario), patients have the right to access their personal health information. Physicians must respond within 30 days, may charge a reasonable fee for copying, and can only refuse access in limited circumstances (e.g., if disclosure could result in serious harm to the patient or a third party).

Provincial health privacy legislation (PHIPA in Ontario, HIA in Alberta, PHIA in Manitoba/Newfoundland) enshrines patients' right to access their health records. Physicians retain ownership of the physical record but patients own the information. The CMPA advises physicians to have clear policies for handling access requests.

**Q:** During a difficult conversation, a patient becomes angry and threatens to leave against medical advice (AMA). What communication strategies should you employ?

**A:** Acknowledge the patient's emotions without judgment, use active listening and reflective statements, explore the underlying reasons for their frustration, clearly explain the risks of leaving AMA in non-threatening language, offer alternatives or compromises, document the discussion including the patient's capacity to make the decision, and ensure they know they can return.

The CMPA recommends that AMA discharges be thoroughly documented, including that the patient was informed of risks, had capacity, and was not coerced to stay. Patients who leave AMA should not be abandoned; they should receive prescriptions, follow-up instructions, and reassurance that they are welcome to return. Punitive responses increase liability risk.

**Q:** What is 'trauma-informed communication' and when should it be applied in clinical practice?

**A:** Trauma-informed communication recognizes that many patients have experienced trauma (e.g., abuse, violence, colonialism). Key principles include: ensuring physical and emotional safety, giving the patient control and choice, being transparent about procedures, avoiding re-traumatization, and asking permission before physical examination. It should be applied universally, not only for patients with known trauma histories.

Trauma-informed care is increasingly emphasized in Canadian medical education, particularly in the context of Indigenous health (recognizing intergenerational trauma from residential schools), refugee care, and gender-based violence. The BC Provincial Mental Health and Substance Use Planning Council has published a widely-used trauma-informed practice guide.

**Q:** A medical student on your team wrote a clinical note that contains inaccurate information about a patient's medication list. What is the correct approach to managing documentation errors?

**A:** The error should be corrected using a proper addendum, not by deleting or overwriting the original entry. The addendum should clearly state the date of correction, identify the error, provide the correct information, and be signed by the correcting clinician. The original entry should remain visible (single line through for paper charts, audit trail in EMR).

Altering medical records without proper documentation of changes is considered fraudulent and has serious medico-legal consequences. The CMPA strongly advises against deleting or obscuring original entries. Electronic medical records must maintain audit trails of all changes, as required by provincial privacy legislation and health records standards.

**Q:** What are signs of child physical abuse that should trigger reporting?

**A:** Bruising in non-mobile infants, patterned injuries (belt/cord marks), burns in stocking-glove distribution, multiple fractures at different healing stages, retinal hemorrhages (shaken baby), inconsistent history

Mandatory reporting in ALL Canadian provinces when reasonable suspicion. Report to Children's Aid Society. Do not need certainty — suspicion is sufficient threshold. Document carefully with body diagrams.

**Q:** How do you screen for intimate partner violence?

**A:** Routine screening recommended (SOGC). Tools: HITS (Hurt, Insult, Threaten, Scream), SAFE questions. Ask in private without partner present. Validate, safety plan, document, offer resources

1 in 3 Canadian women experience IPV. Barriers to disclosure: fear, shame, financial dependence, immigration status. Not mandatory reporting for competent adults (vs child abuse). Document injuries objectively.

**Q:** What are the types of child abuse, and what physical examination findings should raise suspicion for non-accidental trauma?

**A:** Types: physical, sexual, emotional/psychological, neglect (most common). Suspicious findings: bruises in non-mobile infants, patterned injuries (loop marks, cigarette burns), multiple fractures in different stages of healing, retinal hemorrhages (shaken baby), injuries inconsistent with developmental stage or reported mechanism.

The 'TEN-4' rule identifies concerning bruising: bruising on the Torso, Ears, or Neck in children <4 years, or any bruising in infants <4 months. Physicians in all Canadian provinces are mandated reporters — failure to report suspected child abuse is a legal offense. Documentation should include verbatim history from the caregiver and child (separately).

**Q:** What are the indicators of child neglect, and how does it differ from other forms of child maltreatment?

**A:** Neglect: failure to meet basic needs (food, shelter, clothing, medical care, supervision, education). Indicators: failure to thrive, poor hygiene, untreated medical/dental conditions, frequent school absences, inadequate supervision for developmental age. It is the most common and often most harmful form of child maltreatment.

Neglect can be harder to identify than physical abuse as it represents an absence of action rather than a harmful action. Chronic neglect causes more long-term developmental and psychological harm than acute physical abuse. Risk factors include poverty, parental substance abuse, mental illness, and social isolation. Poverty alone does not constitute neglect.

**Q:** What is the legal obligation of physicians regarding suspected child abuse in Canada, and what information should be included in a report?

**A:** All physicians are mandated reporters in every Canadian province/territory — must report reasonable suspicion (not certainty) of child abuse or neglect to child protective services. Report should include: child's identity, nature of concerns, observed injuries, child's statements, caregiver's explanation, and any previous concerns.

The threshold for reporting is 'reasonable grounds to suspect,' not proof. Failure to report is a provincial offense. Physicians are protected from liability for reports made in good faith. Parental consent is not required for reporting, and the physician should not investigate independently but should document findings and report promptly.

**Q:** What is the HITS screening tool for intimate partner violence, and when should screening be performed?

**A:** HITS: How often does your partner — Hurt you physically, Insult you, Threaten you, Scream at you? Each scored 1 (never) to 5 (frequently), total ≥10 suggests IPV. Screen in routine health visits, prenatal care, ED visits with suspicious injuries, and when indicators are present.

IPV affects approximately 1 in 3 women and 1 in 4 men over their lifetime. Universal screening remains debated, but targeted screening at key touchpoints is widely recommended. Pregnancy is a high-risk period for IPV initiation or escalation. Always interview the patient alone, without the partner present, in a safe and confidential setting.

**Q:** What are the key components of safety planning for a patient experiencing intimate partner violence?

**A:** Safety planning: identify safe places to go in emergency, pack essential documents (ID, medications, financial records), memorize emergency numbers, develop code word with trusted person, plan for children and pets, connect to shelter/crisis resources, document injuries medically, consider protection order.

The most dangerous time for a victim is when leaving or shortly after leaving the relationship. Physicians should avoid pressuring patients to leave, as this can increase danger. Instead, express concern, validate the patient's experience, provide resources, and respect their autonomy in decision-making. Lethality assessment tools help identify highest-risk situations.

**Q:** How should a physician document suspected intimate partner violence in the medical record?

**A:** Document: patient's own words (in quotes), detailed injury description (size, shape, location, color, age), body diagram or photographs (with consent), mechanism reported and whether it is consistent with injuries, patient's affect and demeanor, safety assessment, referrals made, and follow-up plan.

Medical records may be used in legal proceedings, so documentation should be factual, objective, and thorough. Avoid judgmental language or opinions about truthfulness. Write 'patient states' rather than 'patient claims.' If the mechanism is inconsistent with injuries, document both the stated mechanism and the physical findings. Maintain confidentiality and avoid documenting in shared electronic portals accessible to the abuser.

**Q:** What are the risk factors for elder financial abuse, and what signs should clinicians watch for?

**A:** Risk factors: cognitive impairment, social isolation, dependence on caregiver, recent bereavement, unfamiliar people accompanying patient. Signs: sudden inability to pay for medications/care, unexplained changes in financial documents or wills, caregiver controlling finances, unpaid bills despite adequate resources, new 'friends' or caregivers.

Financial abuse is the most common form of elder abuse. Cognitive decline makes elders particularly vulnerable to exploitation by family members, caregivers, and strangers. Physicians can screen by asking about who manages finances, whether the patient feels their money is being used appropriately, and whether anyone has pressured them to sign documents.

**Q:** What are the differences in mandatory reporting obligations for elder abuse across Canadian provinces?

**A:** Reporting varies by province: some (e.g., Nova Scotia, Newfoundland) have mandatory reporting for all adults at risk. Others (e.g., Ontario) have mandatory reporting only in long-term care facilities. All provinces have adult protection legislation enabling investigation and intervention.

Unlike child abuse, where mandatory reporting is universal across Canada, elder abuse reporting requirements differ by jurisdiction. Physicians should know their provincial legislation. Even where reporting is not mandatory, it is ethically appropriate to involve adult protective services when abuse is suspected and the patient lacks capacity to protect themselves.

**Q:** A woman presents with chronic pelvic pain, depression, and multiple somatic complaints. She is accompanied by a partner who answers all questions and refuses to leave the room. What should you consider, and how should you proceed?

**A:** Consider intimate partner violence. Proceed by: finding a reason to separate the patient from the partner (e.g., 'we need a urine sample' or 'hospital policy requires private interview'), then screen with direct empathetic questions (e.g., 'Do you feel safe at home?'), assess safety, provide resources.

Controlling behavior by a partner is itself a form of abuse. Somatic presentations including chronic pain, headaches, GI symptoms, and depression are common in IPV survivors. Multiple ED visits with vague complaints should raise suspicion. Never confront the suspected abuser or disclose screening results in the partner's presence, as this endangers the patient.

**Q:** What types of sexual abuse in children should raise clinical suspicion, and what is the appropriate clinical response?

**A:** Suspicious findings: genital/anal injuries without adequate explanation, STIs in prepubertal children (gonorrhea, syphilis, HIV are diagnostic), age-inappropriate sexual knowledge/behavior, recurrent UTIs, encopresis/enuresis. Response: do not perform invasive genital exam (refer to specialized center), report to CPS, consult child abuse pediatrician, provide emotional support.

Most sexually abused children have a normal physical examination, so a normal exam does not rule out abuse. A child's disclosure is the most important diagnostic indicator. Forensic interviewing should be performed by trained professionals to avoid contaminating evidence. The physician's role is to report, document, ensure safety, and address immediate medical needs — not to investigate.

**Q:** What is the Emergency Rule for consent in Canadian emergency medicine?

**A:** Consent is not required when: (1) the patient is at imminent risk from a serious injury AND (2) obtaining consent is either not possible OR would increase risk to the patient. Assumes most people would want to be saved.

The Emergency Rule is an exception to the standard requirement for informed consent. It does NOT apply when a competent patient has previously refused similar treatment, an advance directive (e.g., DNR) exists, or a capable SDM refuses on the patient's behalf.

**Q:** Under what circumstances can emergency treatment NOT be initiated despite the Emergency Rule in Canada?

**A:** 1. A competent patient previously refused the same or similar treatment and there is no evidence wishes have changed. 2. A valid advance directive (e.g., DNR order) is available. NOTE: Refusal of help in a suicide attempt is NOT an exception — care must be given.

Advance directives and prior competent refusals override the Emergency Rule. However, suicidal refusal of care does not constitute a valid exception; the emergency physician must provide treatment.

**Q:** What is a 'mature minor' in the context of emergency consent for children in Canada?

**A:** A child below the age of majority who is deemed capable of understanding the nature and consequences of treatment decisions. Such a minor can consent to or refuse treatment independently. Age cut-off varies by province.

The mature minor doctrine recognises that cognitive capacity, not chronological age, determines decision-making ability. Emancipated minors (e.g., living independently, married) are similarly granted adult consent rights.

**Q:** What are the rights of Jehovah's Witnesses regarding blood transfusion in Canadian emergency medicine?

**A:** Capable adult Jehovah's Witnesses have the right to refuse blood products (pRBCs, platelets, plasma, whole blood) even if life-saving. However, children cannot be refused life-saving treatment solely on religious grounds — act in the child's best interest and involve CAS immediately.

Competent refusal must be respected. The physician should clarify which specific products are refused (some accept albumin, immunoglobulins). Thorough documentation of discussions and alternatives is mandatory.

**Q:** What is the general approach to sexual assault victims in the emergency department?

**A:** ABCs and treat acute injuries; emotional support; obtain consent for exam, evidence collection, and police disclosure; involve sexual assault nurse examiner (SANE); document with Sexual Assault Kit if <72 h; label samples and pass directly to police; offer community resources; do not report unless victim requests OR patient is <16 years old (mandatory reporting).

Physician priority is medical treatment and clearance. Re-traumatization must be avoided — intimate exams should be deferred to SANE if available. Samples collected without chain of custody may be inadmissible in court. Interprofessional sexual assault teams improve patient care and forensic evidence collection.

**Q:** What are the key legal and access considerations regarding abortion in Canada?

**A:** There is no current law in Canada concerning abortion. Access is provincially regulated and highly variable. Per CPSO guidelines, a physician must provide a referral for abortion services regardless of personal beliefs but is not compelled to personally perform the procedure. In Canada, 91% of induced abortions occur before 12 weeks GA.

The Supreme Court struck down abortion law in R v. Morgentaler (1988). Conscientious objection is accommodated but must not impede patient access — physicians must refer. Understanding provincial variability is important for MCCQE1 ethics and social determinants questions.

## End-of-Life Care & MAID

**Q:** What is Medical Assistance in Dying (MAID) in Canada and who is eligible?

**A:** MAID (Bill C-14, 2016; amended Bill C-7, 2021): adult with serious and incurable illness, disease, or disability; advanced state of irreversible decline; experiencing grievous and intolerable suffering (physical or psychological); grievous and irremediable condition — natural death need not be reasonably foreseeable

MAID in Canada is the most comprehensive in the world. Exclusions: mental illness as sole underlying condition (planned eligibility 2024); non-therapeutic permanent disabilities; minors. Two assessors + 10-day reflection period. Optional for physicians (conscience exemption must ensure patient access to another provider).

**Q:** What is the difference between palliative sedation, palliative care, and MAID?

**A:** Palliative sedation: sedation to unconsciousness to relieve refractory suffering at end of life (NOT intended to hasten death); Palliative care: comfort-focused care that may be concurrent with disease-directed treatment; MAID: physician actively ending patient's life at their explicit request

Double effect principle justifies palliative sedation and high-dose opioids for symptom control if the primary intent is comfort, not death acceleration. MAID requires explicit, capable, repeated request and meets legal eligibility criteria. Palliative sedation does not require MAID eligibility criteria.

**Q:** What are the principles of good palliative care?

**A:** Pain and symptom management, comfort-focused goals of care, psychosocial-spiritual support, advance care planning, family support (including bereavement), avoiding unnecessary interventions, patient-centered care

Canadian Hospice Palliative Care Association (CHPCA): palliative care should begin at diagnosis for serious illness (concurrent with disease-directed treatment), not just at end of life. Early palliative care referral improves quality of life and may extend survival (NEJM Temel study).

**Q:** A patient with terminal cancer has severe pain despite high-dose oral opioids. What options exist?

**A:** Rotate opioids (incomplete cross-tolerance may improve analgesia), increase dose, add adjuvant analgesics (steroids, anticonvulsants, TCAs for neuropathic pain), consider interventional pain management (nerve blocks, epidural), palliative sedation for refractory suffering

CHPCA guidelines: opioids are the cornerstone of cancer pain management. WHO analgesic ladder: non-opioids → weak opioids → strong opioids ± adjuvants. Opioids do not hasten death when used appropriately. Opiophobia and undertreatment of cancer pain are significant problems.

**Q:** What are the ethical principles involved in withdrawing life-sustaining treatment?

**A:** Withdrawing life-sustaining treatment (mechanical ventilation, dialysis, artificial nutrition) when: patient or SDM requests withdrawal, goals of care have shifted, or treatment is no longer in patient's best interests — ethically equivalent to not starting treatment (withholding)

Canadian ethicists and the courts agree: there is no ethical or legal difference between withholding and withdrawing life support. Once started, treatment can be withdrawn. SDM must guide decisions for incapable patients using prior wishes or best interests. Palliative sedation may accompany ventilator withdrawal.

**Q:** What is prognostication and why is it important in end-of-life care?

**A:** Prognostication: estimating likely disease course and life expectancy; important for: goals of care discussions, advance care planning, appropriate palliative care referral, avoiding unwanted aggressive interventions, and enabling final preparations

Clinicians systematically over-estimate prognosis in cancer and serious illness. Validated tools: PPS (Palliative Performance Scale), Surprise Question ("Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next year?"), PaP score. Honest communication improves decision-making and reduces aggressive end-of-life care.

**Q:** What are the signs of imminent death in the dying patient?

**A:** Signs of imminent death (hours to days): Cheyne-Stokes or irregular breathing, mottled skin (livedo reticularis), cooling of extremities, Kussmaul breathing, gurgling respirations (death rattle), decreased consciousness, inability to swallow

CHPCA End-of-Life Care Recommendations: family education about signs of dying reduces distress. Glycopyrrolate reduces death rattle (secretion management). Comfort measures: position for comfort, moist mouth care, discontinue vitals and monitoring, restrict unnecessary medications, reposition q4h.

**Q:** What is the role of truth-telling in communicating a serious diagnosis?

**A:** Physicians are ethically and legally obligated to be truthful with patients who have capacity — withholding a diagnosis violates patient autonomy; frame truth compassionately, give information in digestible amounts, ensure follow-up support

CMPA guidelines: cultural practices of withholding diagnosis from patients (common in some cultures) cannot override patient's right to know their diagnosis. However, physician can explore patient's information preferences ("how much detail would you like me to share?"). Third-party requests to withhold information must be addressed.

**Q:** What are the obligations when a patient requests MAID but you have a conscientious objection?

**A:** Physician must provide an effective referral to a provider who will provide MAID — refusing to provide referral violates the patient's Charter right to access a legal medical service

CMA and provincial colleges: conscientious objection is a protected right but does not extend to obstructing patient access. Physician must provide all relevant clinical information to the patient and facilitate timely transfer of care. System must not allow conscientious objection to create barriers to access.

**Q:** What is a goals of care conversation and what does it involve?

**A:** Goals of care: exploring patient values, understanding prognosis, identifying treatment goals (cure, extend life, maintain function, quality of life, comfort) and matching treatment plan to goals — using SPIKES protocol for difficult conversations

SPIKES protocol: Setting up, Perception (what does patient know/want to know), Invitation (how much detail), Knowledge (giving information), Empathy and Exploration, Strategy and Summary. CHPCA recommends goals of care conversations for all patients with serious illness.

**Q:** A 72-year-old man with COPD completed an advance directive 5 years ago stating 'no mechanical ventilation under any circumstances.' He now presents with a severe COPD exacerbation and is drowsy but arousable. He nods when asked if he wants to feel better. His daughter (SDM) insists on intubation. The advance directive was not updated after a mild MI last year. What governs here?

A) The advance directive governs because it is the most recent written expression of his wishes
B) The SDM's decision governs because the patient's nodding represents a new contemporaneous expression overriding the directive
C) The advance directive governs only if the physician determines the current situation is exactly what the patient envisioned when signing it
D) The advance directive is invalidated by failure to update after a significant health event
E) The patient's current nodding constitutes valid consent for intubation and overrides the advance directive

**A:** E) The patient's current nodding constitutes valid consent for intubation and overrides the advance directive

A key principle: if a CAPABLE patient makes a contemporaneous decision, it takes precedence over a prior advance directive. The patient is arousable and nodding — this may represent current capable expression of preference for treatment. Physicians must assess whether he currently has capacity. If he does, his current wishes control. If he does not, the advance directive guides the SDM. The failure to update after the MI does not automatically invalidate the directive — but the nodding raises a critical question about present capacity.

**Q:** Under Bill C-7 (2021 amendments to the Criminal Code regarding MAiD), which of the following BEST describes Track 2 eligibility?

A) Natural death is foreseeable and there is an irremediable serious illness
B) Natural death is NOT reasonably foreseeable, the person has a serious and incurable illness, disease, or disability, is in advanced decline, and experiences enduring physical or psychological suffering that cannot be relieved under acceptable conditions
C) MAiD may be requested solely on the basis of mental illness as of March 2023
D) The person must have exhausted all available treatments before applying
E) The 10-day reflection period has been eliminated for all track 2 patients

**A:** B) Natural death is NOT reasonably foreseeable, the person has a serious and incurable illness, disease, or disability, is in advanced decline, and experiences enduring physical or psychological suffering that cannot be relieved under acceptable conditions

Bill C-7 created two tracks. Track 1: natural death IS reasonably foreseeable (the 10-day reflection period was ELIMINATED here). Track 2: natural death is NOT reasonably foreseeable — requires additional safeguards: 90-day minimum assessment period, two independent assessors (one must have expertise in the relevant condition), serious and incurable illness causing enduring and intolerable suffering. As of March 2024, MAiD for mental illness ALONE was extended (not yet implemented). The 'exhausted all treatments' requirement was removed from Track 1 in C-7 but Track 2 requires consideration of alternative means.

**Q:** A physician receives a MAiD request from a 58-year-old woman with ALS who has clear decision-making capacity. The physician has a sincere conscientious objection to providing MAiD. What are the physician's obligations under Canadian professional standards?

A) The physician may refuse all involvement, including referral, as conscientious objection is absolute
B) The physician must provide MAiD regardless of personal beliefs as it is a legal medical procedure
C) The physician may decline to provide MAiD but must provide an effective referral or transfer of care to ensure timely access
D) The physician must refer only to another physician within the same specialty
E) The physician is required to inform the patient that MAiD is not available at this institution

**A:** C) The physician may decline to provide MAiD but must provide an effective referral or transfer of care to ensure timely access

In Canada, conscientious objection to MAiD is respected, but physicians retain an obligation to ensure patients receive TIMELY ACCESS to care. The standard (per CMA guidelines and most provincial colleges) is an 'effective referral' — actively connecting the patient with a willing provider or service, not merely suggesting they look elsewhere. In provinces like Ontario (CPSO), effective referral is explicitly required. Simply refusing and leaving the patient without recourse is unacceptable. The physician must not obstruct access.

**Q:** A patient with early-stage Alzheimer's disease has decision-making capacity today and wishes to create an advance request for MAiD to be administered in the future when she loses capacity. Which statement is MOST accurate regarding Canadian law as of 2024?

A) Advance requests for MAiD are currently legally permitted in Canada following the C-7 amendments
B) Advance requests for MAiD are NOT currently legally permitted in Canada — the assessments and consent must occur when the person still has capacity
C) Advance requests are permitted only for patients with a terminal illness with less than 6 months prognosis
D) Advance requests may be accepted if witnessed by a notary and two physicians
E) A substitute decision-maker can request MAiD on behalf of an incapacitated patient

**A:** B) Advance requests for MAiD are NOT currently legally permitted in Canada — the assessments and consent must occur when the person still has capacity

As of 2024, advance requests for MAiD are NOT legally permitted in Canada under the Criminal Code amendments — assessments and the final consent must occur while the person retains capacity. This is a significant gap for neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's. Bill C-7 directed the government to study advance requests; Parliament has been debating expanding the law to permit them but as of 2024, legislation has not been passed. SDMs cannot request MAiD on behalf of an incapacitated person. This is a commonly tested MCCQE1 nuance.

**Q:** On the day of a scheduled MAiD procedure, the patient has received her pre-medication. Just before administration of the lethal medication, she states 'wait, I'm not sure.' She appears distressed. The practitioner should:

A) Proceed as scheduled — pre-medication does not constitute withdrawal of consent and mild distress is expected
B) Stop immediately and assess whether the patient is expressing a genuine withdrawal of consent or peri-procedural anxiety; MAiD cannot proceed if the patient withdraws consent
C) Proceed unless the patient explicitly states 'I do not want to die today'
D) Consult the second assessor before proceeding
E) Delay 24 hours and reassess

**A:** B) Stop immediately and assess whether the patient is expressing a genuine withdrawal of consent or peri-procedural anxiety; MAiD cannot proceed if the patient withdraws consent

Under Canadian MAiD legislation, the person may WITHDRAW their request at any time, including moments before the procedure. The expression 'I'm not sure' is ambiguous and could represent: (1) genuine withdrawal of consent, or (2) peri-procedural anxiety. The practitioner must STOP and explore this clearly. If there is any doubt about whether the patient still wants to proceed, MAiD cannot ethically or legally continue. Capacity and voluntary consent must be confirmed immediately before administration. Proceeding under ambiguity would constitute unlawful homicide.

**Q:** A 44-year-old person with treatment-resistant depression (TRD) and no other concurrent physical illness requests MAiD. They have capacity and have tried multiple medication regimens, ECT, and ketamine infusions. Under the current Canadian legal framework, which is MOST accurate?

A) This person is eligible for MAiD now under Track 2 — mental illness is explicitly excluded from the eligibility criteria
B) This person may be eligible under Track 2 if MAiD for mental illness has been implemented and all eligibility criteria are met, but as of March 2024 the implementation has been delayed
C) This person qualifies because TRD meets the 'serious and incurable illness' criterion regardless of the mental illness exclusion
D) This person cannot be assessed for MAiD as they are under 65
E) MAiD may be provided only if TRD is accompanied by a concurrent physical illness

**A:** B) This person may be eligible under Track 2 if MAiD for mental illness has been implemented and all eligibility criteria are met, but as of March 2024 the implementation has been delayed

Bill C-7 explicitly EXCLUDED mental illness as the sole underlying condition from MAiD eligibility (a temporary exclusion in s.241.2(2.1) of the Criminal Code). The original sunset date (March 17, 2023) was extended, then extended again to March 17, 2024, and then further delayed by Parliament pending a regulatory framework and training standards. As of 2024, MAiD for mental illness alone is NOT yet legally available despite being conceptually within the expanded framework. This is a rapidly evolving area — candidates must know the current status.

**Q:** An ICU team determines that continued mechanical ventilation for a 92-year-old patient with multi-organ failure is physiologically futile — the patient will not survive to hospital discharge regardless of intervention intensity. The family demands 'everything be done.' What is the most ethically and legally defensible position?

A) The family's wishes take precedence — physicians must provide any treatment requested by a family
B) The physician has no obligation to provide treatments that are physiologically futile; the family should be counselled but consent is not required to withdraw futile treatment
C) The team must seek a court order before withdrawing mechanical ventilation
D) Withdrawal requires unanimous consent from all family members
E) A second opinion from another intensivist is mandatory before withdrawal can proceed

**A:** B) The physician has no obligation to provide treatments that are physiologically futile; the family should be counselled but consent is not required to withdraw futile treatment

Physiologic futility means the treatment CANNOT achieve its intended physiologic effect. Unlike value-laden futility (where benefit is debated), physiologically futile treatment need not be provided even if the family demands it. Canadian courts (e.g., Rasouli v. Sunnybrook, now Cuthbertson v. Rasouli at the SCC) have established that in Ontario, a physician seeking to withdraw life support over family objection must go through the Consent and Capacity Board. However, the underlying ETHICAL principle is that physicians are not obligated to provide futile treatment. Jurisdictional specifics matter in exam questions.

**Q:** Following the Supreme Court of Canada's decision in Cuthbertson v. Rasouli (2013), in Ontario, when a physician wishes to withdraw life support from an incapable patient over the objection of the SDM, the physician must:

A) Proceed with withdrawal after notifying the family 48 hours in advance
B) Obtain a court order from the Superior Court of Justice
C) Apply to the Consent and Capacity Board for a determination
D) Seek a second opinion from two independent physicians
E) File a complaint with the CPSO to obtain authorization

**A:** C) Apply to the Consent and Capacity Board for a determination

In Cuthbertson v. Rasouli, the SCC held that in Ontario, withdrawal of life support IS a 'treatment' under the Health Care Consent Act, and therefore requires either consent from the SDM or a ruling from the CONSENT AND CAPACITY BOARD (CCB) if the SDM refuses consent. This is Ontario-specific — other provinces may handle this differently. The CCB (not courts, not other physicians) is the mandated forum. This is a high-yield MCCQE1 legal fact about Ontario's unique framework.

**Q:** A 66-year-old patient with metastatic pancreatic cancer has an advance directive explicitly refusing CPR. He is found pulseless in a hospital hallway and a nurse begins CPR before seeing the bracelet. Once the DNR order is identified, CPR is stopped after 2 minutes. The patient is resuscitated but suffers anoxic brain injury. Which legal/ethical framework applies to this error?

A) The nurse committed assault by performing CPR against the advance directive
B) The advance directive creates an absolute obligation to not resuscitate in all circumstances
C) A valid DNR/advance directive refusing CPR must be honored; initiating CPR against a known directive without re-assessment may constitute battery
D) The error is excusable because the directive was not immediately visible and constitutes a good faith exception
E) No liability exists because CPR was ultimately stopped before completion

**A:** C) A valid DNR/advance directive refusing CPR must be honored; initiating CPR against a known directive without re-assessment may constitute battery

A valid, clearly documented DNR order in a hospital setting creates a legal obligation NOT to resuscitate. Initiating CPR against a known advance directive (once known) may constitute battery (unconsented touching). The 'good faith' exception may mitigate liability for the seconds before the directive was identified, but there is no general exception for system failures. The key teaching point: once the DNR is identified, CPR MUST STOP immediately. The institution bears responsibility for ensuring advance directives are prominently communicated at the bedside.

**Q:** A palliative care physician titrates a patient's intravenous morphine to control intractable dyspnea, fully aware that doses sufficient to relieve symptoms may hasten death. The patient has capacity and has consented to the treatment. This clinical situation is best described by which ethical principle?

A) This constitutes slow euthanasia and is illegal in Canada
B) This is ethically justified under the principle of double effect, provided the intent is symptom relief, not death
C) This requires a MAiD consultation as it involves intentional hastening of death
D) This is acceptable only if the patient has a DNR order in place
E) This requires consent from the patient's next of kin in addition to the patient

**A:** B) This is ethically justified under the principle of double effect, provided the intent is symptom relief, not death

The DOCTRINE OF DOUBLE EFFECT (DDE) justifies actions with both a good and a potentially harmful effect when: (1) the action is not itself immoral, (2) the agent intends the good effect only, (3) the harmful effect is foreseen but not intended, and (4) the good effect is proportionate to the risk. Palliative sedation/opioid titration for symptom control is a classic application. It is LEGALLY and ETHICALLY distinct from MAiD because intent is relief of suffering, not death. Canadian courts and medical ethics support this distinction.

**Q:** A 17-year-old with osteosarcoma and full decision-making capacity refuses limb-sparing surgery and chemotherapy, citing quality of life concerns. His parents consent on his behalf. He is cognitively intact, articulate about his values, and has received repeated counselling. In most Canadian jurisdictions, how should this be managed?

A) Parental consent overrides the minor's refusal for life-saving treatment in all cases
B) If the minor is assessed as a mature minor with capacity, his refusal carries legal weight and generally should be respected in most provinces
C) Proceed with surgery as the parents' consent legally governs treatment decisions until age 18
D) Seek a court order to override both the patient's refusal and the parents' consent
E) Refer to psychiatry to rule out depression before accepting the refusal

**A:** B) If the minor is assessed as a mature minor with capacity, his refusal carries legal weight and generally should be respected in most provinces

While the mature minor doctrine is more nuanced when a minor refuses life-saving treatment, a 17-year-old with full capacity who articulates a coherent values-based refusal carries significant legal and ethical weight. In provinces without specific statutory age of consent (e.g., outside Quebec's s.14 threshold), courts have recognized mature minors' right to refuse even life-saving treatment (see AC v. Manitoba, 2009 SCC). Courts must balance the best interests standard against the mature minor's autonomy. Blanket parental override is inappropriate. Psychiatric consultation to assess decision-making quality (not to override) is reasonable but should not be used to coerce consent.

**Q:** A capable patient with terminal lung cancer, in agreement with his family, requests a 'slow code' — a visually active but deliberately ineffective resuscitation attempt for the benefit of family members who cannot accept a full DNR. What is the most appropriate physician response?

A) Agree, as this respects family wishes and avoids confrontation
B) Slow codes are ethically impermissible as they involve deception and a breach of professional integrity
C) Agree provided the patient has provided written consent
D) This is acceptable only in acute care settings when family is present
E) Refer the request to the hospital ethics committee for approval

**A:** B) Slow codes are ethically impermissible as they involve deception and a breach of professional integrity

Slow codes are almost universally condemned in medical ethics. They constitute DECEPTION of both the family (who believe a genuine resuscitation is occurring) and violate the physician's duty of honesty. They also involve delivering a medical intervention (CPR) that the physician knows to be purposeless, which itself raises ethical issues. The correct approach is compassionate, direct family communication about the prognosis and the rationale for a DNR order. Ethics consultation may assist in navigating family dynamics. A deceptive performance is never ethically justified.

**Q:** What is the palliative approach to symptom management in end-of-life care?

**A:** Pain: WHO ladder (non-opioid, weak opioid, strong opioid). Dyspnea: low-dose opioids, fan/air. Nausea: identify cause (haloperidol, metoclopramide, ondansetron). Delirium: haloperidol. Secretions: glycopyrrolate

Palliative sedation: for refractory symptoms. Advance care planning: goals of care discussions, substitute decision-makers. MAID: eligible if grievous and irremediable medical condition (Criminal Code criteria updated 2021).

## Confidentiality & Disclosure

**Q:** When is a physician permitted to break patient confidentiality?

**A:** Mandatory reporting: communicable diseases (public health legislation), child abuse/neglect, gunshot wounds; Discretionary: imminent danger to identifiable third party (Tarasoff duty to protect), fitness to drive impairment, child protection

CMPA guidelines: confidentiality is a cornerstone of the physician-patient relationship. Exceptions require a higher ethical duty. Physicians in Canada do not have a "duty to warn" in the same way as the US Tarasoff ruling — but provinces have various discretionary and mandatory reporting laws.

**Q:** What are the mandatory reporting obligations for physicians in Ontario regarding child abuse?

**A:** ALL healthcare professionals (and others) who reasonably suspect a child under 16 (Ontario — 18 in some provinces) is being abused or neglected MUST report to the Children's Aid Society (CAS) — duty is personal and cannot be delegated

CMPA: reporting child abuse is a positive legal duty — failure to report is an offense under the Child, Youth and Family Services Act. Report any reasonable suspicion — physicians do not need certainty. Report even if child or parents ask you not to. CAS investigates; physician's role is to report, not investigate.

**Q:** A patient tells you they plan to seriously harm their ex-partner. What are your obligations?

**A:** Duty to protect: warn the potential victim, notify police, consider involuntary hospitalization — balance confidentiality against duty to protect identifiable third party from serious bodily harm

Canadian law (Tsilhqot'in v. BC, provincial legislation): no universal Tarasoff-style duty to warn in all provinces, but most colleges and CMPA guidelines support discretionary disclosure to protect identifiable third party from serious, imminent harm. Exhaust other options first (hospitalization, persuasion to seek help).

**Q:** What is the physician's obligation regarding reporting unfit drivers?

**A:** Report to Ministry of Transportation (mandatory in Ontario and most provinces) when patient has a medical condition affecting safe driving ability that the patient refuses to voluntarily stop driving or report themselves

Highway Traffic Act (Ontario): physician has mandatory duty to report patients with medical conditions affecting fitness to drive if patient refuses to self-report. Protected from liability for reporting in good faith. Advise patient of the reporting obligation first. Vision, dementia, epilepsy, cardiac conditions are common.

**Q:** What information can be disclosed to insurance companies or employers?

**A:** Only information the patient has explicitly authorized in writing — insurance companies and employers are NOT entitled to information without patient consent; physician should provide only information relevant to the request

CMPA: informed consent for disclosure to third parties is required. Patient should understand what information will be released and to whom. Physicians should exercise professional judgment about what is clinically relevant. IME (Independent Medical Examination) reports have different confidentiality rules.

**Q:** What are a patient's rights regarding access to their own medical records?

**A:** Patients have the right to access their own medical records in most circumstances — physician may withhold information if disclosure would cause direct harm (PHIPA Ontario); 30-day response time required

PHIPA (Personal Health Information Protection Act) Ontario: patient right to access records within 30 days. Physician may refuse access if: disclosure would result in risk of serious bodily harm, information is about third parties. Patient can request amendment of their record.

**Q:** What is the duty of a physician when they learn a colleague is impaired?

**A:** Report to the relevant regulatory college (CPSO in Ontario) or hospital department chief; Canadian provincial colleges have policies on physician impairment — duty to report and protect patients

CMPA and CPSO: physician health programs (e.g., PHP in Ontario) exist to support impaired physicians. Colleagues should encourage voluntary participation. If patient safety is at risk, mandatory reporting is required. CMA Code of Ethics: duty to protect public from impaired colleagues.

**Q:** A patient with HIV refuses to disclose their status to their sexual partner. What should the physician do?

**A:** Counsel patient about risk to partners; if patient refuses disclosure and continues to pose risk: notify public health (mandatory for HIV under public health legislation in most provinces); public health officer can then notify partner

Most Canadian provinces: HIV is notifiable to public health. Public health officer (not physician directly) notifies contacts if patient refuses. Physician should exhaust persuasion before notifying. Balancing confidentiality and public health protection is a legal and ethical obligation.

**Q:** What is the therapeutic exception to informed consent?

**A:** Therapeutic exception: information can be withheld if it would cause direct psychological harm sufficient to impair rational decision-making capacity — extremely narrow exception, NOT to be used to withhold information to avoid "upsetting" patient

CMPA: therapeutic exception is controversial and rarely applicable. Physician must document justification clearly. Cultural requests to withhold information must be balanced against patient autonomy. Pre-empting distress by providing information thoughtfully is preferable to invoking therapeutic exception.

**Q:** What are the obligations when a patient discloses domestic violence?

**A:** Screen for safety, offer resources (shelters, legal aid, counseling), document in chart with patient's consent, report to police ONLY if patient consents (except for mandatory reporting of child abuse if children at risk) — do NOT report without consent in adult DV

CMPA and CMA: mandatory reporting of domestic violence against competent adults does NOT exist in Canada (unlike child abuse). Respecting patient autonomy means not reporting without consent — could worsen safety if abuser is notified. Safety planning, community resources, and follow-up are the physician's role.

**Q:** What is the physician's obligation when child abuse is suspected in Canada?

**Q:** What is intimate partner violence (IPV) and what are the two validated screening tools used in Canada?

**A:** IPV includes physical, sexual, emotional, psychological, and financial abuse. Screening tools: (A) WAST-SHORT (2 questions about relationship tension and conflict resolution — either endorsed with 'a lot of tension' or 'with great difficulty' is significant); (B) HITS (Hit, Insult, Threaten, Scream — scored 1-5 each, total ≥10 is significant).

**A:** The victim is most at risk for homicide when attempting to leave the home or following separation.

**Q:** What are the mandatory reporting requirements for abuse in Canada?

**A:** Reporting of suspected or known child abuse is MANDATORY. Reporting of spousal/partner abuse is NOT mandatory without the person's permission (criminal act but not reportable). Elder abuse reporting is mandatory for those in long-term care homes.

Canadian law distinguishes between child, elder, and partner abuse in terms of mandatory reporting. Physicians must balance patient confidentiality with legal duties. All abuse-related visits should be documented with verbatim quotations for medico-legal purposes.

**Q:** What is the prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) in Canada and what groups have increased incidence?

**A:** Lifetime prevalence of IPV against women: 25-30%. Similar prevalence against men (less likely reported due to stigma). Increased incidence in: pregnancy, disabled women, transgender and non-binary individuals, bisexual individuals, 18-24 age group. 25-50% chance of child abuse when partner abuse occurs.

**Q:** In Canada, in which provinces is physician reporting of medically unfit drivers to the Ministry of Transportation mandatory vs. discretionary?

**Q:** What are the driving restrictions after a TIA, stroke, STEMI, and first unprovoked seizure in Canada?

**Q:** What are the driving restrictions for epilepsy and insulin-dependent diabetes in Canada?

These are common scenarios in clinical and ethical contexts. Diet-controlled or oral hypoglycemic agents have no restrictions in the absence of diabetes complications.

**Q:** What are the visual acuity and visual field thresholds that contraindicate driving?

**A:** Visual acuity: contraindicated if <20/50 with both eyes examined simultaneously. Visual field: contraindicated if <120° along horizontal meridian and <15° continuous above and below fixation with both eyes examined simultaneously.

Minimum visual standards ensure drivers can safely perceive hazards. Physicians should refer patients with borderline vision for ophthalmologic assessment.

**Q:** What cognitive impairment level is a contraindication to driving, and which cognitive tests can suggest impaired driving fitness?

**A:** Moderate to severe dementia is a contraindication to driving, defined as inability to independently perform 2 or more IADLs or any basic ADL. ## Elom

**Q:** What are the five principles of the Canada Health Act?

**A:** Public administration, Comprehensiveness, Universality, Portability, Accessibility.

The Canada Health Act (1984) establishes the conditions provinces must meet to receive federal health transfer payments. All five principles must be satisfied: publicly administered, covering all medically necessary services, available to all eligible residents, portable across provinces, and accessible without financial barriers.

**Q:** What are the four basic requirements of valid consent in Canadian medical law?

**A:** Voluntary, Capacity, Specific, Informed.

Valid consent requires the patient gives it freely without coercion (voluntary), has the ability to understand and appreciate the decision (capacity), consents to the specific procedure and provider (specific), and has been given sufficient information about risks, benefits, and alternatives (informed).

**Q:** What are the four principles of medical ethics (principlism)?

**A:** Autonomy, Beneficence, Non-maleficence, Justice.

Autonomy respects patients' right to make their own decisions. Beneficence obligates acting in the patient's best interest. Non-maleficence is the duty to avoid causing harm (primum non nocere). Justice requires fair distribution of benefits and harms regardless of social factors.

**Q:** In Ontario, what is the hierarchy of substitute decision-makers (SDMs) when a patient lacks capacity?

**A:** 1) Court-appointed guardian, 2) Attorney for personal care (POA), 3) Representative appointed by CCB, 4) Spouse/partner, 5) Child/parent, 6) Sibling, 7) Other relative, 8) Public guardian and trustee.

The Health Care Consent Act (HCCA) in Ontario specifies a hierarchy of SDMs. Legally appointed guardians take priority over automatic SDMs. The SDM must act according to previously expressed wishes or, if unknown, in the patient's best interest.

**Q:** What are the mandatory statutory reporting obligations that override patient confidentiality in Canada?

**A:** 1) Suspected child abuse/neglect, 2) Fitness to drive, 3) Communicable diseases, 4) Improper conduct of other health professionals, 5) Vital statistics (births/deaths), 6) Reporting to coroners.

Legislation defines specific instances where public interest overrides patient confidentiality. Failure to report can result in prosecution, penalty, and liability if a third party is harmed. The specific requirements vary by province.

**Q:** What are the criteria for Medical Assistance in Dying (MAID) eligibility under Bill C-14?

**A:** Eligible for publicly-funded health services in Canada, at least 18 years old with capacity, grievous and irremediable medical condition in advanced state of irreversible decline, suffering intolerable to the patient and not relieved under conditions they consider acceptable.

Bill C-14 (2016) legalized MAID. The 2021 update eliminated the requirement that natural death be reasonably foreseeable. For patients whose death is not reasonably foreseeable, a minimum 90-day assessment period applies. MAID eligibility for solely mental illness is postponed to March 2027 (Bill C-62).

**Q:** What conditions must be met to invoke the duty to warn/protect regarding a dangerous patient?

**A:** 1) Imminent risk, 2) To an identifiable person or group, 3) Of serious bodily harm or death.

When a patient reveals intent to harm a specific, identifiable person or group with a concrete plan and available means, the physician should notify police and potentially the victim. Canadian courts have not imposed a mandatory duty to warn, but CMA Code of Ethics and some regulatory authorities may require it.

**Q:** What four elements comprise negligence/malpractice in medical law?

**A:** 1) Duty of care existed (physician-patient relationship), 2) Breach of the standard of care, 3) Causation (breach caused harm), 4) Damages (harm occurred to the patient).

The standard of care is defined as what a reasonable, prudent physician of the same training and experience would provide under similar circumstances. The onus of proof rests with the plaintiff. An error of care is not automatically negligence — errors can occur even with reasonable care.

**Q:** What is Jordan's Principle in Canadian healthcare?

**A:** A legal obligation that First Nations children receive prompt and equitable access to healthcare services, with payment disputes between federal and provincial governments settled later.

Jordan Anderson was a First Nations child who spent two unnecessary years in hospital because governments disputed who should pay for his home care. He died at age 5 without going home. In 2016, the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal found Canada was discriminating against First Nations children by failing to implement Jordan's Principle.

**Q:** What is the difference between capacity and competence in Canadian medical law?

**A:** Capacity: clinical determination by the treating physician — the ability to understand information relevant to a treatment decision AND appreciate the reasonably foreseeable consequences. Competence: legal determination made by the courts. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific; a patient may be capable for one decision but not another.

A patient is presumed capable unless evidence suggests otherwise. Disagreement with medical advice does NOT determine incapacity. Capacity assessment requires the patient to: understand the medical problem and proposed treatment, understand alternatives, appreciate consequences of accepting/refusing treatment, and make a decision not substantially based on delusions.
