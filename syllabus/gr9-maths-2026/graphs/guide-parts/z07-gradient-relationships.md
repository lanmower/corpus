### Gradient Relationships: Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

The gradient tells us how two lines are related.

**Parallel lines** never meet, so they have exactly the same steepness: their gradients are **equal**, m₁ = m₂. For example, y = 3x + 1 and y = 3x − 4 are parallel — both have gradient 3, differing only in their y-intercepts. To test whether two lines are parallel, put each in y = mx + c form and compare the gradients. For y = 2x + 5 and 2y = 4x − 3, divide the second by 2 to get y = 2x − 3/2; both have gradient 2, so they are parallel.

To find a line parallel to y = −3x + 2 through (1;4): the gradient stays −3, so y = −3x + c; substituting (1;4) gives 4 = −3 + c, so c = 7 and the equation is y = −3x + 7.

**Perpendicular lines** cross at a right angle (90°). The **product of their gradients is −1**: m₁ × m₂ = −1. This means each gradient is the **negative reciprocal** of the other — flip the fraction and change the sign. If m₁ = 2, then m₂ = −1/2, because 2 × (−1/2) = −1. If a line has gradient 3/4, the perpendicular gradient is −4/3 (flip to 4/3, then negate); check: (3/4) × (−4/3) = −1 ✓.

**Classifying a pair.** For y = 2x + 1 and y = −(1/2)x + 4: the gradients 2 and −1/2 are not equal, so not parallel; their product is 2 × (−1/2) = −1, so they are perpendicular.

**Pitfall.** For perpendicular lines, do not forget the negative sign. The perpendicular gradient is the *negative* reciprocal, not just the reciprocal. If m₁ = 5, the perpendicular gradient is −1/5, not 1/5 — because the product must equal −1.
