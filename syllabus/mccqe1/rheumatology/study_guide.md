# Rheumatology

## Rheumatoid Arthritis & Autoimmune

**Q:** Which antibody is most specific for rheumatoid arthritis and predicts erosive disease?

**A:** Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (anti-CCP / ACPA)

Anti-CCP has ~95% specificity for RA and is present in 60–70% of RA patients. It predicts erosive, destructive joint disease and may be positive years before symptom onset. RF has lower specificity.

**Q:** A 42-year-old woman presents with 8 weeks of symmetric swollen MCPs, PIPs, and wrists with >1 hour of morning stiffness. RF and anti-CCP are positive. What is the diagnosis and first-line DMARD?

**A:** Rheumatoid arthritis; methotrexate (MTX) is first-line DMARD

Early aggressive treatment with MTX prevents joint destruction. ACR/EULAR recommend starting MTX within 3 months of diagnosis. Folic acid supplementation reduces MTX toxicity. Target is remission or low disease activity.

**Q:** What blood tests should be monitored in a patient on methotrexate for RA?

**A:** CBC, LFTs, creatinine every 4–8 weeks initially, then every 3 months

MTX causes hepatotoxicity, myelosuppression, and nephrotoxicity. Alcohol use and other hepatotoxins increase risk. Monitoring guidelines: CBC + differential, AST/ALT, creatinine per ACR/CORA recommendations.

**Q:** A patient with RA has failed methotrexate monotherapy after 3 months. Disease activity remains moderate. What is the next step per Canadian guidelines?

**A:** Add a biologic DMARD (e.g., TNF inhibitor) or targeted synthetic DMARD (e.g., JAK inhibitor) in combination with MTX

CORA (Canadian Rheumatology Association) guidelines recommend combination therapy when target (remission/low disease activity) is not achieved at 3 months. TNFi (etanercept, adalimumab) plus MTX is most evidence-based combination.

**Q:** What is the recommended screening before starting a TNF inhibitor for RA?

**A:** TB screening (TST or IGRA), hepatitis B serology, and updated vaccinations (especially live vaccines before starting biologics)

TNFi increase risk of reactivation TB and hepatitis B. All patients must be screened for latent TB (treat if positive before starting biologic) and hepatitis B (antiviral prophylaxis if HBsAg+). Live vaccines must be given before biologics.

**Q:** Which extra-articular manifestation of RA is the most common cause of premature death?

**A:** Cardiovascular disease (accelerated atherosclerosis)

RA is associated with a 1.5–2x increased CV risk independent of traditional risk factors. Systemic inflammation promotes atherosclerosis. Aggressive disease control and CV risk factor management are essential.

**Q:** What is Felty syndrome?

**A:** RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia (with recurrent infections)

Felty syndrome occurs in <1% of RA patients, usually with severe seropositive erosive disease. It increases risk of serious infections and lymphoma. Treatment includes aggressive RA control; G-CSF for severe neutropenia.

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman with RA on biologics develops a dry cough and dyspnea. CXR shows bilateral lower lobe ground-glass opacities. What should be considered?

**A:** Methotrexate pneumonitis or rheumatoid interstitial lung disease (ILD)

Both MTX (hypersensitivity pneumonitis) and RA-ILD can present this way. Discontinue MTX. HRCT chest and pulmonology referral are needed. RA-ILD (UIP pattern) may require antifibrotics (nintedanib) or immunosuppression.

**Q:** Which DAS28 score indicates remission in rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** DAS28 <2.6

The Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28) uses tender/swollen joint counts, ESR or CRP, and patient global assessment. Scores: <2.6 = remission, 2.6–3.2 = low activity, 3.2–5.1 = moderate, >5.1 = high activity. Treat-to-target aims for DAS28 <2.6.

**Q:** What is the leading cause of atlantoaxial subluxation in patients with long-standing RA?

**A:** Erosion of the transverse ligament and odontoid process by chronic synovitis of C1-C2 joint

Atlantoaxial instability (C1-C2) occurs in 30–40% of RA patients with long disease duration and can cause cervical myelopathy. Flexion-extension cervical X-rays screen for it. Pre-operative cervical spine assessment is essential before intubation.

**Q:** What are the ACR/EULAR 2010 RA classification criteria?

**A:** Score >=6/10: joint involvement (0-5), serology RF/ACPA (0-3), acute phase reactants (0-1), duration >=6wks (1). Must have definite synovitis in >=1 joint

First-line treatment: methotrexate (anchor DMARD). Target: remission or low disease activity within 6 months. Add biologic (anti-TNF, abatacept, tocilizumab) if inadequate response.

**Q:** What are the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for RA?

**A:** Score >=6/10: Joint involvement (0-5), Serology RF/anti-CCP (0-3), Acute phase ESR/CRP (0-1), Duration >=6wk (0-1). Requires at least 1 swollen joint not explained otherwise

Early RA diagnosis critical - treat within window of opportunity (first 3-6 months). Anti-CCP >95% specific. Seronegative RA exists. CRA 2012 guidelines recommend early aggressive DMARD therapy.

**Q:** What is the treat-to-target approach in RA?

**A:** DMARD within 3mo of diagnosis. Target remission or low disease activity by 6mo. MTX first-line 15-25mg/wk + folic acid. If inadequate at 3mo: add/switch DMARD or biologic

CRA 2012. Monitor CBC/LFTs/Cr q4-8wk on MTX. Biologic options: TNFi, abatacept, tocilizumab, rituximab, JAK inhibitors. Bridge with short-course prednisone <=7.5mg while waiting for DMARD effect.

**Q:** What are the conventional DMARDs and their key monitoring?

**A:** MTX: hepatotoxicity, myelosuppression (CBC/LFTs). Sulfasalazine: GI, myelosuppression. Leflunomide: hepatotoxicity, teratogenic. HCQ: retinal toxicity (eye exam after 5yr)

All take 6-12wk for effect. MTX contraindicated in pregnancy, liver disease, renal impairment. Folic acid reduces MTX side effects. Leflunomide requires washout with cholestyramine if pregnancy desired.

**Q:** What biologics are used in RA and what pre-screening is required?

**A:** TNFi, IL-6i (tocilizumab), abatacept, rituximab, JAKi (tofacitinib). Pre-screen: TB (IGRA), HBV/HCV, CXR, update vaccines. Avoid live vaccines on biologics

Treat latent TB before starting. TNFi CI in Class III/IV HF and demyelinating disease. JAKi: VTE and malignancy concerns (ORAL Surveillance trial). Biologics increase infection risk significantly.

**Q:** What are the extra-articular manifestations of RA?

**A:** Rheumatoid nodules, ILD, pericarditis/pleuritis, scleritis, vasculitis, Felty syndrome (splenomegaly+neutropenia), atlantoaxial subluxation, accelerated atherosclerosis

1.5-2x CV mortality. Screen CV risk factors. ILD in ~10%. C-spine XR before surgery. Secondary amyloidosis in poorly controlled disease. Cervical myelopathy risk with atlantoaxial instability.

**Q:** How do RA and OA differ clinically?

**A:** RA: symmetric, MCP/PIP/wrist, stiffness >60min, inflammatory, soft tissue swelling. OA: asymmetric, DIP/CMC/knee/hip, stiffness <30min, bony enlargement, non-inflammatory

RA spares DIP (DIP = OA or PsA). RA: ulnar deviation, swan-neck, boutonniere. OA: Heberden (DIP) and Bouchard (PIP) nodes. XR: RA=erosions+periarticular osteopenia; OA=osteophytes+joint space narrowing.

**Q:** How is an RA flare managed?

**A:** Short-course prednisone 10-15mg taper 2-4wk, IA steroid for monoarticular flare, optimize DMARD, assess adherence. Rule out septic arthritis in acute monoarthritis

Avoid long-term glucocorticoids. >=2 flares on current regimen: escalate therapy. NSAIDs for symptoms but no disease modification. Septic joint is always in differential for acute monoarticular flare.

**Q:** What perioperative considerations exist for RA patients on DMARDs?

**A:** MTX: continue (reduces flare). Biologics: hold 2-5 half-lives pre-surgery. C-spine XR for intubation safety. Stress-dose steroids if on chronic prednisone. Resume biologics when wound healed

CRA/ACR perioperative guidelines. Infection risk increased post-surgery on biologics. Flexion-extension C-spine views assess atlantoaxial stability. Fragile skin and osteoporosis considerations.

**Q:** What is the management of acute gout vs chronic gout prevention?

**A:** Acute: NSAIDs or colchicine (1.2mg then 0.6mg at 1h) or prednisone 30-40mg x5d. Do NOT start/stop allopurinol during flare. Chronic: allopurinol start 100mg titrate to urate <360 umol/L

Crystal confirmation gold standard (negatively birefringent MSU). Allopurinol start low (50mg if CKD). Febuxostat if intolerant. Colchicine prophylaxis 0.6mg daily x6mo when starting ULT. Lifestyle: weight loss, limit alcohol/purines.

**Q:** How is pseudogout (CPPD) differentiated from gout?

**A:** CPPD: positively birefringent rhomboid crystals, chondrocalcinosis on XR, large joints (knee/wrist). No ULT equivalent. Treat acute: NSAIDs/colchicine/steroids. If <60: investigate metabolic causes (hyperPTH, hemochromatosis)

Unlike gout no disease-modifying therapy exists. Chronic CPPD can mimic RA or OA. Associated: hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, hypomagnesemia. Joint aspiration is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

**Q:** What is the difference between psoriatic arthritis and rheumatoid arthritis regarding nail changes?

**A:** Psoriatic arthritis (affecting 20-30% of psoriasis patients) is associated with nail involvement (pitting, onycholysis, oil spots). Nail or scalp psoriasis increases risk of developing psoriatic arthritis. RA does not typically cause nail changes.

Nail involvement is a key predictor of psoriatic arthritis development in psoriasis patients. Psoriatic arthritis can present with asymmetric oligoarthritis, DIP joint involvement, dactylitis, and enthesitis — features that distinguish it from RA.

**Q:** What skin findings are associated with reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome)?

**A:** Keratoderma blennorrhagica (on feet) and balanitis circinata (on male penis).

Reactive arthritis classically follows GI or genitourinary infection and presents with the triad of urethritis, arthritis, and conjunctivitis. The skin findings are characteristic and help distinguish it from other seronegative arthropathies.

**Q:** What are the features of giant cell arteritis (temporal arteritis) and what is the gold standard test?

**A:** Vasculitis of large and mid-sized arteries; female:male = 3:1; most common age >70 yr. Features: headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, arthralgia, myalgia, fever, malaise, weight loss. Exam: tender temporal artery, RAPD, optic disc edema. Labs: elevated ESR, elevated CRP. Gold standard: temporal artery biopsy. Associated with polymyalgia rheumatica. Treatment: high-dose steroids immediately — do NOT wait for biopsy results.

Untreated GCA can cause sudden irreversible blindness (from anterior ischemic optic neuropathy). High-dose prednisone (40–60 mg/day) must be started immediately when GCA is clinically suspected. Biopsy should ideally be done within 1–2 weeks of starting steroids but results don't change the decision to treat.

**Q:** What features distinguish inflammatory from non-inflammatory joint pain?

**A:** Inflammatory: morning stiffness >30 min, pain/stiffness that eases with activity, mid-day fatigue, soft tissue swelling. Non-inflammatory: morning stiffness <30 min, stiffness short-lived after inactivity, increasing pain with activity.

This distinction guides the differential diagnosis. Inflammatory arthritis includes RA, crystalline arthritis (gout, pseudogout), reactive arthritis, and septic arthritis. Non-inflammatory includes osteoarthritis and mechanical causes. The pattern of joint involvement (mono vs. polyarticular, symmetric vs. asymmetric) further refines the differential.

**Q:** What is the ED management of a septic joint?

**A:** Empiric IV antibiotics PLUS orthopedic consultation for joint decompression and drainage. Joint aspirate should be sent for: culture, WBC, polymorphonuclear cells, glucose, Gram stain, and crystals.

Septic arthritis (usually Staph aureus, or Neisseria gonorrhoeae in sexually active young adults) is a medical emergency. Delay in drainage causes irreversible cartilage damage. WBC >50,000/μL with >75% PMNs strongly suggests septic arthritis; crystals suggest gout/pseudogout but do not exclude concurrent infection.

## Osteoarthritis & Gout

**Q:** What are the characteristic X-ray features of osteoarthritis in a hand joint?

**A:** Joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, osteophytes (bone spurs), and subchondral cysts — no periarticular osteopenia

OA affects cartilage and subchondral bone without systemic inflammation. This contrasts with RA (periarticular osteopenia, uniform joint space loss, erosions) and gout (punched-out erosions with overhanging edges).

**Q:** A 65-year-old woman has knee pain that worsens with activity and improves with rest. Examination shows bony enlargement, crepitus, and decreased ROM. What is the most appropriate initial therapy?

**A:** Exercise therapy (aerobic + strengthening) and weight loss for overweight patients

Exercise is the cornerstone of OA management per OARSI and Canadian guidelines. Strengthening the quadriceps reduces knee load. Weight loss of 10% significantly reduces knee OA symptoms. Topical NSAIDs are preferred over oral in elderly.

**Q:** Which joints are characteristically involved in primary nodal osteoarthritis?

**A:** DIP joints (Heberden nodes), PIP joints (Bouchard nodes), and CMC-1 (base of thumb)

Nodal OA is the most common form in women post-menopause. DIP and PIP bony enlargements (osteophytes) are characteristic. CMC-1 OA causes "square hand" deformity. These joints are typically spared in RA.

**Q:** What is the preferred analgesic for osteoarthritis pain in elderly patients?

**A:** Acetaminophen up to 3–4 g/day (or 2 g/day in heavy alcohol users) as first-line; topical diclofenac as adjunct

Oral NSAIDs carry GI, renal, and CV risks in elderly. Topical NSAIDs (diclofenac gel) provide similar analgesia to oral NSAIDs with less systemic absorption. Opioids are third-line only for refractory OA not eligible for surgery.

**Q:** Intra-articular corticosteroid injection is used for knee OA. What is the maximum recommended frequency?

**A:** No more than 3–4 times per year in the same joint

Frequent intra-articular steroid injections accelerate cartilage loss. Short-term pain relief (2–8 weeks) is the goal. Patients with inflammatory flares (effusions, warmth) respond best. Hyaluronic acid injections have modest evidence.

**Q:** What criteria indicate referral for total knee replacement (TKR)?

**A:** Moderate-severe OA with functional limitation, inadequate pain relief despite 3+ months of conservative therapy, and patient willing/fit for surgery

TKR improves pain and function in end-stage knee OA. Patient readiness and fitness (cardiac, metabolic optimization) are key. Average Canadian wait time exceeds 6 months; timely referral is essential.

**Q:** Which risk factor most strongly predicts progression from normal to symptomatic knee OA?

**A:** Obesity (BMI >30)

Each unit increase in BMI increases knee OA risk significantly. Adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory adipokines (leptin, adiponectin) that directly affect cartilage. Weight loss is the only intervention shown to slow radiographic OA progression.

**Q:** What is calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) disease and how does it present acutely?

**A:** Crystal arthropathy caused by calcium pyrophosphate crystals; acute CPPD (pseudogout) presents as acute mono-/oligoarthritis most commonly in the knee with chondrocalcinosis on X-ray

CPPD/pseudogout is often triggered by illness or surgery. Synovial fluid shows positively birefringent (weakly) rhomboid-shaped crystals under polarized microscopy. Treatment: NSAIDs, colchicine, or intra-articular steroids.

**Q:** What is the role of glucosamine/chondroitin supplements in OA management per Canadian guidelines?

**A:** Not routinely recommended — evidence for pain relief and structural benefit is insufficient

OARSI 2019 guidelines: glucosamine/chondroitin are not recommended due to inconsistent evidence. Some patients report symptomatic benefit. They are safe but should not replace evidence-based therapies.

**Q:** A 70-year-old woman with known knee OA presents with a large, tense knee effusion after a fall. Aspiration reveals turbid fluid with 40,000 WBC and positive Gram stain. What is the diagnosis?

**A:** Septic arthritis (bacterial joint infection) — not OA flare

Septic arthritis requires urgent joint drainage and IV antibiotics. S. aureus is the most common organism. High WBC (>50,000 strongly suggests infection), positive Gram stain, or positive culture confirms diagnosis. Treat as emergency.

**Q:** What is acute gout management?

**A:** NSAIDs (indomethacin/naproxen), colchicine (1.2mg then 0.6mg 1h later), or corticosteroids. DO NOT start/change ULT during acute flare

ULT (allopurinol, febuxostat) starts after flare resolution, with prophylaxis (colchicine 0.6mg daily x6 months). Target urate <360umol/L. Allopurinol start low (100mg), titrate slowly.

**Q:** What are the clinical and radiographic features of OA?

**A:** Clinical: pain with activity, stiffness <30min, bony enlargement, crepitus. XR: joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts. Labs normal

Clinical diagnosis - XR not required. Severity of XR changes correlates poorly with symptoms. Common joints: knee, hip, hand (DIP>PIP, 1st CMC), spine. No blood markers for OA.

**Q:** What is the stepwise management of knee OA?

**A:** 1st: exercise + weight loss + education. 2nd: topical NSAIDs, acetaminophen. 3rd: oral NSAIDs (lowest dose/shortest). 4th: IA corticosteroid. 5th: TKA. No evidence for glucosamine or IA hyaluronic acid

Exercise is most effective non-surgical intervention. 5-10% weight loss significantly reduces symptoms. Max 3-4 IA steroid/year/joint. OARSI guidelines. Physiotherapy referral for structured exercise program.

**Q:** When is joint replacement indicated for OA?

**A:** Severe pain limiting function despite optimal conservative treatment, significant QoL impact, radiographic severe joint destruction. No age cutoff. Pre-op: weight loss, smoking cessation, prehab

Prosthesis lasts 15-20yr. Infection risk 1-2%. DVT prophylaxis post-op. Periprosthetic joint infection: urgent orthopedic referral. BMI >40 increases surgical risk.

**Q:** What are the modifiable risk factors for OA?

**A:** Obesity (strongest modifiable - 4kg less knee load per 1kg weight loss), joint injury (ACL tear = 4-6x OA risk), occupational repetitive loading, malalignment, muscle weakness

Exercise does NOT cause OA in normal joints. Quadriceps strengthening protects knee. No pharmacological prevention. Weight management is most impactful modifiable intervention.

**Q:** What are the risks of NSAIDs in OA management?

**A:** GI: bleeding 2-4x (add PPI if >=65 or history). CV: increased events (naproxen safest). Renal: avoid eGFR<30. HTN worsening. Celecoxib: lower GI risk. Topical NSAIDs: minimal systemic risk (first-line)

Avoid NSAIDs + anticoagulants. Avoid in HF. Short courses preferred. Drug interactions: warfarin, lithium, methotrexate, antihypertensives. Topical diclofenac gel effective with minimal systemic absorption.

**Q:** What is erosive OA?

**A:** Inflammatory OA subtype: DIP/PIP, central erosions (gull-wing pattern on XR), intermittent inflammatory flares, female predominance. Differentiate from RA (DIP, no RF/CCP) and PsA (no skin/nail findings)

Erosive OA: central (not marginal) erosions distinguishes from RA. Management: same as OA + colchicine or HCQ for inflammatory flares. Self-limited inflammatory episodes. Does not respond to DMARDs.

**Q:** What is the role of IA injections in OA?

**A:** Corticosteroid: short-term relief 4-8wk, max 3-4/yr/joint. Hyaluronic acid: NOT recommended. PRP: insufficient evidence. US guidance improves accuracy especially for hip

IA steroid for acute flare. Repeated injections may accelerate cartilage loss. Avoid if septic joint suspected. Post-injection flare 24-48h possible. Monitor glucose in diabetics for 48-72h.

**Q:** What non-pharmacological treatments have best evidence in OA?

**A:** Strong: land-based exercise, aquatic exercise, weight loss, self-management. Moderate: assistive devices (cane, brace), PT, OT. Weak: acupuncture, TENS

Exercise adherence is main challenge. Group programs, telehealth improve adherence. Quadriceps for knee, hip strengthening for hip OA. Cane in contralateral hand. OT for hand OA (splints, adaptive devices).

**Q:** How is hand OA managed?

**A:** Splinting (thumb CMC), exercises, topical NSAIDs (first-line Rx), acetaminophen, oral NSAIDs short-term. Surgery for severe 1st CMC. OT for adaptive devices

1st CMC very common and debilitating. Thumb spica splint provides relief. Heberden (DIP) and Bouchard (PIP) nodes are bony. No DMARDs for hand OA. Avoid disuse from over-splinting.

**Q:** What is the differential for acute monoarthritis?

**A:** Septic arthritis (emergency), gout, CPPD, trauma, reactive arthritis, OA flare, hemarthrosis. MUST aspirate if infection possible: WBC, crystals, gram stain, culture

Septic: >50k WBC, >75% PMN. Gout: neg birefringent needles. CPPD: pos birefringent rhomboids. Inflammatory: 2-50k WBC. Non-inflammatory: <2k. Never miss septic arthritis.

**Q:** A 50-year-old male presents with acute monoarthritis of the first MTP joint with severe pain, redness, and swelling. He recently started hydrochlorothiazide. What is the most likely diagnosis and definitive diagnostic test?

**A:** Acute gout (podagra). Definitive diagnosis: joint aspiration showing negatively birefringent, needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals under polarized light microscopy. Synovial fluid will show inflammatory WBC count (>2000-50,000). Acute treatment: NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids. Thiazide diuretics are a common precipitant as they reduce urate excretion.

The first MTP joint is the classic location for gout (podagra), though any joint can be affected. Joint aspiration is essential to differentiate gout from septic arthritis, which is a medical emergency. Serum uric acid may be paradoxically normal or even low during an acute flare and should not be used to rule out gout.

**Q:** How do you differentiate septic arthritis from gout and pseudogout in a patient with acute monoarthritis?

**A:** All three: joint aspiration is MANDATORY. Septic arthritis: WBC >50,000 (often >100,000), >90% PMNs, positive Gram stain/culture, no crystals. Gout: negatively birefringent needle-shaped crystals. Pseudogout (CPPD): positively birefringent rhomboid-shaped crystals, chondrocalcinosis on X-ray. Septic arthritis is the most dangerous — if suspected, treat empirically with IV antibiotics pending culture.

Septic arthritis and crystal arthropathy can coexist, so the presence of crystals does not exclude infection. A hot, swollen joint with fever and markedly elevated WBC count in the synovial fluid should be treated as septic arthritis until cultures are finalized. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism in native joints.

**Q:** A 25-year-old sexually active male presents with acute monoarthritis of the knee, fever, and skin pustules on an erythematous base. What is the most likely diagnosis?

**A:** Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI). The classic triad is migratory polyarthralgia → monoarthritis, tenosynovitis, and dermatitis (painless papules/pustules). Diagnosis: joint aspiration for Gram stain/culture plus NAAT from urogenital, rectal, and pharyngeal sites. Treat with IV ceftriaxone followed by oral cefixime, plus treat for concurrent chlamydia.

Gonococcal arthritis is the most common cause of septic arthritis in sexually active young adults. Joint fluid cultures are positive in only 25-50% of cases, so NAAT testing from mucosal sites improves diagnostic yield. All sexual partners within 60 days should be treated, and the patient should be tested for other STIs including HIV.

**Q:** What is fibromyalgia and how is it diagnosed?

**A:** Widespread pain >=3 months + widespread pain index (WPI) >=7 + symptom severity scale (SSS) >=5, OR WPI 4-6 + SSS >=9. No alternative explanation. Associated: fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, sleep disturbance

Central sensitization disorder. Treatment: exercise (strongest evidence), CBT, duloxetine/milnacipran (SNRIs), pregabalin/gabapentin. Avoid opioids. Multimodal approach essential. Reassurance and education.

**Q:** What are the ACR criteria for fibromyalgia and what is the current treatment approach?

**A:** ACR 2010/2016 criteria: Widespread Pain Index (WPI) ≥7 + Symptom Severity Score (SSS) ≥5, OR WPI 4-6 + SSS ≥9; symptoms present ≥3 months; NOT better explained by another diagnosis. Key symptoms: widespread pain, fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive difficulties ('fibro fog'), headaches, IBS symptoms, depression/anxiety. Treatment: Education (validate symptoms, explain central sensitization); Exercise (aerobic + strengthening — strongest evidence); Pharmacotherapy: duloxetine 60 mg/day (SNRI), pregabalin 150-450 mg/day, milnacipran (SNRI), amitriptyline low-dose (10-50 mg); CBT for pain management; Sleep hygiene; Avoid opioids (ineffective, risk of dependence).

Fibromyalgia is a central sensitization syndrome — the CNS amplifies pain signals. It affects ~2-4% of the population (predominantly female). Canadian Pain Society guidelines emphasize multimodal treatment combining exercise, education, and pharmacotherapy. Duloxetine and pregabalin are the only Health Canada-approved medications specifically for fibromyalgia.

**Q:** What is the ED management of an acute gout flare (crystalline synovitis)?

**A:** NSAIDs at high dose (first-line), colchicine within first 24 hours, or corticosteroids. Do NOT start allopurinol during an acute flare — it may worsen the attack.

Gout is caused by monosodium urate crystal deposition. Colchicine is most effective within the first 24–36 hours of a flare. Allopurinol (urate-lowering therapy) changes serum urate levels, which can mobilize crystals and prolong or worsen an acute flare — it should only be started 2–4 weeks after resolution, with concurrent prophylaxis.

## SLE & Connective Tissue Disease

**Q:** A 28-year-old woman presents with a malar rash, arthritis, proteinuria, and a positive ANA (1:320). Which antibody is most specific for SLE?

**A:** Anti-dsDNA (anti-double-stranded DNA antibody)

Anti-dsDNA has ~95% specificity for SLE and correlates with disease activity and lupus nephritis. Anti-Sm is also highly specific but less sensitive. ANA is sensitive (>95%) but not specific.

**Q:** Which organ involvement in SLE most significantly impacts long-term prognosis?

**A:** Lupus nephritis (renal involvement)

Lupus nephritis occurs in 50% of SLE patients and is a major cause of morbidity and mortality. Class III/IV proliferative nephritis requires aggressive immunosuppression (hydroxychloroquine + mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide + high-dose steroids).

**Q:** What is the mechanism of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) complicating SLE?

**A:** Antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, anti-β2GP1) cause hypercoagulability leading to arterial/venous thrombosis and pregnancy loss

APS in SLE increases risk of DVT, stroke, and recurrent miscarriage. Diagnosis requires a clinical event plus persistent positive antibody test >12 weeks apart. Treatment: long-term anticoagulation with warfarin.

**Q:** Which drug is recommended for all patients with SLE regardless of disease severity?

**A:** Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)

HCQ reduces SLE flares, organ damage accrual, thrombosis risk, and improves pregnancy outcomes. It is recommended indefinitely for all SLE patients. Annual ophthalmology screening for retinopathy is required.

**Q:** A 25-year-old woman with SLE is planning pregnancy. Which antibody predicts risk of neonatal lupus and congenital heart block?

**A:** Anti-Ro/SSA (and anti-La/SSB) antibodies

Anti-Ro antibodies cross the placenta and can cause neonatal lupus (transient rash, cytopenias) and congenital complete heart block (irreversible). Fetal cardiac monitoring by echocardiography is recommended from 16–28 weeks in anti-Ro+ mothers.

**Q:** What are the SLEDAI criteria used for?

**A:** Scoring SLE disease activity (SLE Disease Activity Index)

SLEDAI assesses 24 clinical and laboratory features across 9 organ systems. Scores >4 indicate active disease requiring treatment adjustment. It is used in clinical care and research to monitor disease activity over time.

**Q:** A patient with SLE develops a butterfly rash flare 3 days after starting trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for a UTI. What phenomenon is this?

**A:** Drug-induced lupus or SLE flare triggered by sulfonamide antibiotic

Sulfonamides, procainamide, hydralazine, and minocycline can trigger drug-induced lupus or exacerbate SLE. Nitrofurantoin or cephalosporins are safer UTI treatment options in SLE patients.

**Q:** What are the SLICC criteria for SLE diagnosis?

**A:** >=4 of 11 clinical criteria (including malar rash, discoid, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, arthritis, serositis, renal, neuro, hematologic, immunologic, ANA) OR biopsy-proven lupus nephritis + ANA or anti-dsDNA

Anti-dsDNA specific for SLE and correlates with nephritis activity. Anti-Smith most specific. Complement C3/C4 low in active disease. Hydroxychloroquine for all SLE patients (reduces flares, thrombosis, mortality).

**Q:** What are the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for SLE?

**A:** Entry: ANA >=1:80. Score domains (>=10): hematologic, neuropsych, mucocutaneous, serosal, MSK, renal + immunology (anti-dsDNA, complement, aPL). Each criterion not attributable to other diagnosis

ANA 98% sensitive, not specific. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith are specific. Weighted scoring replaced 1997 criteria. Anti-dsDNA: monitor for flare (rising titer). Low complement: active disease.

**Q:** How is SLE managed by severity?

**A:** Mild (skin/joints): HCQ + NSAIDs + topical steroids. Moderate (serositis/cytopenias): + low-dose pred + MTX/AZA. Severe (nephritis/CNS): + high-dose steroids + MMF or cyclophosphamide + belimumab

HCQ for ALL SLE: reduces flares, damage, mortality, thrombosis. 5mg/kg/day. Eye exam after 5yr. Lupus nephritis: biopsy to classify, Class III/IV need aggressive IS. Voclosporin newer option for nephritis.

**Q:** What lab monitoring is needed in SLE?

**A:** CBC (cytopenias), Cr, urinalysis + uPCR, C3/C4 (low=active), anti-dsDNA (rising=flare), ESR. Active nephritis: low complement + rising dsDNA + active sediment (RBC casts, proteinuria)

SLEDAI for disease activity. Drug monitoring: CBC for AZA/MTX, LFTs for MTX, eye exam for HCQ. Distinguish flare from infection: procalcitonin may help. Annual: lipids, glucose (accelerated atherosclerosis).

**Q:** What are the major complications of SLE?

**A:** Nephritis (50%), accelerated atherosclerosis (leading late death), infections (leading early death), AVN (steroids), APS (20-30%), lymphoma risk

CV disease 5-10x. Manage CV risk aggressively. Pregnancy: high-risk, plan when quiescent x6mo. Anti-Ro/La: neonatal lupus risk, congenital heart block 2%.

**Q:** What is antiphospholipid syndrome and how is it managed?

**A:** Clinical (thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity) + lab (LAC, aCL, anti-B2GP x2 tests 12wk apart). Treatment: lifelong warfarin (NOT DOACs per TRAPS trial). Pregnancy: LMWH + ASA

APS primary or secondary to SLE. INR 2-3 venous, consider 3-4 arterial. Catastrophic APS: multi-organ failure, treat with plasma exchange + anticoag + steroids + IVIG. DOACs inferior to warfarin.

**Q:** How is SLE managed in pregnancy?

**A:** Continue HCQ (safe, protective). Stop MTX/MMF/CYC (teratogenic). Use AZA if IS needed. ASA from 12wk. Monitor anti-Ro (2% CHB risk, fetal HR from 16wk). Plan when quiescent >=6mo

Flare vs preeclampsia: low complement + active sediment = flare. High-risk: preeclampsia, flare, IUGR, preterm. Multidisciplinary care. Breastfeeding safe with HCQ, AZA, prednisone, NSAIDs.

**Q:** What is drug-induced lupus?

**A:** Triggers: hydralazine, procainamide, INH, minocycline, TNFi. Features: arthralgia, serositis, no renal/CNS. Anti-histone Ab+ (>95%), anti-dsDNA usually negative. Resolves on discontinuation

Milder than SLE. No nephritis or significant cytopenias. Symptoms resolve weeks-months after stopping drug. Short-course NSAIDs or low-dose prednisone for symptom control if needed.

**Q:** What skin manifestations occur in SLE?

**A:** Malar rash (spares nasolabial folds), discoid (scarring), photosensitivity, oral ulcers (usually painless), alopecia, Raynaud. Subacute cutaneous: annular, anti-Ro+, photosensitive

Malar vs rosacea (involves nasolabial folds) vs seborrheic dermatitis. Discoid can occur without systemic SLE (5-10% progress). Livedo reticularis suggests APS. Bullous lupus with vesicles/bullae.

**Q:** What is lupus nephritis classification and treatment?

**A:** ISN/RPS I-VI. Class III/IV (proliferative): induction with MMF or CYC + steroids, maintenance with MMF/AZA. Class V (membranous): treat if nephrotic. Add belimumab or voclosporin

Biopsy essential for classification. Class IV worst prognosis. Target: proteinuria <0.5-0.8g/d by 12mo. 10-30% progress to ESRD. Monitor creatinine, proteinuria, complement, anti-dsDNA serially.

**Q:** What infection risks exist in immunosuppressed SLE patients?

**A:** Increased: bacterial (pneumonia, UTI), herpes zoster, PCP, CMV, TB. Vaccinate before IS (no live vaccines on IS). PCP prophylaxis with TMP-SMX on CYC or high-dose steroids

Infections = leading cause early SLE death. Screen latent TB before biologics/CYC. Update pneumococcal, influenza, COVID, HBV. Shingrix (non-live) safe on IS. Fever: distinguish flare vs infection.

**Q:** Classify the vasculitides and describe the key features of GPA, GCA, PAN, and IgA vasculitis.

**A:** Classification by vessel size: Large: GCA, Takayasu; Medium: PAN, Kawasaki; Small (ANCA-associated): GPA, MPA, EGPA; Small (immune complex): IgA vasculitis, cryoglobulinemic. GPA (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, formerly Wegener): c-ANCA/PR3+, triad of upper airway (sinusitis, saddle nose, epistaxis), lungs (nodules, cavities, hemorrhage), kidneys (RPGN); Tx: cyclophosphamide + glucocorticoids induction, then rituximab or azathioprine maintenance. GCA (giant cell arteritis): age >50, temporal headache, jaw claudication, visual loss (EMERGENCY — can cause bilateral blindness), elevated ESR/CRP; Tx: high-dose prednisone immediately (do NOT wait for biopsy), temporal artery biopsy. PAN: medium vessel, NO glomerulonephritis, associated with HBV, mesenteric ischemia, mononeuritis multiplex, renal artery microaneurysms; ANCA-negative. IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schonlein purpura): palpable purpura (buttocks/legs), arthritis, abdominal pain, renal involvement (IgA nephropathy); mostly children.

ANCA testing is critical for small vessel vasculitis: c-ANCA/PR3 = GPA, p-ANCA/MPO = MPA/EGPA. GCA is the most common systemic vasculitis in adults >50. Temporal artery biopsy should be performed within 2 weeks of starting steroids (inflammation persists). Canadian Vasculitis Research Network (CanVasc) provides national treatment guidelines.

**Q:** What are the treatment principles for ANCA-associated vasculitis (GPA, MPA)?

**A:** Induction (severe/organ-threatening): rituximab (anti-CD20) OR cyclophosphamide + high-dose glucocorticoids (pulse IV methylprednisolone for severe disease, then prednisone taper). Plasma exchange (PLEX): for severe RPGN or pulmonary hemorrhage (evidence evolving — PEXIVAS trial showed no significant benefit for mortality but may help in select cases). Maintenance: rituximab (preferred) or azathioprine for ≥2 years after remission; methotrexate alternative for non-renal GPA. Adjuncts: TMP-SMX for Pneumocystis prophylaxis (all patients on immunosuppression), pneumococcal/influenza vaccines. Monitoring: ANCA titers (can guide relapse risk), renal function, urinalysis.

The RAVE trial established rituximab as non-inferior to cyclophosphamide for induction, with better outcomes in relapsing disease. CanVasc consensus guidelines recommend rituximab for maintenance. GPA has a high relapse rate (~50% within 5 years) — long-term monitoring is essential. TMP-SMX may also help prevent relapse in upper airway GPA (in addition to PJP prophylaxis).

**Q:** What nail finding is pathognomonic for scleroderma (systemic sclerosis)?

**A:** Nail fold telangiectasias (periungual telangiectasias with cuticular hemorrhages and capillary changes). Also: pterygium inversum unguis (distal nail plate does not separate from nail bed).

Nailfold capillaroscopy showing giant capillaries and capillary loss is a key diagnostic finding in scleroderma. These nail changes also appear in SLE and dermatomyositis.

**Q:** What skin manifestation is classic for Behçet's disease?

**A:** Painful aphthous ulcers in oral cavity AND genital mucous membranes, plus erythema nodosum and acneiform papules.

Behçet disease is a systemic vasculitis. The combination of oral and genital ulcers is the hallmark. Other features include uveitis, arthritis, and neurological involvement. No definitive diagnostic test exists.

**Q:** What are the classic skin findings of dermatomyositis?

**A:** Heliotrope rash (periorbital violaceous erythema with edema), Gottron's papules (violaceous flat-topped papules over knuckles), periungual erythema/telangiectasia, photosensitive erythema over extensor surfaces, calcinosis cutis.

Dermatomyositis is associated with malignancy (lung, breast, ovary) in adults, so cancer screening is essential at diagnosis. Anti-Mi-2 antibodies are specific; anti-MDA5 correlates with ILD.

**Q:** What skin manifestations are associated with SLE?

**A:** Malar (butterfly) rash, discoid rash (erythematous plaques with keratotic scale, follicular plugging, atrophic scarring), photosensitivity, palpable purpura, alopecia, mucosal ulcers, hemorrhagic bullae.

The malar rash spares the nasolabial folds (distinguishing from rosacea and seborrheic dermatitis). Discoid lupus can cause permanent scarring alopecia. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies are specific for SLE.

**Q:** What are the skin manifestations of scleroderma/systemic sclerosis?

**A:** Raynaud's phenomenon, non-pitting edema, digital ulcers, cutaneous calcification (calcinosis cutis), periungual telangiectasias, acrosclerosis, salt-and-pepper pigmentation. Morphea: localized scleroderma with waxy/shiny/tense atrophic skin.

CREST syndrome (Calcinosis, Raynaud's, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasias) is a limited form of scleroderma. Anti-centromere antibodies are associated with CREST; anti-Scl-70 with diffuse scleroderma.

## General

**Q:** What is the effect of scleroderma on the esophagus?

**A:** Smooth muscle atrophy and fibrosis leading to decreased LES tone and weak peristalsis

Scleroderma causes esophageal involvement with atrophy of smooth muscle, resulting in reflux and dysphagia due to weak peristalsis and incompetent LES.

**Q:** What esophageal abnormality is seen in scleroderma?

**A:** Absent or weak peristalsis in the esophageal body with decreased LES tone

Scleroderma causes fibrosis of smooth muscle in the esophagus, leading to aperistalsis and hypotensive LES - a key feature distinguishing it from other motor disorders.

**Q:** What is the defining feature of seropositive rheumatic diseases?

**A:** Presence of a serologic marker such as positive RF or ANA

A small subset of vasculitides (ANCA-associated) has measurable serological components but are often considered separate entities.

**Q:** What are the modifiable risk factors for gout?

**A:** Diet (alcohol, purine-rich foods like meats and seafood, fructose/sugar-sweetened foods), dehydration (e.g., from diuretics), metabolic syndrome

Alcohol (especially beer and spirits), seafood, organ meats, and fructose-rich foods increase uric acid production or reduce excretion.

**Q:** What are the non-modifiable risk factors for gout?

**A:** Genetic mutations, male gender, and advanced age

Males are at higher risk, particularly middle-aged males, and postmenopausal females are also at increased risk.

**Q:** What acute treatments are used for gout flare?

**A:** NSAIDs (high-dose, then taper), corticosteroids (IA preferred, PO, or IM/IV), colchicine (best within first 12-24h)

Treatment choice depends on comorbidities - colchicine caution in renal failure, corticosteroids for NSAID contraindications.

**Q:** What are indications for starting urate-lowering therapy (ULT) in gout?

**A:** ≥2 attacks/year, presence of tophi, or bone erosions/arthritis on imaging

ULT is recommended for patients with frequent flares, tophi, or structural joint damage from gout.

**Q:** What are the clinical presentations of CPPD disease?

**A:** Asymptomatic crystal deposition, acute crystal arthritis (resembling gout), pseudo-OA (progressive degeneration with acute flares), pseudo-RA (symmetrical polyarticular pattern with morning stiffness)

CPPD can present in multiple ways, making diagnosis challenging without joint aspiration.

**Q:** What is the ultrasound finding characteristic of gout?

**A:** Double-contour sign

This ultrasound finding represents urate crystal deposition on cartilage surface.

**Q:** What is a tophus in gout?

**A:** Urate deposits on cartilage, tendons, bursae, soft tissues, and synovial membranes, appearing as subcutaneous nodules

Tophi are collections of monosodium urate crystals that form in chronic gout and indicate poorly controlled disease.

**Q:** What is the recommended prophylaxis when starting urate-lowering therapy?

**A:** Low-dose NSAID, colchicine, or prednisone/prednisolone for 3-6 months

This prevents acute gout flares that can occur when starting ULT, as urate crystals mobilize.

**Q:** Compare gout vs pseudogout: treatment differences

**A:** Both use NSAIDs, corticosteroids, colchicine for acute attacks. Chronic gout uses urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol, febuxostat). Pseudogout has no effective ULT; treatment focuses on acute management and underlying condition control.

The main difference is chronic management - gout has effective ULTs while pseudogout does not.

## Rheum Arthritis

**Q:** What are the joints commonly affected in hemochromatosis?

**A:** Any joint can be affected, but especially the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints; also chondrocalcinosis

Arthropathy in hemochromatosis often involves the MCP joints and is a common presenting feature.

**Q:** What joint findings are associated with hemochromatosis?

**A:** Arthralgia affecting any joint, especially metacarpophalangeal joints, and chondrocalcinosis

Arthropathy in hemochromatosis results from iron deposition in joints, commonly affecting the MCP joints.

**Q:** What joint involvement is characteristic in hemochromatosis?

**A:** Arthralgia, especially of the metacarpophalangeal joints, and chondrocalcinosis

MCP joint arthritis is classic. Chondrocalcinosis (calcium pyrophosphate deposition) is also common and can cause acute arthritis attacks.

**Q:** What is the serologic hallmark of Rheumatoid Arthritis?

**A:** Rheumatoid Factor (RF)

RF is present in 70-80% of RA patients and is directed against the Fc domain of IgG.

**Q:** What are the 4 classic clinical features of Rheumatoid Arthritis?

**A:** Symmetrical polyarthritis (small joints), morning stiffness >1 hour, subcutaneous nodules, and progressive joint deformities

RA is characterized by inflammatory joint disease with extra-articular features including subcutaneous nodules.

**Q:** What is the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria score threshold for definite Rheumatoid Arthritis?

**A:** 6 out of 10 points

The score-based algorithm includes joint involvement (0-5), serology (0-3), acute phase reactants (0-1), and symptom duration (0-1).

**Q:** In the 2010 ACR/EULAR RA criteria, how many points does involvement of 1-3 small joints score?

**A:** 2 points

Joint involvement scoring: 1 large joint = 0, 2-10 large joints = 1, 1-3 small joints = 2, 4-10 small joints = 3, >10 joints = 5.

**Q:** What is the most sensitive autoantibody test for Rheumatoid Arthritis?

**A:** Rheumatoid Factor (RF)

RF is sensitive (70-80%) but not specific; it can be negative early in disease course.

**Q:** What is the key radiographic finding in early Rheumatoid Arthritis?

**A:** Periarticular osteopenia

Later findings include joint space narrowing and erosions.

**Q:** What is the relationship between RF positivity and joint disease in RA?

**A:** Positive RF is associated with more aggressive joint disease and extra-articular features (e.g., nodules)

RF positivity indicates a more severe disease phenotype.

**Q:** What immunosuppressive agents are added to glucocorticoid therapy for dermatomyositis?

**A:** Azathioprine or methotrexate (MTX)

These steroid-sparing agents help maintain remission and reduce long-term glucocorticoid exposure.

**Q:** What is pseudogout (CPPD disease)?

**A:** Joint inflammation caused by calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystal deposition in connective tissue

CPP crystals trigger acute inflammatory arthritis when phagocytosed by neutrophils, releasing inflammatory mediators.

**Q:** What are the common sites of pseudogout involvement?

**A:** Knees, wrists, metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints, hips, shoulders; less commonly elbows, ankles, big toe, spine

Unlike gout which favors lower extremities, pseudogout commonly affects the knee and wrist.

## Rheumatology

**Q:** A 45-year-old woman presents with symmetric polyarthritis of the MCP and PIP joints, morning stiffness >1 hour, and subcutaneous nodules. RF and anti-CCP are positive. What is the diagnosis and what does anti-CCP positivity indicate?

**A:** Rheumatoid arthritis. Anti-CCP is 94-98% specific for RA and predicts persistent, erosive disease.

Anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) antibodies are highly specific for RA and may appear before clinical disease. They predict erosive disease and increased extra-articular manifestations. RF is sensitive but not specific (found in many conditions). Treatment should start with DMARDs as soon as possible, with MTX as first-line.

**Q:** What are the radiographic hallmarks that differentiate osteoarthritis from inflammatory arthritis?

**A:** OA: non-uniform joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts, osteophytes. Inflammatory: uniform joint space narrowing, soft tissue swelling, erosions, periarticular osteopenia.

OA affects weight-bearing and overused joints (knee, hip, DIP) in older patients with new bone formation. Inflammatory arthritis (e.g., RA) affects the C-spine, carpus, MCP, and MTP joints, often in younger patients, with erosions and periarticular osteopenia. The mnemonic 'LOSS' helps remember OA findings: Loss of joint space, Osteophytes, Subchondral cysts, Subchondral sclerosis.

**Q:** What are Gottron's papules and what disease are they pathognomonic for?

**A:** Pink-violaceous, flat-topped papules overlying the dorsal MCP and IP joints, pathognomonic for dermatomyositis (occurring in 70% of patients).

Dermatomyositis presents with progressive symmetric proximal muscle weakness and characteristic skin findings including Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash (violaceous rash over eyelids), shawl sign, and mechanic's hands. DM is associated with malignancy (especially in patients >50) and requires cancer screening. Treatment includes high-dose glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive agents.

**Q:** A 30-year-old man presents with conjunctivitis, urethritis, and asymmetric oligoarthritis of the lower extremities 3 weeks after a Chlamydia infection. What is the diagnosis and what skin findings are diagnostic?

**A:** Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome). Diagnostic skin findings: keratoderma blennorrhagicum (hyperkeratotic lesions on palms/soles) and balanitis circinata (painless ulcers on glans penis).

Reactive arthritis is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy triggered by GI (Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia) or GU (Chlamydia) infections. The classic triad is arthritis, conjunctivitis/uveitis, and urethritis ('Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree'). HLA-B27 positive patients tend to have more axial involvement.

**Q:** How do you distinguish gout crystals from pseudogout crystals on joint aspirate polarized microscopy?

**A:** Gout: monosodium urate crystals are needle-shaped with NEGATIVE birefringence (yellow when parallel to compensator). Pseudogout: CPP crystals are rhomboid-shaped with POSITIVE birefringence (blue when parallel).

Crystal identification on synovial fluid analysis is the gold standard for diagnosis. Gout classically affects the first MTP joint (podagra) in middle-aged males. Pseudogout (CPPD) typically affects the knee and wrist in elderly patients. Gout x-rays show erosions with 'overhanging edges' while pseudogout shows chondrocalcinosis.

**Q:** What is the classification criteria for Sjögren's Syndrome and what is the major malignancy risk?

**A:** ACR/EULAR criteria require a score ≥4 from: labial salivary gland biopsy with focal lymphocytic sialadenitis (3 pts), Anti-SSA/Ro positive (3 pts), ocular staining score ≥5 (1 pt), Schirmer's test <5mm/5min (1 pt), unstimulated saliva flow <0.1 mL/min (1 pt). Major risk: non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (6-7% lifetime incidence).

Sjögren's syndrome is an autoimmune condition causing dry eyes and dry mouth from lymphocytic infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands. It is primarily seen in women (F:M 10:1) aged 40-60. Treatment is largely symptomatic: artificial tears, dental hygiene, pilocarpine for salivary flow. Rituximab may be used for severe organ-threatening disease.

**Q:** What are the CASPAR criteria for psoriatic arthritis?

**A:** Patient must have inflammatory articular disease PLUS ≥3 points from: current psoriasis (2 pts), past psoriasis or family history (1 pt each), psoriatic nail dystrophy (1 pt), negative RF (1 pt), current/past dactylitis (1 pt), juxta-articular bone formation on hand/foot x-rays (1 pt).

Psoriatic arthritis is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy that can affect joints, spine, or entheses. Treatment includes NSAIDs, IA steroids, DMARDs (MTX, SSZ, leflunomide), and biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17, anti-IL-23, JAK inhibitors). Unlike RA, PsA shows new bone formation on x-rays rather than just erosions.

**Q:** What is polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) and what percentage is associated with hepatitis B?

**A:** PAN is a systemic necrotizing vasculitis of medium-sized vessels that is ANCA-negative and classically lung-sparing. 5-10% is associated with hepatitis B positivity.

PAN presents with systemic symptoms (fatigue, weight loss, fever), livedo reticularis, renal insufficiency/HTN, mononeuropathy multiplex, and mesenteric arteritis. Imaging shows beading of blood vessels. Treatment is guided by the five-factor score (FFS): glucocorticoids alone for FFS=0, glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide for FFS≥1.

**Q:** A 55-year-old woman with SLE presents with malar rash, oral ulcers, arthritis, proteinuria, and positive anti-dsDNA. Using the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria, what is the entry criterion and how is SLE classified?

**A:** Entry criterion: ANA titre ≥1:80. SLE is classified if total score ≥10 with at least 1 clinical criterion. Her score: acute cutaneous lupus (6) + oral ulcers (2) + joint involvement (6) + proteinuria >0.5g/24h (4) + anti-dsDNA (6) = 24 points.

The 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria use a weighted scoring system across clinical and immunology domains. Only the highest weighted criterion within each domain counts. Anti-dsDNA is 95% specific for SLE and levels can correlate with disease activity. First-line treatment is hydroxychloroquine, which is associated with reduced mortality.

**Q:** What are the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** Score ≥6/10: Joint involvement (0-5 pts), Serology RF/anti-CCP (0-3 pts), Acute phase reactants ESR/CRP (0-1 pt), Duration ≥6 weeks (0-1 pt). Must have ≥1 joint with definite clinical swelling not explained by another disease.

RA is the most common inflammatory arthritis (1% prevalence). MTX is the gold standard first-line DMARD. Treatment should start as soon as diagnosis is made, with a 'window of opportunity' within the first 3 months for better control/remission.

**Q:** What are the extra-articular features of rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** Skin: rheumatoid nodules, periungual infarction. Ocular: keratoconjunctivitis sicca, scleritis. Cardiac: pericarditis, valvular disease. Pulmonary: fibrosis, pleural effusion, nodules. Neurologic: peripheral neuropathy. Hematologic: Felty's syndrome (splenomegaly + neutropenia).

RA is an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease. Poor prognostic factors include young age of onset, high RF titre, elevated ESR, >20 active joints, and extra-articular features. Caplan's syndrome is the rare combination of RA + pneumoconiosis.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic for SLE diagnostic criteria?

**A:** MD SOAP BRAIN: Malar rash, Discoid rash, Oral ulcers, Photosensitivity, Blood (cytopenias), Renal, Arthritis, Immune (anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, APLA), Neurologic, Serositis.

SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease with M:F ratio of 1:10, onset in reproductive years. ANA is 98% sensitive but poorly specific. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are highly specific (95-99%). Treatment starts with hydroxychloroquine, which reduces mortality and prevents flares.

**Q:** What distinguishes the arthritis of SLE from RA?

**A:** SLE arthritis can be deforming but is non-erosive and reducible (Jaccoud's arthritis), whereas RA causes erosive, irreversible joint damage.

SLE joint involvement affects similar distributions to RA but imaging shows no erosions. The 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria use a score-based system requiring ANA ≥1:80 as entry criterion and total score ≥10 across clinical and immunological domains.

**Q:** What is the CREST syndrome?

**A:** Calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia — the limited cutaneous form of systemic sclerosis.

Limited SSc has better prognosis (>70% 10-year survival) compared to diffuse SSc (40-60%). Anti-centromere antibody is associated with CREST/limited SSc, while anti-topoisomerase I (Scl-70) is associated with diffuse SSc. Lung disease is the most common cause of morbidity and mortality.

**Q:** What are the characteristic skin findings in dermatomyositis?

**A:** Gottron's papules (violaceous papules over MCP/IP joints), heliotrope rash (violaceous periorbital rash with edema), shawl sign (photosensitive erythema over chest/shoulders), mechanic's hands (cracked palmar lesions).

DMM is associated with malignancy, especially in patients >50 years. Malignancy surveillance should include CXR, abdominal/pelvic US, fecal occult blood, mammogram, and CT scan. CK is elevated in 80% and is the key diagnostic marker for muscle inflammation.

**Q:** What is Sjogren's syndrome and what is the most serious complication?

**A:** Autoimmune condition causing dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) and dry mouth (xerostomia) from lymphocytic infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands. Most serious complication: increased risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (lifetime incidence 6-7%).

Primary Sjogren's occurs alone; secondary is associated with RA, SLE, or DMM. Prevalence is 0.5%, F>>M at 10:1. Diagnosis requires anti-SSA/Ro antibody or labial salivary gland biopsy. Treatment is mainly supportive with artificial tears and parasympathomimetics.

**Q:** What autoantibody is most specific for rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** Anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) — 94-98% specific, 80% sensitive.

Anti-CCP may precede symptom onset and is useful in early disease and to predict persistent/erosive disease. RF is 80% sensitive but less specific and can be positive in many conditions (IE, TB, hepatitis C, sarcoidosis). High-positive RF or anti-CCP (>3x ULN) scores 3 points on the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria.

**Q:** What are the features that distinguish inflammatory from mechanical back pain?

**A:** Inflammatory: insidious onset, age <45, morning stiffness >1 hour, improves with exercise, worse with rest, disturbs sleep (worse in second half of night). Mechanical: acute onset, any age, stiffness <30 min, worse with exercise, better with rest.

Inflammatory back pain suggests ankylosing spondylitis or other spondyloarthropathy. AS involves the SI joints and vertebrae with enthesitis as a major feature. 90-95% of AS patients are HLA-B27 positive. NSAIDs are first-line treatment for both axial and peripheral disease.

**Q:** How do you differentiate gout from pseudogout on joint aspiration?

**A:** Gout: monosodium urate crystals, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent (yellow when parallel to compensator). Pseudogout: calcium pyrophosphate crystals, rhomboid-shaped, positively birefringent (blue when parallel).

Gout classically affects the 1st MTP joint (podagra) and occurs in middle-aged males. Pseudogout typically affects knees and wrists in elderly patients. X-ray findings differ: gout shows erosions with overhanging edges; pseudogout shows chondrocalcinosis.

**Q:** What are the indications for uric acid lowering therapy in gout?

**A:** Gout attacks ≥2/year, tophi, bone erosions/arthritis. First-line agent is allopurinol (including CKD stage ≥3).

When starting urate-lowering therapy, concurrent anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (e.g., colchicine, NSAIDs, prednisone) should be initiated for 3-6 months to prevent flares. Target serum urate is <6 mg/dL. Febuxostat is second-line but should be avoided in cardiovascular disease.

**Q:** What is the clinical triad of reactive arthritis?

**A:** Arthritis, conjunctivitis/uveitis, urethritis/cervicitis ('Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree').

ReA is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy triggered by GI (Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia) or GU (Chlamydia) infections. It occurs 1-4 weeks post-infection and is self-limited (3-5 months) but chronic in 15-20%. Keratoderma blennorrhagicum and balanitis circinata are diagnostic skin findings.

**Q:** What is the treatment for giant cell arteritis and why is it urgent?

**A:** Immediately start high-dose prednisone 1 mg/kg PO. If presenting with vision loss: methylprednisolone 1000 mg/d IV for 3 days first. This is a medical emergency because untreated GCA leads to permanent blindness in 20-25%.

GCA is the most common vasculitis in North America, affecting patients >50 years. Diagnosis is by clinical suspicion + elevated ESR/CRP + temporal artery biopsy or imaging (halo sign on Doppler US). Tocilizumab may be added for glucocorticoid-sparing. Late complications include aortic aneurysm.

**Q:** What features distinguish GPA (Wegener's) from EGPA (Churg-Strauss)?

**A:** GPA: upper/lower respiratory granulomatous inflammation + glomerulonephritis, c-ANCA/PR3 positive, saddle nose deformity. EGPA: asthma + eosinophilia + systemic vasculitis (mononeuritis multiplex), p-ANCA/MPO positive in 40-50%.

Both are ANCA-associated small vessel vasculitides. GPA treatment involves induction with glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide or rituximab, followed by maintenance with rituximab (first-line). EGPA is characterized by the triad of allergic rhinitis/asthma, eosinophilic infiltrative disease, and systemic vasculitis.

**Q:** What are the five common patterns of psoriatic arthritis?

**A:** 1. Asymmetric oligoarthritis (most common, 70%), 2. DIP arthritis, 3. Symmetric polyarthritis (similar to RA), 4. Asymmetric sacroiliitis/spondylitis, 5. Arthritis mutilans (destructive small joint polyarthritis).

PsA is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy occurring in 10-30% of psoriasis patients. Key features include dactylitis, enthesopathy, and psoriatic nail changes (pitting, onycholysis, oil drop sign). Treatment includes NSAIDs, DMARDs (MTX), and biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17, anti-IL-23, JAK inhibitors).

**Q:** What is the Modified Schober test and what does it assess?

**A:** Mark 10 cm above and 5 cm below the posterior superior iliac spines. Have the patient bend forward maximally. Normal: distance increases by ≥5 cm. Decreased lumbar flexion suggests ankylosing spondylitis.

AS causes spinal restriction through inflammation → osteopenia → erosion → ossification → osteoproliferation (syndesmophytes). X-ray findings include 'squaring of vertebral bodies,' bridging syndesmophytes ('bamboo spine'), and symmetric sacroiliitis. MRI detects early bone marrow edema.

**Q:** What is PMR (Polymyalgia Rheumatica) and how does it differ from GCA?

**A:** PMR: pain and stiffness of proximal extremities (shoulder/hip girdles) in patients >50, elevated ESR/CRP, no muscle weakness, normal CK. PMR is the most common extracranial symptom of GCA. 50% of PMR patients have GCA.

PMR treatment starts with prednisone 12.5-25 mg PO daily with dramatic response expected within days. Taper slowly over 1 year. Monitor for steroid side effects and GCA symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, vision changes). 50% relapse rate.

**Q:** What is the most important etiologic factor in Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans)?

**A:** Cigarette smoking.

Buerger's disease causes inflammation and clotting of small and medium-sized arteries and veins of distal extremities, leading to distal claudication and potentially gangrene. It is most common in young Asian males. Smoking cessation is the cornerstone of treatment and is the only intervention that can halt disease progression.

**Q:** What is the antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS)?

**A:** A multi-system vasculopathy manifested by recurrent thromboembolic events, spontaneous abortions, and thrombocytopenia, with circulating antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, anti-B2GP1).

Primary APS occurs without other disease; secondary APS occurs with CTD (especially SLE). Lab criteria must be positive on 2 occasions ≥12 weeks apart. Treatment: lifelong warfarin (INR 2-3 for first venous event), heparin + ASA during pregnancy for obstetric APS. Catastrophic APS has high mortality.

**Q:** A 50-year-old woman presents with joint pain. What features suggest inflammatory arthritis rather than osteoarthritis?

**A:** Inflammatory: WARM(S) joints - Worse with rest/better with activity, Awakening in latter half of night, Morning stiffness >30 min, Soft tissue swelling/erythema. OA: pain with activity, improved with rest, stiffness <30 min.

Distinguishing inflammatory from non-inflammatory joint disease guides workup and management. Inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) and specific serologies (RF, anti-CCP, ANA) help narrow the differential diagnosis.

**Q:** What is the first-line pharmacotherapy for osteoarthritis, and why is this choice appropriate?

**A:** First-line: acetaminophen up to 4 g/day. This is appropriate because OA is primarily a degenerative (not inflammatory) disorder, so anti-inflammatory agents are second-line.

OA management follows a stepwise approach: 1st line acetaminophen, 2nd line NSAIDs (COX2 selective) in low doses for short durations, 3rd line duloxetine or combination analgesics. Intra-articular corticosteroid injections are pharmacological adjuncts.

**Q:** What are the hallmark radiographic features of osteoarthritis?

**A:** Joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts, and osteophytes.

OA is the most common form of arthritis. Almost everyone >65 shows OA signs on x-ray but only 33% are symptomatic. There are no laboratory tests for diagnosis. Treatment is stepwise: acetaminophen → NSAIDs → duloxetine/combination analgesics.

**Q:** What extra-articular features of RA are classified by vasculitic vs. lymphocytic infiltrate pathophysiology?

**A:** Vasculitic: periungual infarction, cutaneous ulcers, episcleritis/scleritis, peri/myocarditis, pulmonary fibrosis, peripheral neuropathy, mononeuritis multiplex. Lymphocytic: rheumatoid nodules, keratoconjunctivitis sicca, xerostomia, Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

RA is an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease. Important syndromes: Felty's (arthritis + splenomegaly + neutropenia), Caplan's (RA + pneumoconiosis), and secondary Sjogren's (dry eyes/mouth). Poor prognosis: young onset, high RF, elevated ESR, >20 active joints, extra-articular features.

**Q:** What joint deformities are characteristic of rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** Swan neck deformity (PIP hyperextension + DIP flexion), boutonnière deformity (PIP flexion + DIP hyperextension), ulnar deviation of MCPs, radial deviation of wrist, hammer/mallet/claw toes.

These deformities result from chronic synovitis causing cartilage and bone destruction, tendon rupture, and joint instability. RA involves C-spine with atlanto-axial and subaxial subluxation, which creates risk for dangerous intubation. Carpal tunnel syndrome and ruptured Baker's cyst (mimics DVT) are also common.

**Q:** What is the treatment algorithm for rheumatoid arthritis?

**A:** Start DMARDs immediately upon diagnosis. MTX is first-line (gold standard). If inadequate response in 3-6 months, combine or switch DMARDs. If persistent activity despite MTX, add biologics (anti-TNF agents are first-line biologics). NSAIDs/corticosteroids are supportive only.

DMARDs and biologics (not NSAIDs) can alter the course of RA. Biologics include anti-TNF (infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab), abatacept, rituximab, tocilizumab. JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) are oral small-molecule synthetic DMARDs for DMARD failures. Low-dose prednisone (5-10 mg/d) bridges gap until DMARDs take effect.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic for the diagnostic criteria of SLE?

**A:** MD SOAP BRAIN: Malar rash, Discoid rash, Oral ulcers, Photosensitivity, Blood (cytopenias), Renal, Arthritis, Immune (anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, APLA), Neurologic (seizures, psychosis), Serositis.

SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease with multi-system inflammation (M:F = 1:10, onset 15-45 yr). ANA is 98% sensitive but poor specificity. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are specific (95-99%). The 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria use a score-based algorithm with ANA ≥1:80 as entry criterion and ≥10 points for classification.

**Q:** How does the arthritis of SLE differ from RA?

**A:** SLE arthritis is deforming but non-erosive and reducible (Jaccoud's arthritis), while RA is erosive and irreducible. SLE is polyarticular with reducible deformities, and x-rays show no erosions.

Both conditions present with polyarthritis, but the pathophysiology differs. SLE involves immune complex deposition, while RA involves pannus-mediated destruction. Hydroxychloroquine is central to SLE treatment, reducing mortality and preventing flares.

**Q:** What drugs commonly cause drug-induced lupus and what antibody is characteristically associated?

**A:** Hydralazine, procainamide, phenytoin, isoniazid, biologics, minocycline. Anti-histone antibodies are characteristically present (95% of drug-induced SLE cases).

Drug-induced SLE typically presents with constitutional symptoms, arthralgias, and serositis, but renal and CNS involvement are rare. Symptoms resolve with discontinuation of the offending drug. This distinguishes it from idiopathic SLE.

**Q:** What are the clinical criteria for antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS)?

**A:** Vascular thrombosis (arterial, venous, or small vessel) OR pregnancy morbidity (fetal death ≥10 wk, premature birth <34 wk due to eclampsia/preeclampsia, or ≥3 consecutive spontaneous abortions <10 wk). Plus laboratory: positive lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin Ab, or anti-β2GP1 Ab on 2 occasions ≥12 weeks apart.

APS can be primary (no underlying disease) or secondary (commonly with SLE). Catastrophic APS involves small vessel thrombosis in ≥3 organ systems within 1 week and has high mortality. Treatment: lifelong anticoagulation with warfarin; obstetric APS uses heparin + ASA during pregnancy.

**Q:** What is CREST syndrome and which antibody is associated with it?

**A:** CREST = Calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia. It represents limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis. Anti-centromere antibody is associated (>80% positive).

CREST/limited SSc has skin sclerosis restricted to hands, face, and neck, with pulmonary HTN being a common complication. Diffuse SSc involves widespread skin disease with early visceral involvement and is associated with anti-topoisomerase I (Scl-70) antibody. Lung disease is the most common cause of morbidity and mortality in scleroderma.

**Q:** What is the most important complication of scleroderma renal crisis and how is it treated?

**A:** Malignant arterial HTN with oliguria and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. Treated with ACE inhibitors.

Scleroderma renal crisis occurs in 10-15% of patients, usually with diffuse SSc. Anti-RNA polymerase III antibody is associated with increased risk. Prednisone >20 mg may provoke renal crisis. Raynaud's phenomenon treatment includes vasodilators (CCBs, nitroglycerine cream, PDE5 inhibitors).

**Q:** What are the characteristic skin findings of dermatomyositis?

**A:** Gottron's papules (pink-violaceous flat-topped papules over dorsal MCP and IP joints, pathognomonic), heliotrope rash (violaceous rash over eyelids with edema), shawl sign (poikilodermatous rash over neck/chest/shoulders), mechanic's hands (dry, cracked lesions on palmar surfaces).

DM is associated with malignancy (screen with thorough exam, CXR, CT, mammogram). CK is elevated in 80%. Treatment: high-dose glucocorticoids + immunosuppressive agents (azathioprine, MTX). IVIG for severe/refractory cases. PM is a T cell-mediated process, while DM is complement-mediated.

**Q:** What are the features of Sjögren's syndrome and what is the increased malignancy risk?

**A:** Autoimmune condition causing dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) and dry mouth (xerostomia) from lymphocytic infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands. Increased risk of non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (lifetime incidence 6-7%).

Prevalence 0.5%, F>>M at 10:1, ages 40-60. Diagnosis: anti-SSA/Ro (40-95%), labial salivary gland biopsy with focus score ≥1, Schirmer's test <5 mm/5 min. Treatment: artificial tears, pilocarpine for dry mouth, good dental hygiene. Rituximab for severe organ-threatening disease.

**Q:** What are the classic features of granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener's)?

**A:** Triad: granulomatous vasculitis of upper and lower respiratory tract + focal segmental glomerulonephritis. Features include sinusitis/rhinitis, nasoseptal perforation, saddle nose deformity, pulmonary nodules/cavitary lesions, and renal involvement. Highly associated with c-ANCA/PR3-ANCA.

GPA is a small vessel ANCA-associated vasculitis. Treatment of severe disease: IV glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide or rituximab for induction, then rituximab for maintenance. All patients need PJP prophylaxis and osteoporosis prevention. Changes in ANCA levels do not reliably predict relapse.

**Q:** What are the key differences between giant cell arteritis (GCA) and polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)?

**A:** GCA: temporal headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, sudden painless vision loss, elevated ESR/CRP, requires high-dose prednisone 1 mg/kg. PMR: proximal shoulder/pelvic girdle pain and stiffness, elevated ESR/CRP, no true weakness, responds to low-dose prednisone 12.5-25 mg.

GCA is a medical emergency as untreated it can cause permanent blindness in 20-25%. PMR is the most common extracranial manifestation of GCA. If GCA suspected, start prednisone immediately (do not wait for biopsy). Temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound (halo sign) confirms diagnosis.

**Q:** What is ankylosing spondylitis and what HLA marker is strongly associated?

**A:** Chronic inflammatory arthritis of SI joints and vertebrae, with enthesitis as a major feature. HLA-B27 positive in 90-95%. M:F = 3:1, onset in 20s. Presents with inflammatory back pain (morning stiffness >1h, improves with exercise, worse with rest).

X-ray findings progress from 'squaring of edges' to 'bamboo spine' (bridging syndesmophytes). Modified Schober test measures lumbar flexion (normal ≥5 cm increase). Extra-articular: anterior uveitis (25-30%), IBD, aortic regurgitation, apical fibrosis. Treatment: NSAIDs first-line; conventional DMARDs only for peripheral arthritis; anti-TNF or anti-IL-17 for axial disease.

**Q:** What are the 5 patterns of arthritis seen in psoriatic arthritis?

**A:** 1. Asymmetric oligoarthritis (most common, 70%). 2. DIP arthritis (with nail changes). 3. Symmetric polyarthritis (RA-like). 4. Asymmetric sacroiliitis and spondylitis. 5. Arthritis mutilans (destructive, deforming).

Psoriasis precedes arthritis in 85% of cases. Characteristic findings include dactylitis, enthesopathy, psoriatic nail changes (pitting, onycholysis, oil drop sign), and radiologic pencil-in-cup appearance. Treatment: DMARDs early; biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17, anti-IL-23) for resistant disease. Check 'hidden' areas for psoriasis: ears, hairline, umbilicus, gluteal cleft, nails.

**Q:** What is the clinical triad of reactive arthritis and what infections trigger it?

**A:** Triad: arthritis + conjunctivitis/uveitis + urethritis/cervicitis ('Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree'). Triggered by GI (Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, C. difficile) or GU (Chlamydia, Mycoplasma) infections, onset 1-4 weeks post-infection.

ReA is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy, HLA-B27 positive in 80%. Diagnostic skin findings: keratoderma blennorrhagicum (hyperkeratotic lesions on palms/soles) and balanitis circinata (painless ulcers on glans penis). Usually self-limited (3-5 months), chronic in 15-20%.

**Q:** How do you differentiate gout from pseudogout by crystal analysis?

**A:** Gout: monosodium urate crystals, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent (yellow when parallel to compensator). Pseudogout: calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals, rhomboid-shaped, positively birefringent (blue when parallel).

Gout classically affects the 1st MTP (podagra) in middle-aged males. Pseudogout affects knees and wrists in elderly patients with chondrocalcinosis on x-ray. Precipitants of gout (mnemonic FACT): Fructose, Aspirin (low-dose)/Alcohol, Cyclosporine, Thiazide diuretics. Foods: SALT (Seafood, Alcohol, Liver/kidney, Turkey).

**Q:** What is the first-line urate-lowering therapy for chronic gout and when should it be initiated?

**A:** Allopurinol is first-line for all patients (including CKD stage ≥3). Start low and titrate up. Indications: ≥2 attacks/year, tophi, bone erosions/arthritis, radiographic damage attributable to gout.

Allopurinol and febuxostat both inhibit xanthine oxidase. Febuxostat should not be used if history of cardiovascular disease. When starting ULT, concomitant anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (colchicine, NSAIDs, or low-dose prednisone) should be given for 3-6 months to prevent acute flares.

**Q:** How do you differentiate inflammatory myositis from PMR from fibromyalgia?

**A:** Inflammatory myositis: proximal weakness (painless), elevated CK, abnormal muscle biopsy/EMG. PMR: proximal pain/stiffness without weakness, markedly elevated ESR/CRP, responds to low-dose steroids. Fibromyalgia: widespread pain without weakness, normal labs, treated with exercise and sleep restoration.

Key differentiator is the presence or absence of true muscle weakness. Inflammatory myositis has elevated CK and EMG changes; PMR has elevated inflammatory markers but normal CK; fibromyalgia has all normal labs. Myositis requires high-dose steroids, PMR requires low-dose, and fibromyalgia does not respond to steroids.

**Q:** What conditions are associated with pseudogout (CPPD disease)?

**A:** Old age, advanced OA, neuropathic joints. Associated metabolic conditions: hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia (low ALP), hemochromatosis.

CPPD can present as acute crystal arthritis (resembling gout), pseudo-OA (progressive degeneration), or pseudo-RA (symmetric polyarticular with morning stiffness). X-rays show chondrocalcinosis in 75%. Treatment: acute - joint aspiration and IA steroids; chronic - NSAIDs with gastroprotection ± low-dose colchicine.

**Q:** What is the most common cause of Raynaud's phenomenon secondary to a connective tissue disease?

**A:** Scleroderma (systemic sclerosis) is the most common cause of secondary Raynaud's phenomenon.

Features of pathologic Raynaud's include: new onset, asymmetric, precipitated by stimuli other than cold/emotion, associated with digital pitting/tissue reabsorption, digital ischemia, and capillary dilatation by capillaroscopy. DDx mnemonic COLD HAND: CREST, Obstruction/Occupational, Lupus/CTDs, DM/Drugs, Hematologic, Arterial, Neurologic, Disease of unknown origin.

**Q:** What autoantibodies and their disease associations are most important for the MCCQE1?

**A:** RF: RA (80%, non-specific). Anti-CCP: RA (80%, 94-98% specific). ANA: SLE (98%, sensitive not specific). Anti-dsDNA: SLE (50-70%, 95% specific, correlates with disease activity). Anti-Sm: SLE (<30%, specific). Anti-centromere: limited SSc/CREST (>80%). Anti-topoisomerase I (Scl-70): diffuse SSc. Anti-Jo1: PM/DM (anti-synthetase syndrome). c-ANCA/PR3: GPA (90%). p-ANCA/MPO: MPA (60-80%).

Ordering ANA/RF only makes sense when pre-test probability of CTD is high, as false positives are common (up to 30% of healthy individuals have low-titre ANA). Anti-dsDNA and complement levels (C3, C4) are useful for monitoring SLE disease activity. Anti-CCP may precede clinical RA symptoms.

**Q:** What are the key features that differentiate mechanical from inflammatory back pain?

**A:** Mechanical: acute onset, age 15-90, morning stiffness <30 min, worse with exercise, better with rest, anatomic pain radiation, possible motor/sensory symptoms. Inflammatory: insidious onset, age <45, morning stiffness >1 hour (worse during 2nd half of night), better with exercise, worse with rest, diffuse radiation, responds well to NSAIDs.

Inflammatory back pain is characteristic of spondyloarthropathies (AS, PsA, ReA, EA). Key distinguishing features are involvement of other systems (uveitis, IBD, psoriasis), onset <45 years, and HLA-B27 association. Imaging may show sacroiliitis (MRI with bone marrow edema on STIR sequences in early disease).

**Q:** What are the 4 types of immune-mediated hypersensitivity reactions and their clinical examples?

**A:** Type I (IgE-mediated): asthma, anaphylaxis, allergic rhinitis. Type II (cytotoxic Ab-mediated): autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Goodpasture syndrome, Graves' disease, ITP. Type III (immune complex): SLE, PAN, post-streptococcal GN. Type IV (cell-mediated/delayed): contact dermatitis, tuberculin skin test, mycobacterial infection.

Understanding hypersensitivity types is essential for mechanistic understanding of rheumatologic diseases. SLE primarily involves Type III (immune complex deposition), Goodpasture and Graves are Type II, and contact dermatitis is the classic Type IV. This framework guides diagnostic approach and treatment selection.

**Q:** Which HLA types are associated with ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren's syndrome respectively?

**A:** HLA-B27: ankylosing spondylitis (relative risk 20x), reactive arthritis, and axial PsA/EA. HLA-DR4/DR1: rheumatoid arthritis (relative risk 2–10x; found in 93% of RA patients). HLA-DR3: Sjögren's syndrome (associated with anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies).

HLA typing is supportive but not diagnostic for rheumatic diseases. HLA-B27 has the highest relative risk association with AS; however, 7% of the White Canadian population carries HLA-B27 without disease. HLA testing is useful when pre-test probability is moderate and diagnosis is uncertain.

**Q:** What is the function of MHC Class I vs. Class II molecules and which T cells recognize each?

**A:** MHC Class I (HLA-A, B, C): present on all nucleated cells; recognized by CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes. MHC Class II (HLA-DP, DQ, DR): present on antigen-presenting cells (macrophages, B cells, dendritic cells); recognized by CD4+ helper T lymphocytes.

MHC class distinction is fundamental to understanding autoimmune pathophysiology. CD8+ cells kill infected/aberrant cells via lytic granules (perforin/granzyme) and FasL. CD4+ helper T cells coordinate immune response by activating macrophages and B cells and releasing cytokines. This underlies treatment rationale for biologics targeting specific immune pathways.

**Q:** What are the key differences between inflammatory and degenerative arthritis on history and physical?

**A:** Inflammatory: pain at rest relieved by activity, morning stiffness >1 hour, cardinal signs of inflammation (warmth/swelling/erythema/tenderness), extra-articular manifestations, symmetric small joints. Degenerative: pain with motion relieved by rest, morning stiffness <1 hour, gelling, bony enlargement, malalignment, end-of-day pain.

This distinction guides the diagnostic workup and treatment approach. Inflammatory arthritis requires ESR/CRP, ANA/RF/ACPA testing and DMARDs; degenerative arthritis (OA) requires radiography to confirm and is managed with non-pharmacological measures and analgesics. Morning stiffness duration is the single most useful discriminating feature.

**Q:** What are the 3 most important tests to perform on synovial fluid, and what WBC/mm³ cut-offs differentiate normal, non-inflammatory, inflammatory, and septic fluid?

**A:** The 3 Cs: Culture and Gram stain, Cell count with differential, Crystal analysis. Cut-offs: Normal <200 WBC; Non-inflammatory <2000; Inflammatory ≥2000 (often much higher in crystal-induced); Septic >50,000 with >75% PMNs.

Synovial fluid analysis is the gold standard for differentiating causes of joint effusion. In septic arthritis, WBC is typically >50,000/mm³ with >75% PMNs; however, gonococcal septic arthritis may have lower WBC and is difficult to culture. Crystal-induced arthritis can have very high WBC (>50,000) mimicking septic arthritis, making crystal analysis essential.

**Q:** What defines septic arthritis as a medical emergency, and what are the most common causes?

**A:** Septic arthritis causes rapid joint destruction and carries 10–15% mortality risk. The knee (50% of cases) and hip are most commonly affected. Most commonly caused by hematogenous spread of Gram-positive cocci (S. aureus most common) > Gram-negative bacilli.

Septic arthritis requires urgent joint aspiration for diagnosis and source control plus IV antibiotics. Risk factors include IV drug use, hemodialysis, recent STI (gonococcal), RA, type 2 DM, immunosuppression, and advanced age. Gonococcal septic arthritis is important in sexually active young adults and may be difficult to culture from synovial fluid.

**Q:** What are the differential causes of secondary osteoarthritis?

**A:** Secondary OA causes include: post-traumatic/mechanical, post-inflammatory (RA) or post-infectious, heritable skeletal disorders (scoliosis), endocrine (acromegaly, hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism), metabolic (gout, pseudogout, hemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, ochronosis), neuropathic (Charcot joints from DM/syphilis), AVN, and congenital malformation.

When a patient presents with OA in an unusual pattern (e.g., young patient, unusual joints like MCP involvement), consider secondary causes. Hemochromatosis classically causes OA of the 2nd and 3rd MCPs (chondrocalcinosis-associated), hypothyroidism causes effusions and pseudogout, and acromegaly causes premature OA due to cartilage overgrowth.

**Q:** What are the 4 radiographic hallmarks of osteoarthritis (LOSS mnemonic)?

**A:** LOSS: Loss of joint space (non-uniform), Osteophytes, Subchondral cysts, Subchondral Sclerosis. OA typically affects knee, hip, and DIP joints in older patients with new bone formation.

OA radiographic findings contrast with inflammatory arthritis: OA shows non-uniform joint space narrowing, new bone formation (osteophytes), and subchondral changes; inflammatory arthritis shows uniform joint space narrowing, erosions, periarticular osteopenia, and soft tissue swelling. These distinctions guide diagnosis and management.

**Q:** What are the typical joint distribution patterns and hand findings in osteoarthritis?

**A:** OA commonly affects: DIP (Heberden's nodes = osteophytes), PIP (Bouchard's nodes), 1st CMC (thumb squaring), 1st MTP, knee (medial > lateral compartment), hip, and lumbar spine (L4-L5, L5-S1). MCPs other than 1st are usually spared.

The distribution of OA helps differentiate from inflammatory arthritis. MCP involvement suggests RA or hemochromatosis, not typical OA. Heberden's nodes (DIP) and Bouchard's nodes (PIP) are characteristic bony osteophytes of hand OA. Thumb CMC involvement causing thumb squaring is highly characteristic of hand OA.

**Q:** What is the stepped pharmacological approach to treating osteoarthritis?

**A:** Stepped approach — local before systemic: (1) Topical NSAIDs/capsaicin (knee, hand OA); (2) IA glucocorticoids (knee, hip OA); (3) Acetaminophen, oral NSAIDs; (4) Centrally acting agents (duloxetine). Not recommended: opioids, cannabinoids, hyaluronates, PRP, stem cells, chondroitin, glucosamine.

Canadian OARSI-aligned guidelines emphasize non-pharmacological therapy first (weight loss, exercise, Tai chi, physiotherapy) before pharmacological treatment, and local therapy before systemic agents. Opioids are not recommended for OA pain management. No current treatment alters natural history of OA; total joint replacement is definitive surgical treatment.

**Q:** How does CRP differ from ESR as an inflammatory marker in rheumatic disease monitoring?

**A:** CRP: more sensitive for inflammation, responds more quickly to clinical changes, fewer false positives/negatives. ESR: increased by renal disease, female sex, older age, pregnancy, DM, myeloma, CHF; useful for low-grade bone/joint infections and monitoring CTDs (SLE, PMR, GCA). Do not order ESR for acute inflammation.

CRP is preferred for monitoring acute inflammatory conditions (RA exacerbations, septic arthritis response to treatment). ESR is more useful for chronic conditions like PMR and GCA where it serves as a surrogate disease activity marker and guides steroid tapering. Both can be elevated in systemic inflammatory diseases, infections, and malignancies.

**Q:** What distinguishes seropositive from seronegative rheumatic diseases in terms of joint distribution, pelvic involvement, and extra-articular features?

**A:** Seropositive (RA, SLE, scleroderma): symmetric peripheral arthritis, small and medium joints (PIP, MCP, wrist), no pelvic/axial disease (except C-spine), no enthesitis; extra-articular features include nodules, vasculitis, Raynaud's, rashes. Seronegative (AS, PsA, ReA, EA): asymmetric, larger joints/lower extremities, axial/pelvic disease (SI joints), enthesitis, dactylitis ('sausage digit'), iritis, GI/GU/skin involvement.

This distinction is clinically critical for diagnosis: SI joint involvement on imaging, HLA-B27 positivity, and enthesitis strongly suggest seronegative spondyloarthropathy. Symmetric small joint involvement with positive RF/ACPA and extra-articular features (nodules, ILD) suggest seropositive disease like RA. Treatment approaches also differ significantly.

**Q:** What is enthesitis and in which rheumatic conditions is it a characteristic feature?

**A:** Enthesitis is inflammation of the tendon or ligament at its site of attachment to bone (e.g., Achilles tendon insertion, plantar fascia). It is characteristic of seronegative spondyloarthropathies: ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, and enteropathic arthritis.

Enthesitis is a hallmark of the spondyloarthropathy family and distinguishes these conditions from seropositive arthritis (RA, SLE). Common sites include the Achilles insertion, plantar fascia, patellar tendon, and iliac crest. On imaging, MRI can detect enthesitis before radiographic changes occur. Treatment includes NSAIDs and biologics (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors).

**Q:** What are the Choosing Wisely Canada recommendations specific to rheumatology ordering practices?

**A:** 1. Do not order ANA as a screening test without specific signs/symptoms of SLE or CTD. 2. Do not order anti-dsDNA or complement without specific signs/symptoms. 3. Do not repeat DEXA more often than every 2 years. 4. Do not prescribe bisphosphonates for patients at low fracture risk. 5. Do not perform whole body bone scans for diagnostic screening of arthritis.

ANA is positive in 20–30% of healthy individuals and in many non-rheumatic conditions; ordering it without clinical suspicion leads to unnecessary investigation cascades. These Choosing Wisely recommendations reduce inappropriate testing while maintaining diagnostic quality. Non-judicious ANA ordering is a common clinical error on the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the role of TNF-alpha as a cytokine in rheumatic disease, and what are its major functions?

**A:** TNF-alpha is produced by T cells and macrophages/monocytes. Major functions: apoptotic cell death, cachexia, induction of other cytokines, T cell stimulation, induction of metalloproteinases and prostaglandins, increased expression of adhesion molecules, and increased vascular permeability (entry of IgG, complement, cells into tissues).

TNF-alpha is a pivotal pro-inflammatory cytokine in RA, AS, and other inflammatory arthritides. TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab, golimumab) are highly effective DMARDs that work by blocking these pathways. Key risks of TNF inhibition include reactivation of latent TB, serious infections, and increased malignancy risk.

**Q:** What is IL-6's role in inflammation and what clinical conditions does it drive?

**A:** IL-6 is produced by many cell types. Functions: anemia of inflammation (via hepcidin production), proliferation of B and T cells, acute phase reactant production (CRP, fibrinogen), induction of natural protease inhibitors, promotion of erosions, elevation of CRP. Clinically important in RA (tocilizumab targets IL-6 receptor).

IL-6 drives the acute phase response, causing elevated ESR/CRP and fibrinogen. The anemia of chronic inflammation via hepcidin is an IL-6-mediated effect. Tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor inhibitor) is effective in RA, giant cell arteritis, and was used in COVID-19 cytokine storm. Monitoring CRP is particularly useful as it reflects IL-6 activity.

**Q:** What non-pharmacological treatments are strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis?

**A:** Weight loss (minimum 5–10 lbs if overweight), exercise (supervised more effective; physiotherapist-led or class-based), Tai chi (strongly recommended for hip/knee OA), self-management programs (goal-setting, patient education), thermal intervention (heat or cold), and occupational therapy (aids, splints, cane, bracing).

Non-pharmacological therapy is first-line for OA. A Cochrane systematic review of 54 studies confirmed land-based therapeutic exercise significantly reduces knee OA pain (6 points/100) and improves physical function sustained 2–6 months post-treatment. Tai chi has specific evidence for both pain reduction and fall prevention in older adults with OA.

**Q:** What is the approach to acute monoarthritis, and what are the main diagnostic categories to consider?

**A:** Categories: (1) Non-inflammatory: hemarthrosis, internal derangement (loose body, torn meniscus). (2) Crystal-induced: monosodium urate (gout), CPPD (pseudogout), hydroxyapatite. (3) Infectious: gonococcal, non-gonococcal, mycobacterial, fungal. (4) Seronegative: AS, PsA, ReA, EA. Joint aspiration (synovial fluid analysis) is essential for diagnosis.

Acute monoarthritis requires urgent joint aspiration to exclude septic arthritis (medical emergency). Crystal-induced arthritis and septic arthritis may both have very high WBC (>50,000/mm³) in synovial fluid; crystals under polarized microscopy and culture/Gram stain are the key discriminating tests. Gonococcal arthritis should be considered in sexually active young adults.

**Q:** Which autoantibody is the serologic hallmark of Rheumatoid Arthritis, and what is its sensitivity and specificity?

**A:** Rheumatoid Factor (RF) is the serologic hallmark of RA. Sensitivity ~80%, but it is non-specific (only ~50% specific for RA; present in SLE 20%, IE, TB, hepatitis C, silicosis, sarcoidosis). Positive RF is associated with more aggressive disease, extra-articular features (nodules), and erosions.

RF is directed against the Fc domain of IgG. While sensitive (~80%), it is not specific to RA and can be present in many inflammatory and infectious conditions. Its presence is associated with worse prognosis including erosive disease and extra-articular manifestations.

**Q:** What is the sensitivity and specificity of anti-CCP antibodies in Rheumatoid Arthritis, and what is their clinical utility?

**A:** Anti-CCP sensitivity ~80%, specificity 94-98% for RA. Useful in early disease and predicting persistent/erosive disease. Can occur before clinical disease becomes apparent. Associated with increased extra-articular RA manifestations.

Anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) antibodies are highly specific for RA and can precede clinical symptoms. Their detection supports early diagnosis and can guide prognosis by identifying patients likely to develop persistent, erosive disease.

**Q:** What is the sensitivity of ANA in SLE, and why is a negative ANA clinically useful?

**A:** ANA is 98% sensitive for SLE. Because of its high sensitivity, a negative ANA can effectively rule out SLE. However, ANA has poor specificity (present in many conditions including other CTDs, healthy individuals up to 30% at low titres). Only measure when pre-test probability is high.

ANA (antinuclear antibody) tests are directed against nuclear components (DNA, RNA, histones, centromere). The high sensitivity means nearly all SLE patients are ANA-positive, making a negative result useful for exclusion. However, low-titre positivity is common in the general population, especially with age.

**Q:** Which autoantibodies are specific for SLE (not just sensitive), and do their levels correlate with disease activity?

**A:** Anti-dsDNA (95% specific, 50-70% sensitive): levels correlate with disease activity (rise with flares, complement levels fall). Anti-Sm (<30% sensitive): highly specific (99%) for SLE but does not correlate with disease activity and remains positive throughout disease course.

Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are the two most SLE-specific antibodies. Anti-dsDNA is the more clinically useful for monitoring because its titre tracks disease activity alongside C3/C4 levels. Anti-Sm, while highly specific, is a static marker not used for monitoring.

**Q:** What are the clinical implications of anti-Ro (SSA) antibodies in pregnancy?

**A:** Anti-Ro (SSA) antibodies in pregnancy increase the risk of neonatal lupus syndrome and congenital heart block in the fetus/neonate. Anti-La (SSB) also increases this risk. These antibodies cross the placenta and can affect fetal cardiac conduction.

Anti-Ro antibodies are seen in Sjögren's syndrome (40-95%), SLE (32%), and RA (15%). When present in pregnancy, they can cross the placenta and cause neonatal lupus and irreversible congenital complete heart block — a key clinical concern requiring fetal cardiac monitoring.

**Q:** What autoantibody is associated with drug-induced SLE, and which drugs most commonly cause it?

**A:** Anti-histone antibodies are highly specific (95%) for drug-induced SLE. Common causative drugs: antihypertensives (hydralazine), anticonvulsants (phenytoin), antiarrhythmics (procainamide), isoniazid, biologics, minocycline. Symptoms resolve with discontinuation of the offending drug.

Drug-induced lupus is characterized by anti-histone antibodies and resolves when the causative drug is stopped. It typically lacks renal and CNS involvement compared to idiopathic SLE. Procainamide and hydralazine are the classic high-risk agents.

**Q:** What autoantibody is specific for CREST syndrome (limited systemic sclerosis), and which antibody is associated with diffuse systemic sclerosis and ILD?

**A:** Anti-centromere antibody: specific for CREST (limited cutaneous SSc). Anti-topoisomerase I (anti-Scl-70): specific for SSc, associated with diffuse cutaneous SSc and increased risk of interstitial lung disease (ILD).

CREST features Calcinosis, Raynaud's, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, and Telangiectasias. Limited SSc (CREST) carries anti-centromere antibody and has pulmonary HTN as a major complication. Diffuse SSc with anti-Scl-70 carries higher risk of ILD and renal crisis.

**Q:** What does c-ANCA (anti-PR3 by ELISA) indicate, and which vasculitis is it most specific for?

**A:** c-ANCA (anti-PR3 by ELISA) is 80-95% specific for Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener's). Present in 90% of active GPA. ELISA method (anti-PR3) is more specific than immunofluorescence technique.

ANCA-associated vasculitides include GPA, MPA, and EGPA. c-ANCA (cytoplasmic pattern) predominantly indicates anti-PR3 antibodies and strongly suggests GPA. p-ANCA (anti-MPO) is more commonly seen in MPA (60-80%) and EGPA. ELISA is preferred over immunofluorescence for specificity.

**Q:** What is the 2010 ACR/EULAR scoring system for RA classification, and what score is required for definite RA?

**A:** Score ≥6/10 is required for definite RA. Categories: A) Joint involvement (0-5 points; ≥10 joints including small = 5); B) Serology (0-3; high-positive RF or anti-CCP >3× ULN = 3); C) Acute phase reactants (0-1; abnormal CRP or ESR = 1); D) Symptom duration (0-1; ≥6 weeks = 1). Must have ≥1 joint with definite clinical synovitis not better explained by another diagnosis.

The 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria replaced the older 1987 criteria to enable earlier diagnosis. Small joint involvement, high-titre seropositivity, elevated inflammatory markers, and persistent symptoms all contribute to the score. The minimum requirement is at least one swollen joint.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of RA at the cellular and molecular level?

**A:** Autoimmune disorder with unknown etiology. Genetic predisposition (HLA-DR4/DR1 in 93%), environmental trigger (cigarette smoking induces citrullination via enzyme upregulation). Anti-CCP antibodies form. Synovial inflammation leads to pannus formation — invasive tissue that degrades cartilage and bone. Elevated TNF increases osteoclasts/decreases osteoblasts → periarticular osteopenia. Upregulation of RANK ligand → osteoclast-mediated bone destruction.

RA pathogenesis involves loss of immune tolerance to citrullinated proteins, likely triggered by smoking. The resulting synovitis transforms the synovium into pannus, an invasive tissue. TNF-α and RANK-L are key mediators of bone erosion, making them therapeutic targets (anti-TNF biologics, denosumab).

**Q:** Which joints are most commonly involved in RA, and what joint deformities are characteristic?

**A:** Most common: MCPs, PIPs, wrists, MTPs (small joints of hands and feet). Symmetric distribution. Spares DIP joints (unlike OA and psoriatic arthritis). Classic deformities: swan neck (PIP hyperextension, DIP flexion), boutonnière (PIP flexion, DIP hyperextension), ulnar deviation and MCP subluxation, radial deviation of wrist, hammer/mallet/claw toes. C-spine: atlanto-axial subluxation (risk during intubation).

RA preferentially affects synovial-lined joints and tendons. The pattern of small joint involvement with MCP/PIP/MTP involvement and DIP sparing helps differentiate RA from OA. C-spine involvement (atlanto-axial subluxation) is a critical surgical consideration requiring pre-operative C-spine X-ray.

**Q:** What are the poor prognostic factors in Rheumatoid Arthritis?

**A:** Poor prognostic factors: young age of onset, high RF titre, elevated ESR, involvement of >20 joints, presence of extra-articular features (e.g. nodules, vasculitis, Felty's syndrome), positive anti-CCP, functional limitations at baseline.

Identifying poor prognostic factors early is critical because it guides treatment escalation (e.g., early biologics). RA is associated with increased overall mortality/morbidity from cardiovascular disease, lymphoma, and infections. Aggressive early treatment ('window of opportunity' in first 3 months) can improve long-term outcomes.

**Q:** What are the extra-articular manifestations of RA classified by pathophysiology?

**A:** Vasculitic: periungual infarctions, cutaneous ulcers, palpable purpura, episcleritis/scleritis, peri/myocarditis, pulmonary fibrosis/effusion/pleuritis, peripheral neuropathy (mononeuritis multiplex). Lymphocytic infiltrate: rheumatoid nodules, keratoconjunctivitis sicca (Sjögren's overlap), xerostomia, Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Hematologic: Felty's syndrome (arthritis + splenomegaly + neutropenia). Renal: amyloidosis.

Extra-articular features reflect systemic inflammation beyond joints. Felty's syndrome (rare) consists of RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia and is associated with severe seropositive disease. Sjögren's overlap is common. Amyloidosis from chronic inflammation is a late renal complication.

**Q:** What is the first-line DMARD for RA, what are its contraindications, and what monitoring is required before starting?

**A:** Methotrexate (MTX) is gold standard and first-line DMARD for RA. Pre-treatment workup: CBC, liver enzymes (ALT), creatinine/creatinine clearance, hepatitis B and C serology, CXR. Contraindications: liver disease, significant alcohol intake, pregnancy, lactation. If MTX contraindicated: hydroxychloroquine (weaker), sulfasalazine, or leflunomide (more potent, also contraindicated in pregnancy, long washout period).

MTX works by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase and has anti-inflammatory effects. Pre-treatment assessment ensures no baseline hepatic/renal impairment or active infection. Combination therapy (MTX + hydroxychloroquine + SSZ, or MTX + biologic) is used when monotherapy is insufficient or poor prognostic features exist.

**Q:** When should biologics (bDMARDs) be initiated in RA, and what are the first-line biologic options?

**A:** Biologics should be initiated if inadequate response to DMARDs (after 3-6 months). Should be combined with DMARD therapy for faster response and longer duration of effect. First-line: anti-TNF agents — infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab, golimumab, certolizumab. Non-anti-TNF options: abatacept, rituximab, tocilizumab (anti-IL-6). Anakinra (IL-1 blocker) almost never used for RA.

Anti-TNF biologics are the primary biologic class in RA and target the key cytokine mediating synovial inflammation. Combination with MTX reduces immunogenicity and enhances efficacy. JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) are oral synthetic DMARDs considered after DMARD failure.

**Q:** What is the RA classification of global functional status?

**A:** Class I: performs all usual ADLs (self-care, vocational, avocational). Class II: performs self-care and vocational; restriction of avocational. Class III: performs self-care only; restriction of vocational and avocational. Class IV: limited ability to perform self-care, vocational, and avocational activities.

This functional classification provides a standardized way to assess RA disease burden and therapeutic response. It complements disease activity measures (swollen joint count, CRP, ESR) and helps justify escalation of therapy or surgical intervention.

**Q:** What is the entry criterion for the 2019 EULAR/ACR SLE classification criteria, and what total score is required for diagnosis?

**A:** Entry criterion: ANA titre ≥1:80. Total score ≥10 with at least one clinical criterion required for SLE classification. Criteria span clinical domains (constitutional, hematologic, neuropsychiatric, mucocutaneous, serosal, musculoskeletal, renal) and immunologic domains (antiphospholipid antibodies, complement, SLE-specific antibodies).

The 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria replaced the older ACR criteria and are weighted/additive. Key high-scoring items include Class III/IV lupus nephritis (10 points), acute cutaneous lupus (6 points), seizure (5 points), and pleural/pericardial effusion (5 points). Anti-dsDNA or anti-Sm positivity contributes 6 points.

**Q:** What are the multisystem clinical features of SLE?

**A:** Systemic: fatigue, fever, lymphadenopathy. Hematologic: hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly. Renal: glomerulonephritis (hematuria, proteinuria, casts). Dermatologic: malar rash, discoid rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, alopecia. Musculoskeletal: Jaccoud's arthritis (non-erosive, reducible). Cardiac: pericarditis, Libman-Sacks endocarditis, CAD. Neurologic: seizures, psychosis, stroke. Vascular: Raynaud's, livedo reticularis. Respiratory: pleuritis, pulmonary HTN.

SLE is a prototypical multisystem autoimmune disease. The malar (butterfly) rash spares nasolabial folds. Jaccoud's arthritis is non-erosive and reducible, distinguishing it from RA. Libman-Sacks is non-bacterial endocarditis associated with APLA. SLE is an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis/CAD.

**Q:** How is SLE monitored serologically, and which tests track disease activity versus diagnosis?

**A:** Disease activity monitoring: anti-dsDNA titre (rises with flares) and serum complement (C3, C4 decrease with flares). ANA titre is NOT useful for monitoring disease course — only used for screening/diagnosis. Anti-Sm is static (remains positive throughout course). APLA (anti-cardiolipin, lupus anticoagulant, anti-B2GP1) assessed for thrombosis risk.

A clinically useful teaching point: ANA is for diagnosis, not monitoring. Anti-dsDNA + C3/C4 are the monitoring pair — rising anti-dsDNA with falling complement predicts a flare. This guides decisions on escalating immunosuppression.

**Q:** What is the treatment approach for SLE organized by organ system and severity?

**A:** Mild/dermatologic: hydroxychloroquine (first-line; reduces mortality, prevents flares) + topical steroids, sunscreen. Musculoskeletal: NSAIDs + hydroxychloroquine. Organ-threatening: high-dose prednisone or IV methylprednisolone. Steroid-sparing: azathioprine, MTX, mycophenolate. Lupus nephritis: cyclophosphamide IV (induction) or mycophenolate mofetil (induction). Refractory: rituximab, anifrolumab (anti-Type I interferon). Moderate refractory: belimumab. APLA: avoid estrogen-containing contraceptives.

Hydroxychloroquine is the cornerstone of SLE treatment for all patients unless contraindicated — it reduces mortality and prevents flares. Cyclophosphamide is reserved for severe organ involvement (nephritis, cerebritis). Anifrolumab is a newer agent blocking Type I interferons, key to SLE pathogenesis.

**Q:** What are the classification criteria for Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APS), and what laboratory tests are required?

**A:** APS requires 1 clinical AND 1 laboratory criterion. Clinical: vascular thrombosis (confirmed by imaging/histopathology) OR pregnancy morbidity (≥1 fetal death ≥10 wk, premature birth <34 wk due to eclampsia/placental insufficiency, or ≥3 consecutive spontaneous abortions <10 wk). Laboratory: lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin IgG/IgM (medium-high titre >40 GPL/MPL), or anti-B2GP1 IgG/IgM (>99th percentile). Labs must be positive on 2 occasions ≥12 weeks apart.

The '12 weeks apart' requirement is critical to exclude transient antibody positivity from infection. Arterial and venous thromboses in APS are usually mutually exclusive. False positive VDRL is seen in APLA carriers due to phospholipid cross-reactivity.

**Q:** What is catastrophic APS, and how is it treated?

**A:** Catastrophic APS: development within 1 week of small vessel thrombotic occlusion in ≥3 organ systems with positive APLA. Associated with high mortality. Treatment: high-dose steroids + anticoagulation + cyclophosphamide + plasmapheresis. Treat any triggering factor (infection, malignancy). Refractory: rituximab (B cell depletion) or eculizumab (complement inhibition).

Catastrophic APS is a medical emergency. The multi-organ microvascular occlusion distinguishes it from typical APS. The treatment combines immunosuppression, anticoagulation, and plasma exchange to simultaneously reduce antibody burden and prevent further thrombosis.

**Q:** What is the anticoagulation target INR in APS for different thrombotic scenarios?

**A:** First venous thrombotic event: target INR 2.0-3.0 (lifelong warfarin). Recurrent venous event or arterial thrombosis: target INR >3.0, or INR 2-3 with addition of ASA. Obstetric APS: LMWH + ASA during pregnancy; prophylactic heparin for 6 weeks postpartum to reduce maternal thrombosis risk.

Lifelong anticoagulation distinguishes APS management from standard DVT treatment. Obstetric APS is treated with anticoagulation during pregnancy (warfarin is teratogenic, so LMWH is used) plus low-dose ASA. The postpartum period carries particularly high thrombosis risk.

**Q:** What are the two main subtypes of systemic sclerosis (scleroderma), and how do they differ?

**A:** Limited systemic sclerosis (CREST): skin involvement restricted to hands, face, neck (distal to elbows/knees); anti-centromere Ab; pulmonary HTN is major complication; survival >70% at 10 years. Diffuse systemic sclerosis: widespread skin disease (proximal, can involve trunk), early visceral involvement (renal, pulmonary fibrosis); anti-topoisomerase I (anti-Scl-70); survival 40-60% at 10 years.

CREST mnemonic: Calcinosis, Raynaud's, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia. Limited SSc has a more favorable prognosis but can develop pulmonary arterial HTN late. Diffuse SSc carries worse prognosis due to early renal and pulmonary fibrosis involvement.

**Q:** What is the ACR/EULAR sufficient criterion for classification of scleroderma, and what score is required for definite diagnosis?

**A:** Sufficient criterion: skin thickening of fingers of both hands extending proximal to the MCP joints (score 9, immediate definite classification). Otherwise, score ≥9 is required using additional items: puffy fingers (2), sclerodactyly (4), digital tip ulcers (4), fingertip pitting scars, telangiectasias (2), abnormal nailfold capillaries (3), pulmonary arterial HTN (2), ILD (2), Raynaud's phenomenon (2), scleroderma-related antibodies (2 each). Sensitivity 0.95, specificity 0.93.

The ACR/EULAR 2013 criteria for SSc replaced the 1980 criteria to improve sensitivity for early/limited disease. The sufficient criterion (proximal skin thickening) immediately classifies a patient. Without this, points are tallied across vascular, dermatologic, antibody, and organ involvement domains.

**Q:** What are the gastrointestinal manifestations of scleroderma, and what is the pathognomonic radiographic finding?

**A:** GI involvement in ~90% of SSc. Distal esophageal hypomotility → dysphagia. Loss of LES function → GERD, ulcerations, strictures. Small bowel hypomotility → bacterial overgrowth, diarrhea, bloating, malabsorption. Large bowel hypomotility + wide-mouth diverticula (pathognomonic on barium study). Treatment: PPIs for GERD; broad-spectrum antibiotics for bacterial overgrowth; prokinetics for motility disturbances.

Wide-mouth (saccular) diverticula on the antimesenteric border of the colon on barium enema are pathognomonic of scleroderma. GI hypomotility results from smooth muscle fibrosis. Unlike most GI conditions, the diverticula in SSc are wide-mouthed due to loss of smooth muscle support rather than mucosal herniation.

**Q:** How is interstitial lung disease (ILD) in systemic sclerosis treated, and which antibody predicts ILD risk?

**A:** Anti-topoisomerase I (anti-Scl-70) is associated with increased risk of ILD in SSc. First-line treatment: mycophenolate mofetil (less toxicity, better tolerated) or cyclophosphamide for 1 year. Other options: tocilizumab, rituximab. Anti-fibrotic therapy: nintedanib (TKI). Pulmonary HTN: vasodilators (bosentan, epoprostenol, PDE5 inhibitors). Rapidly progressive disease: consider hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

ILD and pulmonary HTN are the most common causes of morbidity and mortality in SSc. The SLS-II trial showed mycophenolate mofetil was equivalent to cyclophosphamide in SSc-ILD with better tolerability, making it the preferred agent. Nintedanib is specifically approved for SSc-ILD as an anti-fibrotic.

**Q:** What are Raynaud's phenomenon features that suggest a pathologic (secondary) cause versus primary (idiopathic)?

**A:** Features suggesting pathologic Raynaud's: new onset (not lifelong), asymmetric, precipitated by stimuli other than cold/emotion, associated with digital pulp pitting or tissue reabsorption, digital ischemia, capillary dilatation on capillaroscopy. DDx (COLD HAND): Connective tissue disease, Obstruction/Occupational, Lupus/other CTD, DM/Drugs, Hematologic (polycythemia/leukemia), Arterial problems, Neurologic, Disease unknown (idiopathic). Scleroderma is the most common secondary cause.

Primary Raynaud's is symmetric, triggered by cold/emotion, without tissue injury, and has no underlying disease. Secondary Raynaud's typically develops in middle age, may be asymmetric, causes tissue injury (pitting, gangrene), and has abnormal nailfold capillaries on capillaroscopy — a key screening tool.

**Q:** What are the classification and key features of Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathies (IIM)?

**A:** IIM include: Dermatomyositis (DMM), Polymyositis (PM), Necrotizing autoimmune myopathy (NAM), and Inclusion Body Myositis (IBM). Newer subgroups: immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, anti-synthetase syndrome, anti-MDA5 DM, cancer-associated myositis, overlap myositis. IBM: age >50, M>F, slowly progressive, predominantly distal > proximal weakness, vacuoles on biopsy, unresponsive to treatment. All associated with malignancy risk (DMM > PM).

IIM are characterized by proximal muscle weakness and elevated CK. IBM is unique in causing distal > proximal weakness and is treatment-refractory. The association with malignancy is highest in DMM, particularly with anti-p155/140 antibodies, requiring cancer screening at diagnosis.

**Q:** What are the characteristic skin findings of Dermatomyositis (DMM)?

**A:** Gottron's papules (pathognomonic): pink-violaceous flat-topped papules overlying dorsal surface of MCP and IP joints (occur in 70% of DMM). Gottron's sign: erythematous patches over extensor surfaces of elbows, knees, medial malleoli. Heliotrope rash: violaceous rash over eyelids with edema. Shawl sign: poikilodermatous erythema over neck, upper chest, shoulders. Mechanic's hands: dry, crackled lesions on palmar/lateral surfaces of digits (also seen in anti-synthetase syndrome). Periungual erythema, calcinosis.

Gottron's papules over MCP/IP joints are pathognomonic of DMM. Heliotrope rash is highly characteristic. The shawl sign reflects photodistributed involvement. Mechanic's hands, when present alongside ILD and anti-Jo-1 antibodies, constitute anti-synthetase syndrome.

**Q:** What is the pathophysiology of Polymyositis versus Dermatomyositis at the cellular level?

**A:** Polymyositis (PM): T cell-mediated process; CD8+ T cells are primary effectors; focal endomysial infiltrates surrounding muscle fibres; myocytes are primary target; occurs in adults. Dermatomyositis (DMM): complement-mediated process; perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (CD4+ > CD8+ T cells); leads to perifascicular atrophy of muscle fibres.

The cellular distinction between PM and DMM has implications for understanding immune pathways. PM is cell-mediated (MHC-I on myocytes triggers CD8+ cytotoxicity). DMM is humoral/complement-mediated (deposition of membrane attack complex in capillaries causes ischemic perifascicular atrophy). This explains why DMM has prominent skin findings.

**Q:** What investigations are used to diagnose Polymyositis/Dermatomyositis, and what is the role of muscle biopsy?

**A:** Lab: CK (elevated in 80% of DMM; elevated in PM), CBC, ESR/CRP, TSH, ANA, anti-Jo-1, anti-Mi-2, anti-SRP. EMG: characteristic changes of muscle inflammation/damage. MRI: used to localize biopsy site. Muscle biopsy: can aid in diagnosis but NOT needed if classic skin findings (Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash) plus proximal muscle weakness are present — clinical diagnosis sufficient in typical DMM.

Anti-Jo-1 is the most common myositis-specific antibody and is associated with ILD and anti-synthetase syndrome. Anti-Mi-2 is specific but not sensitive for DMM (15-20%). Anti-SRP is associated with necrotizing myopathy. CK elevation correlates with active muscle inflammation and is used to monitor treatment response.

**Q:** Which malignancy-associated features increase risk of cancer in inflammatory myopathy patients?

**A:** Risk factors for malignancy in IIM: age >50, DMM > PM (DMM has higher risk), elevated CK, peak incidence of malignancy at onset or within first year of myositis, dysphagia, ulcerative skin lesions, cutaneous vasculitis, anti-p155/140 antibodies. Malignancy screening is recommended at diagnosis and periodically thereafter.

The association between IIM and malignancy is well-established, particularly for DMM. Cancer can trigger inflammatory myopathy as a paraneoplastic syndrome. Anti-TIF1-γ (anti-p155/140) is strongly associated with cancer-associated myositis. Common associated cancers: ovarian, lung, GI, and breast carcinomas.

**Q:** A 35-year-old woman presents with symmetric joint swelling in her MCPs and PIPs, morning stiffness lasting 3 hours, elevated CRP, and positive RF and anti-CCP. How would you score her using the 2010 ACR/EULAR RA criteria?

**A:** Joint involvement: multiple small joints involved — if 4-10 small joints = 3 points, or if >10 joints = 5 points (assume at least 3 points). Serology: positive RF and anti-CCP — if >3× ULN = 3 points; if low positive = 2 points. Acute phase reactants: elevated CRP = 1 point. Symptom duration: assuming >6 weeks = 1 point. Total ≥6 = definite RA. Must confirm at least 1 joint with definite synovitis not better explained by another diagnosis.

This vignette is classic RA. The combination of small joint symmetric arthritis, morning stiffness, elevated inflammatory markers, and dual seropositivity (RF + anti-CCP) with symptoms >6 weeks would easily score ≥6. Early diagnosis enables prompt DMARD initiation within the therapeutic 'window of opportunity.'

**Q:** A patient with known SLE develops worsening proteinuria, rising anti-dsDNA titre, and falling C3/C4. What do these findings indicate and what is the next step?

**A:** This pattern indicates an SLE flare with likely lupus nephritis (worsening proteinuria = renal involvement). Rising anti-dsDNA and falling complement (C3/C4) are serologically concordant markers of disease activity. Next step: renal biopsy to classify lupus nephritis (Class I-VI). Treatment based on class: Class III/IV (proliferative) requires induction with cyclophosphamide IV or mycophenolate mofetil, plus high-dose steroids.

Anti-dsDNA and complement levels are the key monitoring tools in SLE (not ANA). Lupus nephritis Classes III and IV are the most severe and require aggressive immunosuppression. Mycophenolate mofetil is now preferred over cyclophosphamide for induction in many patients due to equivalent efficacy and better tolerability.

**Q:** What is anti-synthetase syndrome and what are its key features?

**A:** Anti-synthetase syndrome is a subtype of inflammatory myopathy characterized by: anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase antibodies (most commonly anti-Jo-1), interstitial lung disease (ILD), inflammatory myositis (proximal weakness), mechanic's hands (dry, crackled lesions on palmar/lateral digits), Raynaud's phenomenon, and non-erosive arthritis. It can occur in DMM and PM subsets.

Anti-Jo-1 is the most common myositis-specific antibody and defines the anti-synthetase syndrome. ILD is the most life-threatening feature. Mechanic's hands are a distinctive dermatologic finding. The syndrome requires aggressive immunosuppression targeting both myositis and ILD components.

**Q:** What is Inclusion Body Myositis (IBM) and how does it differ from Polymyositis?

**A:** IBM: age >50, M>F, slowly progressive course over years. Predominantly DISTAL > proximal muscle weakness (opposite of PM/DMM). Muscle biopsy: vacuoles (rimmed vacuoles) with inclusion bodies. Unresponsive to immunosuppressive treatment. Unlike PM (T cell-mediated, responds to steroids), IBM has degenerative features alongside inflammation and is treatment-refractory.

IBM is the most common acquired myopathy in patients >50 years. The distal pattern (finger flexors, knee extensors affected early) contrasts with PM/DMM. The treatment refractoriness is a key MCCQE1 point — steroids and other immunosuppressants do not benefit IBM. Genetic counselling and supportive care are the mainstays.

**Q:** What is the epidemiology of SLE and what factors increase disease risk?

**A:** Prevalence 0.05%. M:F = 1:10. Age of onset in reproductive years (15-45). More common and severe in Hispanic, Asian, and individuals of African descent. Risk increased by: combined OCP use, hormone replacement therapy, UV radiation, smoking, vitamin D deficiency, silica dust exposure. HLA-B8/DR3 association; ~10% positive family history. Bimodal mortality: early (active SLE/nephritis/infection) and late (atherosclerosis).

The strong female predominance (10:1) reflects estrogen's role in promoting SLE — estrogenic metabolites are elevated in affected males. The reproductive-age onset correlates with hormonal influence. Estrogen-containing contraceptives are relatively contraindicated, especially with APLA.

**Q:** What are the first-line and adjunctive pharmacological treatments for inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis/polymyositis)?

**A:** First-line: high-dose glucocorticoids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/d, not exceeding 80 mg/d) with slow taper after ~6 weeks of improvement. Add immunosuppressive agents (azathioprine, MTX). IVIG for severe or refractory muscle weakness with dysphagia/dyspnea. Rituximab for severe/refractory muscle disease or ILD. Hydroxychloroquine for dermatomyositis (DMM) rash.

High-dose steroids remain the cornerstone of induction therapy. Steroid-sparing agents reduce cumulative toxicity. IVIG is used when functional swallowing or breathing is compromised. Rituximab targets B-cell-mediated mechanisms in refractory cases.

**Q:** What malignancy surveillance is recommended for patients with inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis/polymyositis)?

**A:** Detailed history and physical (breast, pelvic, rectal exams). CXR, abdominal and pelvic ultrasound, fecal occult blood, Pap test, mammogram, plus CT scan of thorax, abdomen, and pelvis.

Inflammatory myopathies, especially dermatomyositis, are strongly associated with occult malignancy. Comprehensive surveillance at diagnosis and annually thereafter is essential to identify underlying cancers that may drive the myopathy.

**Q:** What is Sjögren's syndrome and what are its key epidemiologic features?

**A:** Autoimmune condition characterized by dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca/xerophthalmia) and dry mouth (xerostomia), caused by lymphocytic infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands. Prevalence 0.5%, F:M = 10:1, age 40–60 years. Primary and secondary forms (secondary associated with RA, SLE, DMM, HIV). Increased risk of non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (lifetime incidence 6–7%).

Sjögren's has a strong female predominance and can be primary or secondary to other CTDs. The significantly elevated risk of NHL (particularly MALT lymphoma) makes monitoring essential.

**Q:** What are the ACR/EULAR classification criteria items and scores for primary Sjögren's syndrome?

**A:** Score ≥4 required (after inclusion criteria met, no exclusion criteria): Labial salivary gland biopsy with focal lymphocytic sialadenitis (focus score ≥1/4mm²) = 3 points; Anti-SSA/Ro-positive = 3 points; Ocular staining score ≥5 (or van Bijsterveld ≥4 on at least one eye) = 1 point; Schirmer's test <5 mm/5 min on at least one eye = 1 point; Unstimulated whole saliva flow rate <0.1 mL/min = 1 point.

The ACR/EULAR 2017 criteria require ≥4 points with the highest-weighted items being salivary gland biopsy and Anti-SSA/Ro seropositivity. These two alone (total 6 points) can diagnose SS without functional tests.

**Q:** What are the extraglandular manifestations of Sjögren's syndrome?

**A:** Fatigue, low-grade fever; autoimmune thyroid dysfunction; arthralgias/arthritis (small joint, asymmetric, non-erosive); subclinical diffuse ILD, xerotrachea (dry chronic cough); renal disease, glomerulonephritis; palpable purpura, vasculitis; peripheral neuropathy; increased lymphoma risk.

Up to 20% of Sjögren's patients develop systemic extraglandular disease. The arthritis is non-erosive unless the patient has co-existing RA, in which case it becomes erosive and symmetric. Vasculitis and lymphoma risk are serious complications.

**Q:** What are the treatment options for Sjögren's syndrome?

**A:** Ocular: artificial tears/tear gel, moisture-retaining eyewear, humidifiers, surgical punctal occlusion. Oral: good dental hygiene, hydration, avoid alcohol/tobacco, pilocarpine (parasympathomimetic to stimulate salivary flow), topical nystatin or clotrimazole for oral candidiasis. Systemic: hydroxychloroquine and corticosteroids are generally ineffective; rituximab for severe organ-threatening disease (e.g., vasculitis).

Management is mostly symptomatic. Pilocarpine stimulates muscarinic receptors to increase salivation. Systemic immunosuppression has limited efficacy in Sjögren's except for specific organ-threatening complications like vasculitis.

**Q:** What defines Mixed Connective Tissue Disease (MCTD) and what is its key antibody?

**A:** MCTD is a syndrome with features of three different CTDs (SLE, scleroderma, myositis). Common symptoms include Raynaud's phenomenon and swollen fingers. The key antibody is anti-RNP. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a major cause of death.

MCTD overlaps multiple CTDs and is defined by anti-U1-RNP antibodies. The prognosis is variable — some go into remission, others evolve into a distinct CTD, and some develop severe disease with PAH as the leading cause of mortality.

**Q:** How is vasculitis classified by vessel size and what are the main categories?

**A:** Large vessel: GCA/temporal arteritis (affects extracranial arteries, age >50), Takayasu's arteritis (pulseless disease, young Asian adults). Medium vessel: PAN (ANCA-negative, lung-sparing), Kawasaki disease. Small vessel ANCA-associated: GPA (c-ANCA/PR3), MPA (p-ANCA/MPO), EGPA (50% ANCA+, MPO). Small vessel non-ANCA: IgA vasculitis (HSP), cryoglobulinemic vasculitis, cutaneous vasculitis, anti-GBM disease.

The Chapel Hill Consensus classification organizes vasculitides by vessel size, which guides clinical presentation and treatment approach. Knowing the prototypic antibody (ANCA subtype) is essential for MCCQE1.

**Q:** What are the classic clinical features of cutaneous (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis and its common causes?

**A:** Clinical: palpable purpura on lower extremities, vesicles, ulceration, urticaria, macules, papules, bullae, subcutaneous nodules. Renal or joint involvement may occur (especially in children). Causes: drug-induced (allopurinol, gold, sulfonamides, penicillin, phenytoin), viral/bacterial infection, idiopathic. Pathology: leukocytoclastic vasculitis of post-capillary venules with neutrophil debris. Treatment: stop offending drug, treat underlying disease, NSAIDs, low-dose corticosteroids.

Cutaneous vasculitis is the most common form and is characterized by immune complex deposition in small vessels (post-capillary venules). It is usually self-limiting but can recur and occasionally requires immunosuppression.

**Q:** What are the ACR 2022 classification criteria for GPA and the scoring threshold?

**A:** Diagnosed if score ≥5. Nasal involvement (crusting, ulcers, epistaxis, congestion, septal perforation): +3. Cartilaginous involvement (ear/nose, hoarseness, saddle nose): +2. Hearing loss: +1. c-ANCA or anti-PR3 positive: +5. Pulmonary nodules/mass/cavitation on chest imaging: +2. Granuloma/giant cells on biopsy: +2. Sinus/mastoid inflammation on imaging: +1. Pauci-immune GN on biopsy: +2. p-ANCA or anti-MPO: -1. Eosinophils >1×10⁹/L: -4.

The 2022 ACR criteria replace the older 1990 criteria. The high weight on PR3-ANCA (+5) and nasal involvement (+3) reflects their diagnostic specificity. Negative weights for MPO and eosinophilia help distinguish GPA from EGPA/MPA.

**Q:** What is the treatment for severe, life/organ-threatening Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA)?

**A:** Induction: IV glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone 0.5–1.0 g/d IV x1–3 d, then prednisone 1 mg/kg/d PO x2–4 wk with gradual taper) PLUS either cyclophosphamide (2 mg/kg/d PO max 3–6 mo, or IV 15 mg/kg every 2 wk x3 then every 3 wk) OR rituximab (375 mg/m² x4 weekly OR 1 g IV x2 doses 14 d apart). Maintenance: rituximab (1st line), or azathioprine/MTX/mycophenolate. Prophylaxis: TMP-SMX 160/800 mg PO 3x/wk for PJP. Plasma exchange NOT recommended for initial induction.

The RAVE trial demonstrated rituximab non-inferiority to cyclophosphamide for induction in ANCA-vasculitis. Rituximab is now also first-line maintenance. PJP prophylaxis is mandatory given the combination of cyclophosphamide and high-dose steroids.

**Q:** What are the key features that distinguish Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN) from other vasculitides?

**A:** PAN is a systemic necrotizing vasculitis of medium-sized vessels. It is ANCA-negative and classically lung-sparing. 5–10% associated with hepatitis B. Affects visceral arteries and their branches. CT/MRI angiography shows beading of blood vessels with aneurysms. Incidence 0.7/100,000; affects 40–60 year olds; M:F = 1:2.

PAN's lung-sparing nature and ANCA negativity distinguish it from GPA and MPA. The association with hepatitis B is clinically important — treatment differs (antivirals + plasma exchange rather than long-term immunosuppression). Aneurysms on angiography are characteristic.

**Q:** What is the Five-Factor Score (FFS) used for in PAN, and what factors comprise it?

**A:** The FFS is a survival prognostic score for PAN (and EGPA) that guides treatment intensity. Five factors: (1) cardiomyopathy due to vasculitis, (2) CNS involvement due to vasculitis, (3) severe GI involvement due to vasculitis, (4) serum creatinine >140 µmol/L, (5) proteinuria >1 g/day. FFS = 0 (no major organ involvement): glucocorticoids + azathioprine. FFS ≥1: high-dose glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide for 3–6 mo, then maintenance for minimum 18 months.

The FFS stratifies PAN patients by organ-threatening features to guide whether cyclophosphamide induction is required. A higher FFS correlates with worse prognosis and warrants more aggressive immunosuppression.

**Q:** What is the treatment for hepatitis B virus (HBV)-associated PAN?

**A:** Prednisone 1 mg/kg/d PO x7 d (then taper and withdraw by 14 d) + methylprednisolone 15 mg/kg/d IV x1–3 d. After corticosteroid therapy: plasma exchange + antiviral therapy. Rationale: prolonged immunosuppression promotes HBV replication; plasma exchange removes immune complexes; antivirals treat the underlying trigger.

HBV-associated PAN is treated differently because sustained immunosuppression promotes viral replication and worsening liver disease. The goal is to briefly dampen inflammation then transition to antiviral therapy to eliminate the trigger.

**Q:** What are the classification criteria for Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA) and the diagnostic threshold?

**A:** Absolute requirement: age ≥50. Score ≥6 required from: Morning stiffness in shoulders/neck (+2), Sudden visual loss (+3), Jaw or tongue claudication (+2), New temporal headache (+2), Scalp tenderness (+2), Abnormal temporal artery exam (+2), ESR ≥50 mm/h or CRP ≥10 mg/L (+3), Positive temporal artery biopsy or halo sign on temporal artery US (+5), Bilateral axillary involvement (+2), FDG-PET activity throughout aorta (+2). Cumulative score ≥6 = sensitivity 87%, specificity 95%.

The 2022 ACR GCA criteria give the highest weight to temporal artery biopsy/ultrasound halo sign (+5). New imaging modalities (PET, US) have replaced biopsy in some centres. The absolute age requirement (≥50) reflects that GCA almost never occurs in younger patients.

**Q:** What are the clinical features of Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)?

**A:** Most common vasculitis in North America. Age >50 (peak 70–80), F:M = 2:1. Features: new-onset temporal headache + scalp tenderness overlying temporal artery; sudden painless vision loss/diplopia (ophthalmic or posterior ciliary artery involvement); jaw/tongue claudication; PMR (proximal shoulder/pelvic girdle pain, stiffness, elevated ESR) — most common extracranial symptom; aortic arch syndrome (pulseless disease); constitutional symptoms. Untreated: permanent blindness in 20–25%.

GCA is a medical emergency when vision is threatened. The combination of jaw claudication and temporal headache in an elderly patient with elevated ESR/CRP should prompt immediate treatment before confirmation by biopsy to prevent irreversible vision loss.

**Q:** What is the treatment for GCA and what is the approach when vision loss is present?

**A:** Suspected GCA (no vision loss): immediately start prednisone 1 mg/kg PO in divided doses x2–4 wk, then taper 10 mg per 1–2 wk as symptoms resolve. With vision loss at diagnosis: methylprednisolone 1000 mg/d IV x3 d followed by high-dose prednisone 1 mg/kg/d PO with taper. Consider low-dose ASA if vertebral/carotid involvement. Tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor monoclonal Ab) can be added to glucocorticoids (new or relapsing). Screening: yearly CXR + abdominal US (thoracic aortic aneurysm risk).

Treatment must not be delayed for biopsy results as GCA biopsy remains positive for up to 2 weeks after starting steroids. Tocilizumab enables steroid-sparing and is approved for GCA. Long-term risk of thoracic aortic aneurysm warrants annual imaging.

**Q:** What are the ANCA subtypes and which vasculitides are they associated with?

**A:** c-ANCA (cytoplasmic pattern, PR3-ANCA): primarily GPA (Wegener's). p-ANCA (perinuclear pattern, MPO-ANCA): primarily MPA (70% ANCA+), EGPA (40–50%, mostly MPO). Note: p-ANCA with other antigens (lactoferrin, cathepsin, elastase) can be seen in IBD and other conditions. Only MPO antibodies have been associated with the development of vasculitis.

The ANCA subtype (c vs p; PR3 vs MPO) helps classify ANCA-associated vasculitis. PR3-ANCA strongly suggests GPA, while MPO-ANCA suggests MPA or EGPA. ANCA levels do not reliably predict remission or relapse.

**Q:** What is the EGPA (Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis, formerly Churg-Strauss) triad?

**A:** Triad: (1) Allergic rhinitis and asthma (often quiescent at time of vasculitis), (2) Eosinophilic infiltrative disease resembling pneumonia, (3) Systemic vasculitis often with mononeuritis multiplex/peripheral neuropathy and peripheral eosinophilia. 50% ANCA-positive (MPO-ANCA in 40–50%). Other: GI involvement, myocarditis, rarely coronary arteritis. Average age 40s.

EGPA is distinguished from GPA and MPA by its eosinophilia, asthma, and allergic rhinitis. The cardiac involvement (myocarditis, coronary arteritis) is a serious complication. The FFS is also used for EGPA to guide treatment intensity.

**Q:** What are the features of Takayasu's arteritis and how does it differ from GCA?

**A:** Takayasu's: 'Pulseless disease,' chronic inflammation of aorta and its branches. Most common in young adults of Asian descent, ages 10–40, F>M. Risk of aortic aneurysm. Presents with unequal peripheral pulses, limb claudication, BP differences between arms. GCA: age >50, predominantly affects extracranial vessels, temporal headache, jaw claudication, vision loss, PMR.

Both are large vessel vasculitides but differ by age and geography. Takayasu's affects younger Asian women; GCA affects older Northern European/North American patients. Both can cause aortic aneurysm as a late complication.

**Q:** What are the features of Behcet's disease and Buerger's disease (Thromboangiitis Obliterans)?

**A:** Behcet's: multi-system disorder with uveitis, recurrent oral AND genital ulceration, venous thrombosis, skin and joint involvement. Most common in Mediterranean and Asian populations, average age 30, M>F. Buerger's: inflammation and clotting of small/medium arteries and veins of distal extremities, leading to distal claudication and gangrene. Most important etiology: cigarette smoking. Most common in young Asian males.

Behcet's is unique with its combined oral-genital ulceration and uveitis. The pathergy test (pustule formation at skin prick sites) is characteristic. Buerger's is essentially limited to smokers and cessation is the primary treatment.

**Q:** What HLA association and sex ratio characterize ankylosing spondylitis (AS)?

**A:** HLA-B27 positive in 90–95% of AS patients (vs. 9% of general population). M:F = 3:1 (females have milder disease, more peripheral arthritis and upper spine spondylitis, and may be under-diagnosed). Rule of 2s: AS occurs in 2% of HLA-B27 positive individuals, and 20% of HLA-B27 positive individuals with an affected family member.

HLA-B27 is the strongest genetic risk factor for AS but is neither necessary nor sufficient. Its prevalence in the general population explains why most HLA-B27 positive people do not develop AS. Family history increases risk 10-fold.

**Q:** What are the ASAS classification criteria for axial spondyloarthritis?

**A:** Back pain ≥3 months AND age of onset <45 years, PLUS either: (A) Sacroiliitis on imaging (active inflammation on MRI suggestive of sacroiliitis OR definite radiographic sacroiliitis grade ≥2 bilaterally or grade 3–4 unilaterally) + ≥1 SpA feature, OR (B) HLA-B27 positive + ≥2 SpA features. SpA features: HLA-B27, inflammatory back pain, arthritis, enthesitis (heel), uveitis, dactylitis, psoriasis, IBD (Crohn's/colitis), good response to NSAIDs, family history of SpA, elevated CRP.

The ASAS criteria enable classification of axial SpA including non-radiographic axial SpA (nr-axSpA), which lacks structural damage on x-ray but has MRI evidence of active inflammation. This is important for early recognition and treatment.

**Q:** What are the axial clinical features of ankylosing spondylitis?

**A:** Mid and lower back stiffness, morning stiffness >1 h, night pain, alternating buttock pain, painful SI joint (FABER test positive). Spinal restriction: decreased lumbar ROM (Schöber test), decreased chest expansion (<5 cm at T4), decreased cervical extension. Postural changes: decreased lumbar lordosis + increased thoracic kyphosis + increased cervical flexion → increased occiput to wall distance (>5 cm). X-ray: 'bamboo spine' (bridging syndesmophytes), 'shiny corner sign,' symmetric sacroiliitis.

The Modified Schöber test measures lumbar flexion (normal increase ≥5 cm from baseline). The postural changes in AS are progressive, with loss of lumbar lordosis being an early finding. The 'bamboo spine' is a late radiographic finding from bridging syndesmophytes (ossified outer annulus fibrosus).

**Q:** What are the extra-articular manifestations of ankylosing spondylitis (5 A's)?

**A:** Anterior uveitis (acute, 25–30% of patients); Aortic incompetence/aortitis; Amyloidosis (kidneys, late and rare); Autoimmune bowel disease (IBD — UC/Crohn's); Apical fibrosis (rare). Also: cardiac conduction disturbances, IgA nephropathy, cauda equina syndrome (rare), psoriasis.

The mnemonic '5 A's' captures the key extra-articular manifestations. Anterior uveitis is the most common. Cardiac complications (aortic regurgitation, conduction defects) occur in a minority but have significant morbidity. Consider AS in the differential for causes of aortic regurgitation in young males.

**Q:** How does inflammatory back pain differ from mechanical back pain?

**A:** Inflammatory: insidious onset, age <45, improved with exercise, worse with rest, worse in 2nd half of night, morning stiffness >1 h, responds to NSAIDs, involves other systems (uveitis, IBD), positive family history. Mechanical: acute onset, any age (15–90), worse with exercise, better with rest, morning stiffness <30 min, anatomic pain radiation (L5-S1), may have sensory/motor deficits.

Distinguishing inflammatory from mechanical back pain is critical for early AS diagnosis. The key distinguishing features are improvement with exercise, worsening with rest, prolonged morning stiffness, nocturnal pain, and NSAID responsiveness in inflammatory back pain.

**Q:** What is the pharmacological treatment of ankylosing spondylitis?

**A:** First line: NSAIDs (both peripheral and axial disease). Glucocorticoids: topical eye drops, local injections; occasional systemic use. Conventional DMARDs (SSZ, MTX): only for peripheral arthritis — NOT effective for axial disease. If inadequate response to 2 NSAIDs (or DMARD for peripheral disease): anti-TNF agents or anti-IL-17 inhibitors (both axial and peripheral). Manage extra-articular manifestations separately.

The key distinction is that conventional DMARDs do not work for axial AS — only biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17) are effective for axial disease unresponsive to NSAIDs. This differs from RA where conventional DMARDs are first-line. Anti-IL-17 (secukinumab) is preferred when IBD is NOT present.

**Q:** What are the features of enteropathic arthritis (EA)?

**A:** MSK manifestations in UC or Crohn's disease. Two patterns: (1) Spondylitis — HLA-B27 associated, M>F, may precede IBD, does NOT parallel IBD course, symmetric sacroiliitis; (2) Peripheral arthritis — not HLA-B27 associated, M=F, does NOT precede IBD, DOES parallel IBD course. Treatment: NSAIDs (use cautiously as they may exacerbate bowel disease) for spondylitis; NSAIDs, DMARDs, TNF inhibitors for peripheral arthritis.

The important distinction is that the spondylitis component of EA behaves independently of bowel disease activity, while peripheral arthritis tracks with IBD flares. This has treatment implications — treating the IBD may improve peripheral arthritis but not the spondylitis.

**Q:** What are the 5 patterns of joint involvement in psoriatic arthritis (PsA)?

**A:** (1) Asymmetric oligoarthritis: <5 small/large joints, asymmetric — most common (70%); (2) Arthritis of DIPs (nail changes more common); (3) Symmetric polyarthritis (similar to RA); (4) Asymmetric sacroiliitis and spondylitis (usually older male patients); (5) Arthritis mutilans (destructive, deforming small joint polyarthritis). Also: dactylitis, enthesopathy, morning stiffness >30 min (50%).

PsA has heterogeneous joint presentations. The DIP involvement with nail changes and arthritis mutilans are particularly characteristic of PsA and rarely seen in RA. Pencil-in-cup deformity and floating syndesmophytes are classic radiographic findings.

**Q:** What are the CASPAR criteria for psoriatic arthritis and the diagnostic threshold?

**A:** Patient must have inflammatory articular disease (joint, spine, or entheseal) PLUS ≥3 points from: (1) Evidence of psoriasis: current skin (2 pts), past history or family history (1 pt each); (2) Psoriatic nail dystrophy (onycholysis, pitting, hyperkeratosis) = 1 pt; (3) Negative RF = 1 pt; (4) Dactylitis (current or past) = 1 pt; (5) Juxta-articular bone formation on hand/foot x-rays = 1 pt.

The CASPAR criteria (2006) have high sensitivity (91.4%) and specificity (98.7%) for PsA. Current psoriasis is weighted most heavily (2 points). Negative RF helps distinguish PsA from seropositive RA. Juxta-articular bone formation (periostitis) is a distinctive radiographic feature.

**Q:** What are the biologic treatment options for psoriatic arthritis?

**A:** Anti-TNF agents (first-line biologic). Anti-IL-17: secukinumab, ixekizumab, bimekizumab. Anti-IL-23: guselkumab, risankizumab. Anti-IL-12/23: ustekinumab. JAK inhibitors. Also: non-biologic DMARDs (MTX, SSZ, leflunomide) for peripheral joint involvement. NSAIDs and/or IA steroids as adjuvants (not sole therapy >3 months). Treat skin lesions (steroid cream, salicylic/retinoic acid, tar, UV light).

PsA treatment targets both skin and joints. IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors are particularly effective for psoriatic skin involvement. TNF inhibitors remain first-line biologics. JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) are approved for PsA. DMARDs do not treat axial disease.

**Q:** What is reactive arthritis and what are its classic clinical triad and triggering infections?

**A:** Seronegative spondyloarthropathy with peripheral arthritis + ≥1 extra-articular manifestation appearing 1–4 weeks after GI or GU infection. Classic triad: conjunctivitis/uveitis + urethritis/cervicitis + arthritis ('Can't See, Can't Pee, Can't Climb a Tree'). GI triggers: Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, C. difficile. GU triggers: Chlamydia (16–44% of cases), Mycoplasma. Epidemiology: ages 20–40; M>F if GU-associated, M=F if GI-associated.

Reactive arthritis is a post-infectious arthritis where the causative organism is typically NOT cultured from the joint (sterile). The classic triad occurs in <33% of patients but is pathognomonic. Chlamydia is the most common GU trigger in developed countries.

**Q:** What are the skin findings diagnostic of reactive arthritis?

**A:** Keratoderma blennorrhagicum: hyperkeratotic skin lesions on palms and soles (resembles pustular psoriasis). Balanitis circinata: small, shallow, painless ulcers of the glans penis and urethral meatus. These dermatologic findings, combined with the classic triad, are diagnostic of reactive arthritis.

Keratoderma blennorrhagicum and balanitis circinata are pathognomonic dermatologic findings of reactive arthritis. They occur regardless of whether the triggering infection was GI or GU in origin, highlighting the immune-mediated (not directly infectious) nature of the disease.

**Q:** What is the treatment for reactive arthritis?

**A:** Antibiotics for non-articular infections. NSAIDs (naproxen 500 mg PO BID/TID, diclofenac 50 mg PO TID, indomethacin 50 mg PO TID/QID), physiotherapy, exercise. Local therapy: IA steroid injection (triamcinolone acetonide), topical steroids for ocular involvement. Note: course is usually weeks to months, often recurring; spinal involvement may persist.

NSAIDs are the primary treatment for articular symptoms. Antibiotic treatment of the triggering infection (particularly for Chlamydia) may reduce disease duration and recurrence. DMARDs (SSZ, MTX) may be needed for persistent or refractory disease.

**Q:** Compare HLA-B27 positivity rates across the seronegative spondyloarthropathies.

**A:** Ankylosing spondylitis (AS): 90–95%. Reactive arthritis (ReA): 80%. Psoriatic arthritis (PsA): 40% (higher if spondylitis present). Enteropathic arthritis (EA): 30% (spondylitis component is HLA-B27 associated; peripheral arthritis is not). General population: ~9%.

HLA-B27 is most strongly associated with AS. In PsA, HLA-B27 prevalence is low overall but increases when axial involvement is present (approaching AS-like rates). This supports the concept that HLA-B27 drives axial rather than peripheral disease across spondyloarthropathies.

**Q:** What radiographic findings are characteristic of ankylosing spondylitis on spinal X-ray?

**A:** Early: 'Shiny corner sign' — erosion and sclerosis at corners of vertebral bodies. Progressive: squaring of vertebral bodies. Late: ossification of outer fibres of annulus fibrosus → bridging syndesmophytes → 'bamboo spine.' SI joints: pseudowidening (erosion) → bony fusion; symmetric sacroiliitis. MRI (early disease): bone marrow edema best seen on T2 STIR sequences.

The shiny corner sign (Romanus lesion) represents early active inflammation at vertebral endplates. Bridging syndesmophytes (vertical) differ from the bulky, non-marginal osteophytes of DISH and the non-marginal 'skip' syndesmophytes of PsA ('floating syndesmophytes').

**Q:** What features of Behcet's disease distinguish it from other vasculitides?

**A:** Multi-system disorder with: uveitis (ocular), recurrent oral AND genital ulceration (pathognomonic combination), venous thrombosis, skin involvement, joint involvement. Most common in Mediterranean (Turkey highest) and Asian populations. Average age 30, M>F. Pathergy test: pustule formation 24–48 h after skin prick. Not classified as a true vasculitis by Chapel Hill Consensus — it is a 'variable vessel vasculitis.'

The combination of recurrent oral + genital ulcers + uveitis is the clinical hallmark of Behcet's disease. Unlike other vasculitides that affect a specific vessel calibre, Behcet's can affect vessels of any size. It is more common along the ancient Silk Road.

**Q:** What are the features of IgA vasculitis (formerly Henoch-Schönlein Purpura) and how is it classified?

**A:** Small vessel non-ANCA-associated vasculitis. Vascular deposition of IgA causing systemic vasculitis. Classic tetrad: palpable purpura (skin, usually lower extremities), arthritis/arthralgias, abdominal pain (GI involvement), renal involvement (IgA nephropathy-like). Most common systemic vasculitis in childhood. Usually self-limiting. Classified as small vessel non-ANCA-associated immune complex-mediated vasculitis.

IgA vasculitis is the most common vasculitis in children and shares pathophysiology with IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease). Both involve IgA immune complex deposition. Adults tend to have more severe renal involvement. Treatment is usually supportive.

**Q:** What is cryoglobulinemic vasculitis and what conditions are associated with it?

**A:** Systemic vasculitis caused by circulating cryoproteins forming immune complexes. Associations: 60–80% due to hepatitis C, 5–10% due to a CTD (SLE, RA, SS), 5–10% due to a lymphoproliferative disorder, 5–10% idiopathic ('essential'). It is a small vessel non-ANCA-associated vasculitis.

Hepatitis C is by far the most common cause of cryoglobulinemic vasculitis. Cryoglobulins precipitate in cold temperatures and cause immune complex deposition in small vessels. Treatment involves addressing the underlying cause (e.g., HCV antiviral therapy) plus immunosuppression for severe manifestations.

**Q:** What psoriatic nail changes are seen in psoriatic arthritis and why are they clinically important?

**A:** Nail changes: pitting, transverse or longitudinal ridging, discolouration, subungual hyperkeratosis, onycholysis (nail separating from nail bed), oil drop sign. Clinical importance: nail lesions are an important predictor for the development of PsA in patients with cutaneous psoriasis. 'Check hidden areas for psoriatic lesions: ears, hairline, umbilicus, gluteal cleft, nails.'

Nail involvement in psoriasis reflects enthesitis at the nail insertion and correlates with DIP arthritis. The presence of nail changes should prompt screening for PsA. The oil drop sign (salmon-coloured discolouration under the nail) is particularly specific for psoriasis.

**Q:** How does the spondylitis of ankylosing spondylitis progress pathologically?

**A:** Inflammation → osteopenia → erosion → ossification → osteoproliferation (syndesmophytes). Pathologically: inflammatory cells infiltrate the entheses (insertion points of ligaments/tendons) → erosion of bone → reactive new bone formation → bridging syndesmophytes from outer annulus fibrosus ossification → 'bamboo spine.' This contrasts with RA where inflammation leads to erosion without new bone formation.

AS is unique among inflammatory arthritides in that the end result is excessive bone formation (ankylosis/fusion) rather than pure erosion. This paradox (erosion followed by new bone formation) is thought to be driven by TNF-mediated inflammation and Wnt/BMP signalling.

**Q:** What is the classic crystal type in gout, and how does it appear under polarized light microscopy?

**A:** Monosodium urate (MSU) crystals; negatively birefringent (yellow when parallel to the compensator filter), needle-shaped.

Negative birefringence distinguishes MSU crystals from calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals seen in pseudogout, which are positively birefringent (blue when parallel) and rhomboid-shaped. This distinction is essential for diagnosis on joint aspirate.

**Q:** A 55-year-old man presents with acute severe pain, redness and swelling of the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint. Joint aspirate shows negatively birefringent, needle-shaped crystals. What is the diagnosis and what are the first-line acute treatment options?

**A:** Acute gout (podagra). First-line acute treatments: NSAIDs (high-dose, then taper), colchicine (1.2 mg at first signs, then 0.6 mg 1 h later, then 0.6 mg BID until attack resolves), or corticosteroids (IA preferred; PO or IM if NSAIDs contraindicated).

The first MTP joint (podagra) is the classic site of acute gout. Joint aspirate showing >90% MSU crystals confirms the diagnosis. Colchicine is most effective when started within the first 12-24 hours. Corticosteroids are preferred when renal, cardiovascular, or GI disease precludes NSAIDs.

**Q:** What are the indications for initiating urate-lowering therapy (ULT) in gout?

**A:** Indications for ULT: ≥2 gout attacks per year, presence of tophi, bone erosions/arthritis on imaging.

Per 2020 ACR guidelines, allopurinol is preferred over all other ULTs as first-line for all patients including those with CKD stage ≥3. Concomitant anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (colchicine, NSAIDs, or prednisone/prednisolone) should be initiated with ULT. The serum urate target is <6 mg/dL.

**Q:** What drug mnemonic summarizes the major precipitants of acute gout, and what drugs and foods are included?

**A:** Drugs: 'FACT' — low-dose aspirin, Cyclosporine, Thiazide diuretics. Foods: 'SALT' — Seafood, Alcohol (beer and spirits), Liver and kidney, Turkey (meat).

These precipitants raise serum uric acid either by decreasing renal excretion (e.g., thiazides, cyclosporine, low-dose ASA) or by providing exogenous purines (high-purine foods). Fructose/sugar-sweetened foods are also important dietary precipitants.

**Q:** What are the radiographic findings characteristic of gout vs. pseudogout?

**A:** Gout: punched-out bone erosions with 'overhanging edges' (Martel sign), soft tissue tophi. Pseudogout: chondrocalcinosis (radiodensities in fibrocartilage/hyaline cartilage, e.g., knee menisci).

In gout, tophi deposit adjacent to joints and erode bone, creating the classic overhanging edge on x-ray. In pseudogout (CPPD), calcium pyrophosphate deposits in cartilage appear as chondrocalcinosis on plain x-ray, visible in 75% of cases. Ultrasound in gout shows the double-contour sign.

**Q:** Compare gout and pseudogout with regard to gender, age, crystal type, crystal shape, birefringence, and classic joint distribution.

**A:** Gout: M>F, middle-aged males; monosodium urate, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent (yellow when parallel); first MTP (podagra), midfoot, ankle, knee. Pseudogout: M=F, usually elderly or post-menopausal; CPP, rhomboid-shaped, positively birefringent (blue when parallel); knee and wrist most common.

Differentiating these two crystal arthropathies is a high-yield MCCQE1 topic. Both can cause acute inflammatory monoarthritis, but the crystal characteristics, birefringence patterns, demographic profile, and joint distribution differ importantly.

**Q:** What is the first-line urate-lowering therapy for gout, including its use in chronic kidney disease (CKD)?

**A:** Allopurinol is first-line ULT for all patients, including those with CKD stage ≥3. Start low and titrate up. Febuxostat is second-line but should NOT be used in patients with a history of cardiovascular disease.

Both allopurinol and febuxostat inhibit xanthine oxidase, reducing uric acid production. Allopurinol is not nephrotoxic and can be used in CKD with dose adjustment. Febuxostat showed increased cardiovascular mortality in a landmark trial, hence the contraindication in cardiovascular disease. Anti-inflammatory prophylaxis for 3-6 months is required when starting ULT to prevent flares.

**Q:** What conditions are associated with calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) deposition disease (pseudogout)?

**A:** Old age, advanced osteoarthritis, neuropathic joints; metabolic associations: hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia (low ALP), hemochromatosis.

A mnemonic for metabolic causes of CPPD: 'HHHH' — Hyperparathyroidism, Hypothyroidism, Hypomagnesemia, Hemochromatosis, plus Hypophosphatasia. These are commonly tested on licensing exams. Identifying an underlying metabolic cause is important as treatment of the cause may slow crystal deposition.

**Q:** What are the clinical presentations of CPPD disease, and how is acute CPPD managed?

**A:** CPPD presentations: asymptomatic chondrocalcinosis, acute crystal arthritis (pseudogout flare), pseudo-OA, pseudo-RA. Acute CPPD treatment: joint aspiration, intra-articular steroid injection, NSAIDs, cool packs, rest, splinting.

CPPD can mimic several other rheumatologic conditions (hence the 'pseudo' terminology). Acute attacks are often triggered by dehydration, illness, surgery, or trauma. Unlike gout, there is no disease-modifying therapy for CPPD, and no treatment is required for asymptomatic chondrocalcinosis.

**Q:** What are the required criteria and scoring algorithm for classifying Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)?

**A:** Required criteria (all must be present): age >50 years, bilateral shoulder aching, and abnormal ESR or CRP. Scoring (without ultrasound): ≥4 points = PMR. Items scored: morning stiffness >45 min (2 pts), hip pain or limited ROM (1 pt), absence of RF or ACPA (2 pts), absence of other joint involvement (1 pt). With ultrasound: ≥5 points = PMR (additional US criteria can add up to 2 more points).

The 2012 ACR/EULAR PMR classification criteria allow diagnosis based on clinical and laboratory findings, with optional ultrasound to increase sensitivity and specificity. PMR has a strong association with giant cell arteritis (GCA); patients must be monitored for GCA symptoms.

**Q:** How is Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR) treated, and what are key monitoring considerations?

**A:** Start prednisone 12.5-25 mg PO once daily. Expect response within days; reconsider diagnosis if no response. Taper slowly over approximately 1 year. Treat relapses aggressively (50% relapse rate). Monitor for: steroid side effects, glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (prevention), and GCA symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness).

PMR responds dramatically to low-dose corticosteroids — this rapid response is itself diagnostically helpful. Do not treat a rise in ESR alone as a relapse; use clinical assessment. The strong association with GCA means all PMR patients must be counseled about GCA symptoms, as GCA can cause blindness if untreated.

**Q:** What are the 2010 ACR diagnostic criteria for fibromyalgia?

**A:** All three must be met: (1) Widespread Pain Index (WPI) ≥7 AND Symptom Severity (SS) score ≥5, OR WPI 3-6 AND SS score ≥9; (2) symptoms present at similar level for ≥3 months; (3) no other disorder that would otherwise explain the pain. The SS score includes: severity of fatigue, waking unrefreshed, cognitive symptoms, and extent of somatic symptoms (each rated 0-3).

The 2010 criteria replaced the older tender point examination and emphasize the multisystem symptomatic burden of fibromyalgia. The WPI tallies up to 19 body areas. This criteria are validated by the 2012 Canadian Guidelines for Diagnosis and Management of Fibromyalgia.

**Q:** What is the recommended pharmacological management of fibromyalgia?

**A:** First-line: low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline) for sleep restoration. SNRIs: duloxetine, milnacipran. Anticonvulsants: pregabalin, gabapentin. Opioids are NOT encouraged. NSAIDs are no longer encouraged (controversial). Pharmacotherapy is adjunctive — not curative.

Per the 2012 Canadian Guidelines, fibromyalgia management is multimodal. Non-pharmacological therapy (graded aerobic exercise, resistance training, CBT) is the cornerstone. Pharmacological agents provide only modest benefit. Tricyclics are chosen for lower anticholinergic side effect profiles when possible.

**Q:** What non-pharmacological therapies are recommended for fibromyalgia?

**A:** Graded aerobic exercise (>20 min/day, 2-3 days/week), resistance training (>8 repetitions per exercise, 2-3 days/week), counseling on self-management strategies. Therapies with some evidence: acupuncture, cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), hydrotherapy, meditative movement (yoga, Tai chi).

Exercise is the most consistently effective intervention for fibromyalgia. The 2012 Canadian Guidelines and EULAR 2017 revised recommendations both emphasize that a healthy lifestyle and active patient participation are the cornerstone of care. Non-pharmacological therapy should be offered to all patients.

**Q:** How do Inflammatory Myositis, PMR, and Fibromyalgia differ in terms of muscle weakness, ESR/CRP, and treatment?

**A:** Inflammatory Myositis: proximal muscle weakness YES, ESR/CRP usually normal, treatment = high-dose steroids + immunosuppressants. PMR: weakness NO (periarticular pain only), ESR/CRP markedly elevated, treatment = low-dose steroids. Fibromyalgia: weakness NO, ESR/CRP normal, treatment = exercise + sleep restoration.

Muscle weakness is a key distinguishing feature — it is present only in inflammatory myositis (polymyositis/dermatomyositis). PMR has significantly elevated inflammatory markers and responds dramatically to low-dose steroids. Fibromyalgia has entirely normal investigations and is managed non-pharmacologically first.

**Q:** What are the first-line and second-line analgesic treatments for osteoarthritis (OA), and what are the key contraindications to consider?

**A:** First-line: acetaminophen 1000 mg TID (max 3 g/day) — avoid in severe liver disease. Second-line: NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen) — avoid with GI bleed, renal impairment, ASA/NSAID allergy, pregnancy (T3), PUD, anticoagulants. COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib): preferred in dyspepsia/GERD but avoid in renal impairment and cardiovascular disease.

Canadian and ACR/EULAR guidelines recommend a stepwise approach to OA pharmacotherapy. Topical NSAIDs (e.g., diclofenac gel — Pennsaid®, Voltaren Emulgel®) are an option for patients who fail acetaminophen but wish to avoid systemic therapy, particularly for small joints. IA corticosteroid injections provide short-term (weeks to months) benefit.

**Q:** What are the monitoring requirements for hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil®) used as a DMARD?

**A:** Annual ophthalmological screening to monitor for retinopathy. Maintenance dose: 200-400 mg PO once daily (5 mg/kg ideal body weight per day, maximum 400 mg/day). Contraindicated in pre-existing retinal disease.

Hydroxychloroquine-induced retinopathy (bull's-eye maculopathy) is the major serious adverse effect. It is typically dose-dependent and cumulative. Annual ophthalmology screening is mandated. Hydroxychloroquine is widely used in RA, SLE, and Sjögren's syndrome due to its favorable safety profile compared to other DMARDs.

**Q:** What are the major adverse effects and contraindications of methotrexate (MTX) when used as a DMARD?

**A:** Adverse effects: oral ulcers, GI symptoms, cirrhosis, myelosuppression, pneumonitis, tubular necrosis, fatigue. Contraindications: bone marrow suppression, liver disease, significant lung disease, immunodeficiency, pregnancy, EtOH use, moderate-to-severe kidney disease.

MTX is the anchor DMARD for RA. Folic acid supplementation (1 mg/day) is routinely co-administered to reduce side effects, particularly oral ulcers and GI symptoms. LFTs, CBC, and creatinine should be monitored regularly. Pregnancy must be avoided for ≥3 months after stopping MTX.

**Q:** What are the major adverse effects of leflunomide (Arava®), and in which patients is it contraindicated?

**A:** Adverse effects: alopecia, GI symptoms, liver dysfunction, interstitial pulmonary fibrosis, hypertension. Contraindicated in: liver disease, lung disease, pregnancy.

Leflunomide is a pyrimidine synthesis inhibitor used as an alternative DMARD in RA. It has teratogenic potential, and a cholestyramine washout protocol is required before pregnancy. In the landmark leflunomide RA trial, it showed similar efficacy to MTX for active RA.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of the following biologics: adalimumab, etanercept, rituximab, tocilizumab, abatacept?

**A:** Adalimumab: monoclonal anti-TNF. Etanercept: fusion protein of TNF receptor and Fc portion of IgG (decoy receptor). Rituximab: anti-CD20, causes B cell depletion. Tocilizumab: IL-6 receptor antagonist. Abatacept: costimulation modulator of T cell activation (CTLA4-Ig).

These five biologics represent distinct mechanisms of immunomodulation in rheumatology. Biosimilars are available in Canada for adalimumab, etanercept, and rituximab. Understanding the mechanism helps predict the class of adverse effects (e.g., TNF inhibitors increase risk of TB reactivation; rituximab depletes B cells causing hypogammaglobulinemia).

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of tofacitinib and upadacitinib, and how do they differ from traditional biologics?

**A:** Both are JAK inhibitors that interfere with the JAK-STAT intracellular signaling pathway. Tofacitinib (Xeljanz®): selective JAK 1/3 inhibitor, 5 mg PO BID. Upadacitinib (Rinvoq®): selective JAK inhibitor, 15 mg PO once daily. Unlike biologics, JAK inhibitors are small-molecule oral drugs.

JAK inhibitors represent a class of targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs). Their oral bioavailability is a major clinical advantage over injectable biologics. Safety considerations include increased risk of infections, VTE, and cardiovascular events (particularly tofacitinib). Screening for TB, hepatitis, and lipids is required before initiation.

**Q:** What did the COMET trial demonstrate about combination therapy vs. methotrexate monotherapy in early RA?

**A:** MTX + etanercept achieved clinical remission in 50% of patients vs. 28% on MTX alone. Radiographic non-progression occurred in 80% (combination) vs. 59% (MTX alone). Both were statistically significant (P<0.0001).

The COMET trial (Lancet 2008) established that combination therapy with a biologic (etanercept) plus MTX is superior to MTX monotherapy for achieving both clinical remission and preventing radiographic joint damage in early moderate-to-severe RA.

**Q:** What were the key findings of the PREMIER trial regarding adalimumab + MTX combination therapy in early RA?

**A:** ACR50 response achieved in 62% (combination) vs. 46% (MTX alone) vs. 47% (adalimumab alone). Combination therapy caused significantly less radiographic progression than either monotherapy (P<0.002). 49% of combination patients achieved remission at 2 years.

PREMIER (Arthritis Rheum 2006) showed that adalimumab + MTX combination is superior to either agent alone in early aggressive RA. This supports the treat-to-target strategy with early aggressive combination therapy in RA.

**Q:** What did the RAVE trial demonstrate about rituximab vs. cyclophosphamide in ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV)?

**A:** 64% of rituximab patients achieved remission without prednisone at 6 months vs. 53% of cyclophosphamide patients (noninferior, P<0.001). For relapsing disease, rituximab was superior: 67% vs. 42% remission (P=0.01).

RAVE (NEJM 2010) established rituximab as an effective alternative to cyclophosphamide for remission induction in severe AAV, with particular superiority in relapsing disease. This is important because cyclophosphamide carries significant toxicity (hemorrhagic cystitis, bladder cancer, gonadal toxicity).

**Q:** In ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) remission maintenance, which is superior: mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine?

**A:** Azathioprine (AZA) is superior. In the IMPROVE trial, mycophenolate mofetil had significantly more relapses (55%) compared to AZA (37.5%) for maintenance therapy after cyclophosphamide-induced remission.

IMPROVE (JAMA 2010) contrasts with lupus nephritis, where mycophenolate mofetil is preferred. In AAV, AZA remains the standard maintenance therapy. This distinction between AAV and lupus nephritis is a high-yield MCCQE1 differentiation point.

**Q:** What did the Belimumab trial (BLISS) demonstrate in SLE, and what is belimumab's mechanism of action?

**A:** Belimumab (BLyS inhibitor/anti-BAFF monoclonal antibody) significantly improved SLE Responder Index rates vs. placebo: 51% (1 mg/kg) and 58% (10 mg/kg) vs. 44% placebo. It was the first biologic specifically approved for SLE.

BLyS (B lymphocyte stimulator, also called BAFF) promotes B cell survival. Belimumab inhibits BLyS, reducing autoreactive B cell survival and autoantibody production. The Lancet 2011 trial established its efficacy in seropositive, active SLE patients already on standard of care.

**Q:** What did the landmark mycophenolate mofetil vs. cyclophosphamide trial (Ginzler 2005) show for lupus nephritis?

**A:** Mycophenolate mofetil achieved complete remission in 22.5% of patients vs. 5.8% for IV cyclophosphamide (P=0.005). Mycophenolate mofetil was more efficacious with a better safety profile for inducing remission in active lupus nephritis.

This NEJM 2005 trial changed practice by establishing mycophenolate mofetil as the preferred induction therapy for lupus nephritis, replacing the more toxic cyclophosphamide. This contrasts with AAV (where cyclophosphamide remains standard induction), making this distinction clinically and examination-relevant.

**Q:** What did the CYCLOPS trial demonstrate about pulse vs. daily oral cyclophosphamide in ANCA-associated vasculitis?

**A:** Pulse cyclophosphamide (15 mg/kg IV every 2-3 weeks) was equivalent to daily oral cyclophosphamide for inducing remission (88.1% vs. 87.7% at 9 months), required less cumulative cyclophosphamide, and caused fewer cases of leukopenia.

CYCLOPS (Ann Intern Med 2009) supports using pulse rather than daily oral cyclophosphamide for AAV induction, as equivalent efficacy is achieved with reduced cumulative toxicity. This is relevant to minimizing long-term adverse effects of cyclophosphamide (bladder cancer, gonadotoxicity).

**Q:** What did the MAINRITSAN3 trial establish about prolonged rituximab use for AAV maintenance?

**A:** Prolonged rituximab (every 6 months for 18 months) resulted in 96% relapse-free survival vs. 74% in the placebo group at 28 months (absolute difference 22%; HR 7.5, P=0.008). Prolonged rituximab was superior to standard maintenance therapy for preventing AAV relapses.

MAINRITSAN3 (Ann Intern Med 2020) builds on earlier MAINRITSAN trials to support extended rituximab maintenance in AAV. This is particularly relevant for patients with PR3-ANCA and relapsing disease, who are at highest risk of relapse.

**Q:** What was the key finding of the ATLAS trial regarding adalimumab in ankylosing spondylitis (AS)?

**A:** 58.2% of adalimumab-treated AS patients achieved ASAS20 response at week 12 vs. 20.6% placebo (P<0.001). Adalimumab also achieved significantly better ASAS40 and ASAS5/6 responses.

ATLAS (Arthritis Rheum 2006) established the efficacy of anti-TNF therapy (adalimumab) in active AS. The ASAS20 (Assessment in AS International Working Group 20% improvement) is the standard primary outcome measure for AS clinical trials. TNF inhibitors are indicated in AS refractory to NSAIDs.

**Q:** What did the WGET trial demonstrate about etanercept in granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA/Wegener's)?

**A:** Etanercept was NOT effective for remission maintenance in GPA. There were no significant differences between etanercept and placebo for rates of stable low-level disease activity, sustained remission, or time to achieve those outcomes.

WGET (NEJM 2005) is important because it contradicts the use of TNF inhibitors in GPA, unlike their established role in RA and AS. The pathophysiology of AAV differs from RA, and rituximab (anti-CD20) has replaced cyclophosphamide and etanercept for GPA management.

**Q:** What did the febuxostat vs. allopurinol trial (Becker 2005) demonstrate for hyperuricemia and gout?

**A:** Primary endpoint (serum urate <6 mg/dL at last 3 monthly measurements): febuxostat 80 mg achieved 53%, febuxostat 120 mg achieved 62%, vs. allopurinol 300 mg only 21% (P<0.001 for both febuxostat doses vs. allopurinol). Febuxostat was more effective than standard-dose allopurinol at lowering serum urate.

This NEJM 2005 trial established febuxostat as an effective alternative ULT. However, subsequent cardiovascular safety data led to restrictions: febuxostat should not be used in patients with cardiovascular disease. Allopurinol titrated to higher doses can achieve similar serum urate lowering.

**Q:** What is the mechanism of action of secukinumab (Cosentyx®) and in which rheumatologic conditions is it used?

**A:** Secukinumab blocks IL-17A. It is used in ankylosing spondylitis (AS), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and plaque psoriasis. Dose: 150 mg SC monthly.

IL-17A is a key cytokine in the pathogenesis of spondyloarthropathies. Secukinumab represents an important alternative to TNF inhibitors for AS and PsA, particularly in patients who have failed or cannot tolerate anti-TNF therapy. It is part of the newer biologic DMARD armamentarium.

**Q:** What are the adverse effects of cyclophosphamide, and in which rheumatologic conditions is it used?

**A:** Adverse effects: cardiotoxicity, GI symptoms, hemorrhagic cystitis, nephrotoxicity, bone marrow suppression, sterility, bladder cancer. Used in: ANCA-associated vasculitis (remission induction), lupus nephritis (induction), scleroderma-associated ILD.

Cyclophosphamide is an alkylating agent with serious long-term toxicity. Mesna co-administration reduces the risk of hemorrhagic cystitis from the toxic acrolein metabolite. The CYCLOPS trial showed pulse IV is preferred over daily oral to reduce cumulative dose and leukopenia. Adequate hydration is essential.

**Q:** What did the cyclophosphamide vs. placebo scleroderma lung disease trial (Tashkin 2006) demonstrate?

**A:** Oral cyclophosphamide (<2 mg/kg/day) for 1 year produced a mean absolute difference in 12-month adjusted FVC of 2.53% compared to placebo (P<0.03), sustained at 24 months. Cyclophosphamide had significant clinical benefit for scleroderma-related ILD with active alveolitis.

Scleroderma-related ILD is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in systemic sclerosis. This NEJM 2006 trial was landmark in establishing cyclophosphamide as an evidence-based option for symptomatic scleroderma ILD, though the effect size was modest and more recent data support mycophenolate mofetil as an alternative with better tolerability.

**Q:** What are the adverse effects of azathioprine, and what enzyme deficiency predisposes to severe toxicity?

**A:** Adverse effects: skin rash, pancytopenia (especially leukopenia), transaminitis, biliary stasis, vomiting, diarrhea. Thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT) deficiency is a contraindication, as it predisposes to severe myelosuppression due to inability to metabolize the drug.

TPMT genotyping or phenotyping is recommended before starting azathioprine to identify poor metabolizers who are at high risk for life-threatening myelosuppression. Regular CBC and LFT monitoring is required. Azathioprine is used for maintenance in AAV, SLE, inflammatory myopathies, and as a steroid-sparing agent.

**Q:** A 70-year-old woman has chondrocalcinosis on knee x-ray but no symptoms. What treatment is recommended?

**A:** No treatment is required. Asymptomatic chondrocalcinosis does not require pharmacological intervention. Investigate for underlying metabolic causes (hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hemochromatosis, hypophosphatasia).

Per EULAR recommendations, there are no disease-modifying treatments for CPPD crystal arthritis, and no treatment is indicated for asymptomatic chondrocalcinosis. However, any associated metabolic condition should be identified and treated. The clinical relevance lies in not over-treating an incidental finding.

**Q:** What are the common sites of tophi deposition in chronic gout?

**A:** First MTP joint (podagra site), ear helix, olecranon bursae, Achilles tendon insertions. Can also deposit in subcutaneous tissues, synovium, cartilage, and kidney (causing gouty nephropathy and uric acid nephrolithiasis).

Tophi are deposits of monosodium urate crystals surrounded by chronic inflammatory tissue. They appear as firm, white-yellow subcutaneous nodules. On x-ray, tophi cause characteristic punched-out erosions with overhanging edges. Chronic untreated gout can lead to destructive arthropathy.

**Q:** A patient with gout is started on allopurinol. What anti-inflammatory prophylaxis should be co-prescribed, and for how long?

**A:** Low-dose NSAIDs or colchicine should be started concomitantly with urate-lowering therapy (ULT) for 3-6 months to prevent acute gout flares triggered by the mobilization of urate crystals as serum urate drops.

Paradoxically, initiation of ULT can precipitate acute gout flares as serum urate levels fluctuate and crystals dissolve. Anti-inflammatory prophylaxis blunts this risk. The serum urate target is <6 mg/dL (or <5 mg/dL in severe tophaceous gout). ULT should not be initiated or discontinued during an acute flare.

**Q:** What is pegloticase, and when is it indicated in gout management?

**A:** Pegloticase is a PEGylated recombinant uricase (urate oxidase) that converts uric acid to allantoin, which is readily excreted. It is indicated in patients with frequent gout flares or non-resolving subcutaneous tophi who have failed to achieve serum urate <6 mg/dL on maximally tolerated conventional ULT (allopurinol, febuxostat, uricosurics).

Per 2020 ACR gout guidelines, pegloticase is reserved for refractory severe gout. It requires IV infusion every 2 weeks and carries risk of infusion reactions and immunogenicity. Serum urate monitoring during therapy is essential as a rising urate level may precede infusion reactions.

**Q:** What is the ultrasound finding in gout, and what does the CPP crystal aspirate show in pseudogout?

**A:** Gout: double-contour sign on ultrasound (hyperechoic line over the cartilage surface). Pseudogout: CPP crystals are present in only 60% of aspirates, are positively birefringent (blue when parallel to compensator filter), and are rhomboid-shaped.

The double-contour sign in gout represents MSU crystal deposition on the surface of hyaline cartilage. In pseudogout, crystals may be sparse and difficult to find in the aspirate. X-ray showing chondrocalcinosis is present in 75% of CPPD cases and is often the initial diagnostic clue.

**Q:** What are the fibromyalgia epidemiology characteristics and associated conditions?

**A:** M:F = 1:3. Primarily ages 25-45. Prevalence 2-5% in general population. Strongly associated with: psychiatric illness (depression, anxiety), chronic fatigue syndrome, myofascial pain syndrome, IBS, bladder syndrome, migraines, restless leg syndrome, obesity.

The 2012 Canadian Guidelines highlight fibromyalgia as a complex condition with significant psychiatric and somatic comorbidities. These associations support a central sensitization model of chronic pain. Fibromyalgia must be distinguished from inflammatory conditions (normal ESR/CRP, no weakness, normal CK).

**Q:** What is the role of IA corticosteroid injections in osteoarthritis management, and how frequently can they be given?

**A:** IA corticosteroid injections provide short-term (weeks to months) joint-specific pain relief. Infection must be ruled out before injection. There is a theoretical risk of cartilage damage and tendon weakening if used more frequently than every 3 months.

IA corticosteroids are particularly useful for OA flares with significant inflammation. They are not disease-modifying. The 3-month interval guideline is based on the theoretical risk of cumulative cartilage damage from frequent injections. Septic arthritis must always be excluded before injecting any joint.

**Q:** What did the hyaluronan intra-articular injection trial (Ann Rheum Dis 2010) conclude for knee OA?

**A:** IA hyaluronan (sodium hyaluronate) was without clinical effect in patients with moderate-severe knee OA (Lequesne algofunctional index ≥10). No significant differences vs. saline placebo in time to recurrence, LFI, walking pain, paracetamol consumption, or global assessment.

This multicentre RCT (n=337) showed that viscosupplementation with hyaluronan injections does not provide clinically meaningful benefit in moderate-to-severe knee OA. Current guidelines (OARSI, ACR) do not strongly recommend viscosupplementation, particularly for moderate-severe disease.

**Q:** What are the key differences in clinical features between PMR and fibromyalgia regarding pain distribution, investigations, and treatment?

**A:** PMR: pain in shoulder and hip girdle (proximal), markedly elevated ESR/CRP, normal CK, responds dramatically to low-dose prednisone (12.5-25 mg/day). Fibromyalgia: diffuse widespread pain (axial + left/right + upper/lower), normal ESR/CRP, normal TSH required to exclude thyroid disease, treated with exercise and sleep restoration (not steroids).

This distinction is critical: PMR is a steroid-responsive inflammatory condition while fibromyalgia is a central sensitization syndrome with normal inflammatory markers. Misdiagnosing fibromyalgia as PMR leads to unnecessary prolonged corticosteroid exposure with significant adverse effects.

**Q:** What is apremilast (Otezla®) and what is its mechanism of action in rheumatology?

**A:** Apremilast is a PDE4 (phosphodiesterase-4) inhibitor that reduces production of TNF-α and other inflammatory cytokines. It is taken orally (titrated to 30 mg BID). It is used in psoriatic arthritis and plaque psoriasis.

PDE4 inhibition increases intracellular cAMP, reducing inflammatory cytokine production. Apremilast has a favorable safety profile compared to biologics (no immunosuppression risk, no TB reactivation screening required, oral administration) but is generally less efficacious than biologic DMARDs. It is an option for mild-to-moderate PsA.

**Q:** What did the BeSt (Behandel Strategieën) trial demonstrate about therapeutic strategies in early RA?

**A:** Groups 3 (initial combination + tapered high-dose prednisone) and 4 (initial combination + infliximab) showed significantly greater functional improvement (D-HAQ) and less radiographic damage at 3 months and 1 year compared to groups 1 (sequential DMARD monotherapy) and 2 (step-up combination therapy). Earlier aggressive treatment is superior in early RA.

The BeSt study (Arthritis Rheum 2005) demonstrated the 'window of opportunity' concept: treating early RA aggressively with combination therapy (including biologics or high-dose steroids) achieves better outcomes than sequential or step-up approaches. This supports the treat-to-target strategy in current RA guidelines.

**Q:** What is the role of sulfasalazine in reactive arthritis (ReA) and spondylarthropathy?

**A:** Sulfasalazine (SSZ) is used as a DMARD for refractory reactive arthritis with peripheral joint involvement (not axial disease). In spondylarthropathy, the SSZ trial showed 60% improvement vs. 44% placebo; greatest efficacy was in the psoriatic arthritis subgroup. It is used when disease is refractory to NSAIDs.

Sulfasalazine is a traditional DMARD used for peripheral spondyloarthritis but lacks evidence for axial disease in ankylosing spondylitis. In reactive arthritis, it is only used for refractory peripheral disease; axial inflammation requires TNF-α inhibitors when refractory to NSAIDs and DMARDs.
