### Profit and Loss

Trade is about buying and selling. The **cost price (CP)** is what a trader pays to obtain or make an item; the **selling price (SP)** is what the customer pays. Comparing them tells you whether money was made or lost.

**Key definitions and formulas**
- Profit = SP − CP (when SP > CP)
- Loss = CP − SP (when SP < CP)
- Break-even: SP = CP, so profit = 0
- Percentage profit = (profit ÷ CP) × 100
- Percentage loss = (loss ÷ CP) × 100
- Markup = amount added to CP to get SP (markup amount = SP − CP)
- SP = CP × (1 + profit% ÷ 100); SP = CP × (1 − loss% ÷ 100)
- Reverse: CP = SP ÷ (1 + profit% ÷ 100)

In CAPS Grade 9, percentage profit and loss are always calculated **on the cost price** unless a question says otherwise.

**Worked example 1.** A trader buys a bag for R250 and sells it for R325.
Profit = 325 − 250 = R75. % profit = (75 ÷ 250) × 100 = 30%.

**Worked example 2 (reverse).** An item sells for R360 at a 20% profit. Find CP.
SP = CP × 1,20, so CP = 360 ÷ 1,20 = R300. (Do not take 20% of R360 — the percentage is on the cost price.)

**Break-even.** If a tuck shop has fixed costs of R450 and makes R3 profit per sweet, break-even = 450 ÷ 3 = 150 sweets. Below 150 it loses money; above 150 it profits.

**Pitfalls**
- Dividing by the selling price instead of the cost price for percentage profit/loss.
- In reverse-percentage problems, applying the percentage to the selling price instead of the unknown cost price.
- Forgetting that a loss uses a multiplier less than 1, e.g. 0,80 for a 20% loss.


### Discount

A **discount** is a reduction in price. The **marked price (MP)** is the original ticket price; the **sale price** is what you actually pay after the discount.

**Formulas**
- Discount amount = MP × (discount% ÷ 100)
- Sale price = MP − discount = MP × (1 − discount% ÷ 100)
- Percentage discount = (discount amount ÷ MP) × 100
- Reverse: MP = sale price ÷ (1 − discount% ÷ 100)

**Worked example 1.** A jacket marked R800 has 25% off.
Discount = 0,25 × 800 = R200. Sale price = 800 − 200 = R600. (Shortcut: pay 75% → 0,75 × 800 = R600.)

**Worked example 2 (successive discounts).** A coat marked R1 000 gets 20% off, then a further 15% off at the till.
After first: 1 000 × 0,80 = R800. After second: 800 × 0,85 = R680.
Overall: 1 000 × 0,80 × 0,85 = R680. The overall discount is 32%, **not** 35% — you cannot simply add successive percentages, because the second discount is taken on the already-reduced price.

**Reverse example.** After 30% off a TV costs R2 100. Sale price is 70% of MP, so MP = 2 100 ÷ 0,70 = R3 000.

**Pitfalls**
- Adding successive discounts instead of multiplying the multipliers (two 50% discounts give a 75% total discount, never free).
- Calculating the percentage discount on the sale price instead of the marked price.
- In reverse problems, adding the percentage back onto the sale price instead of dividing by (1 − discount%).


### VAT (Value Added Tax)

**VAT** is a tax added to most goods and services. The South African VAT rate is **15%**. A **VAT-exclusive** price is the price before VAT; a **VAT-inclusive** price is the price after VAT (what you actually pay).

**Formulas (15%)**
- Exclusive → inclusive: inclusive = exclusive × 1,15
- Inclusive → exclusive: exclusive = inclusive ÷ 1,15
- VAT from exclusive: VAT = exclusive × 0,15
- VAT from inclusive: VAT = inclusive × (15 ÷ 115) = inclusive − exclusive

The inclusive price is 115% of the exclusive price (100% + 15%). That is why, to find the VAT hidden inside an inclusive price, you use the fraction 15/115 (≈ 13,04% of the inclusive amount), not 15/100.

**Worked example 1.** A laptop costs R8 000 before VAT.
VAT = 0,15 × 8 000 = R1 200. Total = 8 000 × 1,15 = R9 200.

**Worked example 2.** A tag shows R575 including VAT. Find the VAT.
Exclusive = 575 ÷ 1,15 = R500. VAT = 575 − 500 = R75. (Directly: 575 × 15/115 = R75.)

**Zero-rated items.** Some basic foods (brown bread, maize meal, rice, milk, fruit, vegetables) are zero-rated: 0% VAT, so inclusive = exclusive.

**Pitfalls**
- To remove VAT, dividing by 1,15 is correct; subtracting 15% of the inclusive price is wrong (it removes too much, because the 15% was added to the smaller exclusive amount).
- Using 15/100 instead of 15/115 when extracting VAT from an inclusive price.


### Simple Interest

**Simple interest** is calculated on the original principal only, the same amount every year. It applies to many short-term loans and investments.

**Formulas**
- I = P × r × t (also written I = P·i·n)
- A = P + I = P(1 + i·n)
- Solve for P: P = I ÷ (r·t)
- Solve for r: r = I ÷ (P·t)
- Solve for t: t = I ÷ (P·r)

Here P = principal, r or i = annual rate as a **decimal**, t or n = time in **years**, I = interest, A = final amount. Convert a rate by dividing by 100 (7,5% = 0,075) and convert months to years by dividing by 12 (18 months = 1,5 years).

**Worked example 1.** R5 000 at 8% simple interest for 3 years.
I = 5 000 × 0,08 × 3 = R1 200. A = 5 000 + 1 200 = R6 200. (Check: 5 000 × 1,24 = R6 200.)

**Worked example 2 (solve for r).** R4 000 earns R720 over 4 years.
r = 720 ÷ (4 000 × 4) = 720 ÷ 16 000 = 0,045 = 4,5% per year.

For a **loan**, the interest is what you pay extra; for an **investment**, it is what you earn — the formula is identical.

**Pitfalls**
- Using the rate as a whole number (8 instead of 0,08) inflates interest 100×.
- Leaving time in months instead of converting to years.
- Forgetting to convert the rate decimal back to a percentage in the final answer when solving for r.


### Compound Interest

With **compound interest** you earn interest on the interest already added, so the balance grows faster each period. This is the basis of most savings and inflation calculations.

**Formulas**
- A = P(1 + i)ⁿ (growth)
- A = P(1 − i)ⁿ (depreciation / decrease)
- Interest earned = A − P

P = principal, i = rate per period as a decimal, n = number of periods, A = final amount.

**Worked example 1.** R5 000 at 8% compound for 3 years.
A = 5 000 × (1,08)³. (1,08)³ = 1,259712, so A = 5 000 × 1,259712 = R6 298,56. Interest = R1 298,56.

**Compare with simple interest** for the same figures: simple gives A = R6 200, so compound earns R98,56 more. Compound always equals simple after one year and pulls ahead thereafter; the gap widens with time.

**Year-by-year intuition.** R1 000 at 10%: year 1 adds R100 → R1 100; year 2 adds 10% of R1 100 = R110 → R1 210. The formula confirms 1 000 × (1,10)² = R1 210. The extra R10 in year 2 is interest on interest.

**Depreciation example.** A car worth R200 000 losing 12% per year for 2 years: A = 200 000 × (0,88)² = 200 000 × 0,7744 = R154 880.

**Pitfalls**
- Treating compound like simple: writing 5 000 × 1,08 × 3 instead of 5 000 × (1,08)³. Raise (1 + i) to the power n; multiply it by itself n times, not by n.
- Rounding intermediate powers too early — keep many decimals and round only the final Rand answer to cents.


### Hire Purchase

**Hire purchase (HP)** is buying on credit: you pay a **deposit** now and then equal **monthly instalments** over a fixed time. Interest (usually simple interest) is charged on the amount borrowed. HP almost always costs more than paying cash.

**Formulas**
- Balance financed = cash price − deposit
- Interest = balance × r × t (simple interest)
- Amount to repay = balance + interest
- Monthly instalment = amount to repay ÷ number of months
- Total HP cost = deposit + (instalment × number of months)
- Interest charged = total HP cost − cash price

**Worked example.** Cash price R8 000, deposit 10%, simple interest 18% per year on the balance over 2 years.
Deposit = 0,10 × 8 000 = R800. Balance = 8 000 − 800 = R7 200.
Interest = 7 200 × 0,18 × 2 = R2 592. Repay = 7 200 + 2 592 = R9 792.
Months = 24, so instalment = 9 792 ÷ 24 = R408.
Total HP cost = 800 + 9 792 = R10 592, which is R2 592 (the interest) more than the cash price.

**Why interest is on the balance.** You pay the deposit immediately, so you only borrow the balance. Interest is the cost of borrowing, so it applies to that balance, not the full cash price.

**Pitfalls**
- Forgetting to add the deposit when finding the total HP cost.
- Charging interest on the full cash price instead of on the balance after the deposit.
- Not converting the time period to years for the simple-interest step, or the period to months for the instalment count.


### Exchange Rates

An **exchange rate** tells you how much of one currency you get for another. Written as R18,50 = $1, it means one US dollar costs R18,50.

**Rules**
- Foreign currency → Rands: **multiply** by the R/foreign rate.
- Rands → foreign currency: **divide** by the R/foreign rate.

Always match the rate to the currency: use the R/$ rate for dollars, R/£ for pounds, R/€ for euros.

**Worked example 1.** Using R18,50 = $1, convert $250 to rands and R7 400 to dollars.
$250 → 250 × 18,50 = R4 625.
R7 400 → 7 400 ÷ 18,50 = $400.

**Worked example 2 (comparing prices).** A jacket is $80 in the USA and R1 600 in SA, with R18,50 = $1.
Convert to the same currency: $80 × 18,50 = R1 480. Since R1 480 < R1 600, it is cheaper in the USA by R120.

**Multiple currencies.** With R23 = £1 and R20 = €1: £150 → 150 × 23 = R3 450; R5 000 → 5 000 ÷ 20 = €250.

**Pitfalls**
- Multiplying when you should divide (or vice versa). Sanity check: because R1 is worth less than $1, converting a rand amount to dollars must give a smaller number.
- Using the wrong currency's rate.
- Comparing prices in different currencies without converting to one common currency first.


### Budgets, Income and Personal Finance

A **budget** is a plan of expected income and expenditure, usually per month. **Income** is money coming in (salary, wages, commission); **expenditure** is money going out (rent, food, transport, savings). The difference is the balance.

**Formulas**
- Balance = income − expenditure (surplus if positive, deficit if negative)
- Commission = sales × (rate ÷ 100); total earnings = basic salary + commission
- New price after inflation = old price × (1 + i); over n years = P(1 + i)ⁿ
- Closing balance = opening balance + deposits − withdrawals
- Business profit = income − expenses (loss if expenses exceed income)

**Worked example 1 (budget).** Thabo earns R12 000. Expenses: rent R4 500, food R3 000, transport R1 800, airtime R500, savings R1 000.
Total expenditure = R10 800. Balance = 12 000 − 10 800 = R1 200 surplus.

**Worked example 2 (commission).** Basic salary R6 000 plus 5% commission on sales above R20 000; sales are R50 000.
Commission = 5% of (50 000 − 20 000) = 0,05 × 30 000 = R1 500. Total = 6 000 + 1 500 = R7 500.

**Inflation.** Inflation is the general rise in prices, given as a yearly % increase, and it compounds. A R800 basket at 6% over 3 years = 800 × (1,06)³ = R952,81.

**Loans vs rentals.** A loan is borrowed money you repay (often with interest) and you keep the item; a rental is a regular payment to use something you do not own. Both are expenses in a budget.

**Statements.** A bank statement lists deposits (credits) and withdrawals (debits); closing balance = opening + deposits − withdrawals.

**Pitfalls**
- Treating savings as income rather than as money set aside (an expense), which creates a false surplus.
- Applying commission to all sales when it is only on sales above a threshold.
- Treating inflation as simple rather than compound growth over several years.
