# PublicHealth

## Screening Guidelines & Preventive Care

**Q:** What is the Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (CTFPHC) recommendation for colorectal cancer screening?

**A:** Fecal immunochemical test (FIT) every 2 years for adults aged 50-74; flexible sigmoidoscopy every 10 years as alternative; colonoscopy for average-risk as screening (not endorsed by CTFPHC but provincial guidelines vary)

CTFPHC CRC screening guidelines: FIT is non-invasive, cost-effective, and achieves high adherence. Positive FIT requires follow-up colonoscopy. High-risk individuals (IBD, family history, hereditary syndrome) require direct colonoscopy as per CAG/CCA guidelines.

**Q:** What is the CTFPHC recommendation for breast cancer screening by age group?

**A:** Women 50-74: routine screening mammography every 2-3 years (strong recommendation); Women 40-49: recommend against routine screening (moderate recommendation — number needed to screen vs. harms); Women 75+: insufficient evidence

CTFPHC 2018 breast cancer guidelines: benefits of mammography (mortality reduction) must be weighed against harms (false positives, overdiagnosis, unnecessary biopsies). High-risk women (BRCA, family history): annual MRI + mammogram starting at 30-40 per Cancer Care Ontario/BC Cancer.

**Q:** What is the recommended cervical cancer screening strategy in Canada?

**A:** Pap test (liquid-based cytology) every 3 years for women 21-69; HPV primary testing every 5 years increasingly adopted provincially — stop after age 69 with adequate prior screening or after total hysterectomy for benign indication

CTFPHC and SOGC cervical cancer screening guidelines: HPV primary testing has higher sensitivity than cytology. Several provinces are transitioning to HPV testing with cytology reflex. Women with abnormal results require colposcopy per provincial algorithms. Pap testing begins at age 21 (not at first sexual activity as previously).

**Q:** What are the Canadian guidelines for lung cancer screening?

**A:** Low-dose CT (LDCT) screening annually for high-risk individuals: age 50-74 years, smoking history ≥20 pack-years, current smoker OR quit within 15 years — Cancer Care Ontario/BC Cancer recommendations (CTFPHC recommends for 50-74 with ≥20 pack-years)

NLST and NELSON trials showed LDCT reduces lung cancer mortality by 20-25% in high-risk populations. Harms: false positives (20-25% of screens), unnecessary procedures, radiation exposure. Smoking cessation counseling must accompany lung cancer screening programs. Pan-Canadian LDCT implementation is ongoing.

**Q:** What is the CTFPHC recommendation for prostate cancer screening with PSA?

**A:** CTFPHC recommends AGAINST routine PSA screening for average-risk men due to overdiagnosis and treatment harms — individualized decision-making for men 55-69 after full informed discussion of benefits and harms

PSA screening: reduces prostate cancer mortality by ~20% but leads to significant overdiagnosis and overtreatment (incontinence, sexual dysfunction from treatment of cancers that may never cause symptoms). CTFPHC weak recommendation against; Cancer Care Ontario: individualized decision-making for 50-70 years.

**Q:** What is the recommended screening approach for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) in Canada?

**A:** One-time ultrasound screening for men aged 65-80 years who have ever smoked (≥100 cigarettes lifetime) — CTFPHC recommendation; AAA: ≥3 cm; repair if ≥5.5 cm or rapid growth

CTFPHC AAA screening: identifies men with undetected AAA before rupture. Annual surveillance for AAA 3-4.4 cm; q6 months for 4.5-5.4 cm. Endovascular repair (EVAR) or open repair for ≥5.5 cm. Mortality from ruptured AAA is 80-90%. No benefit shown in women or non-smokers.

**Q:** What is universal newborn hearing screening and when is it done?

**A:** Otoacoustic emissions (OAE) or automated ABR (AABR) within 24-48 hours of birth; refer for diagnostic audiology if fail — target to identify and treat by 6 months

CASLPA/CPS guidelines: congenital hearing loss occurs in 1-3/1000 live births; early identification (before 6 months) and intervention significantly improves language development. Universal newborn hearing screening (UNHS) is implemented in all Canadian provinces. CI (cochlear implant) eligibility assessed if profound bilateral SNHL.

**Q:** What is the recommendation for vitamin D supplementation in Canadian adults?

**A:** Health Canada recommends 600 IU/day for adults 19-70; 800 IU/day for adults >70; Osteoporosis Canada recommends 800-2000 IU/day for adults at risk for osteoporosis — routine serum 25-OH vitamin D testing NOT recommended for the general population

Routine testing is not recommended (CTFPHC) due to imprecise lab tests and lack of evidence that supplementation based on testing improves outcomes. High-risk groups (institutionalized, dark skin, veiled women, malabsorption): supplement without testing. Northern latitude communities have very high rates of deficiency.

**Q:** What are the Diabetes Canada criteria for screening for type 2 diabetes?

**A:** Screen all adults ≥40 years every 3 years with fasting plasma glucose or HbA1c; screen earlier if: BMI ≥25 + ≥1 risk factor (family history, high-risk ethnicity, HTN, dyslipidemia, prior GDM, CVD, prediabetes, polycystic ovarian syndrome)

CANRISK questionnaire can identify high-risk individuals for targeted screening. High-risk ethnicities (South Asian, Black, East Asian, Indigenous, Hispanic, Arab) have higher T2DM prevalence at lower BMI. Early detection allows lifestyle intervention and pharmacotherapy to prevent complications.

**Q:** What is the recommended approach to atrial fibrillation screening?

**A:** CTFPHC (2020): recommends against routine ECG screening for AF in adults ≥65 without symptoms — pulse assessment by clinician is still standard of practice; wearable devices increasingly identifying paroxysmal AF

CCS AF guidelines: opportunistic screening (pulse assessment, then ECG if irregular) at routine visits is a pragmatic approach. LOOP trial: insertable cardiac monitoring doubled AF detection in stroke survivors. The benefit of AF screening on stroke prevention outcomes is under investigation.

**Q:** What are the hypertension screening recommendations in Canada?

**A:** Blood pressure measurement at all appropriate clinical encounters for adults ≥18 years; if elevated, follow Hypertension Canada protocol for confirmation — no specific screening interval recommended as hypertension can develop at any time

Hypertension Canada guidelines: BP should be measured at every primary care visit. Opportunistic screening has been highly effective in Canada. AOBP (automated office BP) is the standard measurement method. Electronic alerts in EMR prompt BP measurement and follow-up.

**Q:** What are the indications for chlamydia and gonorrhea screening in Canada?

**A:** Annual screening for: all sexually active women ≤25 years, older women with risk factors (new/multiple partners), all MSM, and individuals with prior STI — PHAC STI guidelines

PHAC recommends screening as part of sexual health history at every clinical encounter for at-risk individuals. Most chlamydia infections are asymptomatic. Universal antenatal chlamydia screening at first prenatal visit. 3-site (pharyngeal, rectal, urogenital) testing for MSM.

**Q:** What is osteoporosis screening per Osteoporosis Canada guidelines?

**A:** DXA for: all women ≥65 and men ≥70; postmenopausal women and men 50-64 with risk factors (prior fragility fracture, steroid use, secondary causes of osteoporosis, FRAX risk ≥10% for major osteoporotic fracture)

Osteoporosis Canada guidelines 2023: FRAX calculation to identify high-risk individuals before DXA. Bone turnover markers and vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) on DXA. Calcium and vitamin D supplementation for all patients at risk. Targeted screening is more cost-effective than universal screening.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC recommendations regarding alcohol and substance use screening?

**A:** Brief alcohol screening (AUDIT-C) in primary care; SBIRT (Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment) for adults with harmful/hazardous alcohol or drug use — CTFPHC recommends screening adults for unhealthy alcohol use

CTFPHC 2018 alcohol screening: 5-minute brief intervention after positive AUDIT screen can reduce alcohol consumption by 15-25%. Motivational interviewing approach. Harm reduction approach for other substance use. Opioid risk tool for opioid prescribing.

**Q:** What is the recommended schedule for Pap testing vs. HPV primary testing?

**A:** Pap test: every 3 years (age 21-69); HPV primary testing: every 5 years (being phased in provincially); HPV+ with negative cytology: re-test in 12 months; HPV+ with abnormal cytology: colposcopy

Ontario and BC are implementing HPV primary testing as standard. HPV16/18 positive on primary test → immediate colposcopy. Other high-risk HPV types positive with abnormal cytology → colposcopy. Advantages of HPV testing: higher sensitivity (90%), longer intervals, earlier detection of CIN.

**Q:** What cancer screenings does CTFPHC recommend?

**A:** Breast: mammography q2-3y ages 50-74. Cervical: Pap q3y ages 25-69. CRC: FIT q2y ages 50-74. Lung: low-dose CT annually for high-risk smokers 55-74

CTFPHC does NOT recommend routine PSA screening (informed discussion). Cervical: some provinces transitioning to HPV primary. No screening recommended for ovarian, pancreatic, or thyroid.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC recommendations for breast cancer screening?

**A:** Age 50-74: mammography q2-3 years. Age 40-49: do NOT routinely screen. No CBE or BSE for screening

CTFPHC 2018 differs from USPSTF (now recommends from 40). Many provinces still offer from 40. High-risk (BRCA, chest radiation) follow different protocol with MRI. Informed decision-making emphasized.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC recommendations for cervical cancer screening?

**A:** Age 25-69: Pap q3 years. Do not screen <25 or >69 (if adequate prior screening). HPV co-testing not currently recommended by CTFPHC

CTFPHC 2013. Starts at 25 (vs US at 21). BC and Ontario transitioning to HPV-primary q5 years. Stop at 69 if 3 consecutive negatives in prior 10 years. Abnormal Pap follow-up per colposcopy guidelines.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC recommendations for colorectal cancer screening?

**A:** Age 50-74: FIT q2 years or flexible sigmoidoscopy q10 years. Colonoscopy NOT recommended as primary screen for average risk

FIT replaced guaiac FOBT (better sensitivity, no dietary restrictions). Positive FIT requires diagnostic colonoscopy. High-risk (FHx, IBD, polyposis) screened earlier with colonoscopy. CTFPHC 2016.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC recommendations for lung cancer screening?

**A:** Age 55-74 with 30+ pack-year history (current or quit <15y): annual low-dose CT. Do not screen never-smokers or <30 pack-years

NLST/NELSON trials: 20% mortality reduction with LDCT. Provincial programs in ON, BC, AB. ~25% false positive rate. Always pair with smoking cessation counseling.

**Q:** What is the CTFPHC position on PSA screening for prostate cancer?

**A:** Recommend AGAINST routine PSA screening for all ages (2014). Shared decision-making if patient requests. CUA supports discussion for men 50-70

CTFPHC: harms outweigh benefits (overdiagnosis, overtreatment, biopsy complications). CUA differs. MRI-targeted biopsy has improved accuracy since guideline. High-risk: Black men, FHx - discuss from age 45.

**Q:** Define sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV with their formulas.

**A:** Sn=TP/(TP+FN). Sp=TN/(TN+FP). PPV=TP/(TP+FP). NPV=TN/(TN+FN). Sn/Sp independent of prevalence; PPV/NPV vary with prevalence

SnNOut: sensitive test negative rules OUT. SpPIn: specific test positive rules IN. Screening tests prioritize sensitivity. Confirmatory tests prioritize specificity. PPV increases with prevalence.

**Q:** How are NNT and NNS calculated and interpreted?

**A:** NNT = 1/ARR. NNS = 1/(prevalence x sensitivity x treatment efficacy). Lower = more effective. NNS for mammography 50-69: ~720 over 11 years

NNT communicates treatment benefit magnitude. NNS helps evaluate screening program utility. Both useful for shared decision-making with patients. ARR = absolute risk reduction = control event rate - experimental event rate.

**Q:** What are the Wilson-Jungner criteria for evaluating screening programs?

**A:** 10 criteria: important problem, accepted treatment, facilities available, recognizable latent stage, suitable test, acceptable to population, natural history understood, agreed treatment policy, cost-effective, ongoing process

1968 WHO criteria remain gold standard. Modern additions: program must reduce mortality (not just detect), benefits must outweigh harms (overdiagnosis, false positives). CTFPHC applies these principles to all recommendations.

**Q:** What types of bias affect screening program evaluation?

**A:** Lead-time: earlier detection without survival change. Length-time: detecting slow-growing tumors preferentially. Selection: healthy volunteers screened. Overdiagnosis: detecting non-harmful disease

Lead-time bias makes screened patients APPEAR to live longer. Mortality reduction (not survival) is the correct outcome. Overdiagnosis particularly relevant in prostate and thyroid screening.

**Q:** What are the Canadian diabetes screening recommendations?

**A:** Screen q3-5y with FPG or A1c from age 40, earlier if high-risk (BMI>25, Indigenous, South Asian, African descent, GDM, HTN). Use CANRISK questionnaire

Diabetes Canada 2018 CPG. Diagnostic: FPG>=7.0, 2h-OGTT>=11.1, A1c>=6.5%, random>=11.1+symptoms. Need 2 abnormal tests if asymptomatic. CANRISK is Canadian-specific risk calculator.

**Q:** What are the CTFPHC recommendations for AAA screening?

**A:** One-time abdominal US for men 65-80 who have ever smoked. Do not screen women or never-smokers

AAA screening reduces AAA mortality ~50%. AAA >=3.0cm. Management by size: <4cm q3y surveillance, 4-4.4 annually, 4.5-5.4 q6mo, >=5.5cm surgical repair. Emergency repair for symptomatic/ruptured at any size.

**Q:** What is the CTFPHC position on depression screening in primary care?

**A:** Recommend AGAINST routine screening for depression in adults (2013). Be alert to symptoms during encounters (case-finding approach)

Controversial. Insufficient evidence screening improves outcomes without integrated treatment program. PHQ-2 then PHQ-9 commonly used tools. Be vigilant in high-risk: chronic illness, postpartum, chronic pain.

**Q:** What prenatal screening tests are offered in Canada by trimester?

**A:** 1st trimester (11-14wk): NT US + PAPP-A + free beta-hCG. 2nd (15-20wk): quad screen. NIPT (cell-free DNA) increasingly offered as primary screen

NIPT >99% sensitivity for trisomy 21, positive still needs amnio/CVS confirmation. Enhanced FTS detects ~85% T21. Coverage varies by province. NIPT cost-covered in most provinces for high-risk or as contingent screen.

**Q:** What newborn screening tests are performed across Canada?

**A:** Blood spot (heel prick 24-48h): metabolic/endocrine/hemoglobin disorders (PKU, hypothyroidism, CF, sickle cell). Universal hearing screen (OAE/ABR). CCHD screen (pulse ox)

Number of conditions varies by province (Ontario ~30). Screening not diagnostic - positives need confirmatory testing. Too early (<24h) increases false positives for PKU. Critical to treat congenital hypothyroidism within 2 weeks.

**Q:** How are ROC curves and likelihood ratios used to evaluate diagnostic tests?

**A:** ROC: AUC 1.0=perfect, 0.5=useless. LR+ = Sn/(1-Sp); >10 is strong. LR- = (1-Sn)/Sp; <0.1 is strong. LRs are prevalence-independent

LRs applied to pre-test probability via Fagan nomogram give post-test probability. ROC curves compare tests and select optimal cut-offs. AUC >0.9 excellent, 0.7-0.9 good, 0.5-0.7 poor.

**Q:** What is the recommended shingles vaccination in Canada and who should receive it?

**A:** Shingrix® (recombinant zoster vaccine) is preferred for adults ≥50 years. Zostavax® (live zoster vaccine) can also be used in adults ≥60 years.

Shingrix® is preferred over Zostavax® due to higher efficacy. Oral antivirals (famciclovir, valacyclovir, or acyclovir) for 7 days must be initiated within 72 hours of rash onset to be of benefit.

**Q:** Who should be screened for dyslipidemia according to Canadian guidelines?

**A:** Screen men and women ≥40 years or post-menopausal women. Screen at any age if: DM, current smoking or COPD, HTN (sBP >140 or dBP >90), obesity (BMI ≥30), family history of premature CVD or dyslipidemia, clinical signs of hyperlipidemia (xanthelasma, xanthoma, arcus cornealis), AAA, atherosclerosis, inflammatory disease (RA, SLE, psoriasis, IBD), HIV on HAART, CKD (eGFR <60), erectile dysfunction, high-risk ethnicity (South Asian, Indigenous).

Dyslipidemia screening components include history and physical, lipid panel (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG), non-HDL cholesterol, BG, eGFR. Optional: urine ACR, ApoB. ApoB reflects total atherogenic particle count. Lp(a) should be measured once in a lifetime.

**Q:** What is the Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (CTFPHC) recommendation for breast cancer screening with mammography?

**A:** Ages 50-74: screening mammography every 2-3 years (conditional recommendation; very low certainty evidence). Ages 40-49: recommend NOT screening with mammography (conditional recommendation; low certainty evidence). Do NOT recommend clinical breast exams, breast self-examination, MRI, tomosynthesis, or U/S in average-risk women.

The 2018 CTFPHC recommendations are more conservative than some US guidelines and reflect a harm-benefit analysis: screening in younger women (40-49) carries higher false positive rates and unnecessary biopsies relative to benefit. High-risk women (e.g., BRCA mutation) should follow separate protocols with annual mammography + MRI starting at age 30-60.

**Q:** What are the Canadian lung cancer screening guidelines (CTFPHC 2016)?

**A:** Annual LDCT (low-dose CT) for adults ages 55-74 with ≥30 pack-year smoking history who currently smoke or quit <15 years ago, for up to 3 consecutive screenings. Must be done in settings with expertise in early diagnosis/treatment. Chest X-ray (with or without sputum cytology) should NOT be used for lung cancer screening. All other adults should NOT be screened with LDCT.

Lung cancer LDCT screening is recommended for high-risk smokers and former smokers because the benefit of early detection outweighs the risks of radiation and false positives. The Ontario Lung Screening Program uses slightly different criteria: ages 55-74 who have smoked every day for at least 20 years.

**Q:** What are the periodic health examination (PHE) recommendations for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) screening?

**A:** One-time screening ultrasound for abdominal aortic aneurysm in men aged 65-80 years (Grade B recommendation).

AAA screening with ultrasound in older men is recommended because AAA is predominantly a disease of older males with significant mortality if ruptured, and ultrasound is a safe, effective screening tool. Women and younger men are not routinely screened as the prevalence is lower and benefit does not outweigh harm.

**Q:** What did the HOPE-3 trial demonstrate about statin use in intermediate-risk patients without CV disease?

**A:** HOPE-3 (2016): Rosuvastatin 10 mg/day in 12,705 intermediate-risk patients (no existing CV disease) reduced LDL by 26.5% and reduced the incidence of vascular events (3.7% vs. 4.8% placebo). Conclusion: Statins benefit intermediate-risk populations without established CV disease.

HOPE-3 supports the use of statins for primary prevention in intermediate-risk populations. This aligns with Canadian Cardiovascular Society lipid management guidelines, which recommend risk-stratified statin therapy.

**Q:** A 28-year-old woman is found to have LSIL on Pap smear with positive HPV. What is the appropriate next step in management per Canadian guidelines?

**A:** For women ≥25 years with LSIL and HPV positive: follow-up colposcopy with cytology and HPV test (if ≥30 years) in 1 year. For women <25 years with cytology LSIL/ASCUS/normal: annual Paps by primary care provider × 3 years, followed by return to normal screening.

Most LSIL (CIN I) lesions regress spontaneously, especially in younger women. The risk-based approach for women ≥25 with HPV-positive LSIL warrants colposcopy, while HPV-negative LSIL returns to routine 3-year screening. The Ontario Cervical Screening Program (January 2022) guidelines form the basis for Canadian cervical screening recommendations.

## Epidemiology & Biostatistics

**Q:** What is the difference between incidence and prevalence?

**A:** Incidence: rate of NEW cases in a defined population over a time period; Prevalence: proportion of EXISTING cases (new + old) in a population at a given time — Point prevalence: at specific moment; Period prevalence: over defined time period

Prevalence = Incidence × Duration (of disease). High incidence with short duration (recovery or death) → low prevalence (influenza). Low incidence with long duration (chronic disease) → higher prevalence than incidence (HIV/AIDS). Changing prevalence can reflect changing incidence, mortality, or disease duration.

**Q:** What is sensitivity vs. specificity of a screening test?

**A:** Sensitivity: proportion of TRUE POSITIVES correctly identified (TP/[TP+FN]); Specificity: proportion of TRUE NEGATIVES correctly identified (TN/[TN+FP]) — high sensitivity = few false negatives; high specificity = few false positives

High sensitivity tests are best for screening (rule out disease — negative test rules disease out: SnNOUT). High specificity tests are best for confirmation (rule in disease — positive test rules disease in: SpPIN). Cancer screening tests prioritize sensitivity; confirmatory tests prioritize specificity.

**Q:** What is positive predictive value (PPV) and how does disease prevalence affect it?

**A:** PPV = TP/(TP+FP) — probability of having disease given a positive test; NPV = TN/(TN+FN); PPV DECREASES when disease prevalence is low (more false positives relative to true positives)

PPV and NPV are affected by disease prevalence (unlike sensitivity and specificity which are fixed characteristics of the test). Same test in a low-prevalence population will have lower PPV than in a high-prevalence population. Crucial when interpreting screening results in general vs. high-risk populations.

**Q:** What is relative risk (RR) and how is it interpreted?

**A:** RR = Risk in exposed / Risk in unexposed; RR=1: no association; RR>1: increased risk; RR<1: decreased risk (protective); used for COHORT studies and RCTs

RR is used for prospective studies. In case-control studies, use Odds Ratio (OR) because incidence cannot be calculated. OR approximates RR when disease is rare (<10%). Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = risk in control − risk in treatment; NNT = 1/ARR.

**Q:** What is the number needed to treat (NNT) and how is it calculated?

**A:** NNT = 1 / ARR (absolute risk reduction); ARR = control event rate − treatment event rate — NNT is the number of patients who need to be treated for one patient to benefit

Low NNT = more effective treatment. NNT must always be interpreted with time frame (NNT at 1 year vs. 5 years). NNH (Number Needed to Harm) = 1/ARI (absolute risk increase). Clinicians should understand NNT to discuss treatment benefits meaningfully with patients.

**Q:** What is selection bias and provide an example?

**A:** Selection bias: systematic error in how study participants are selected, resulting in non-representative sample; Example: Berkson bias (hospitalized patients more likely to have multiple diseases, making two conditions appear associated when they are not in the general population)

Selection bias leads to study results that cannot be generalized. Healthy worker bias: employed workers are healthier than general population, making occupational studies underestimate mortality risk. Volunteer bias: participants who volunteer may have different characteristics than those who decline.

**Q:** What is confounding and how can it be controlled?

**A:** Confounding: third variable is associated with both the exposure and outcome, causing a spurious or biased association; Control methods: randomization (RCTs), restriction, matching, stratification, multivariable analysis (regression)

Example: alcohol use appears protective against MI — but smokers drink more alcohol. Smoking is a confounder. Residual confounding: occurs when known confounders are not fully adjusted for. Unmeasured confounders are a limitation of observational studies.

**Q:** What is the attributable risk (AR) and population attributable risk (PAR)?

**A:** AR = Incidence in exposed − Incidence in unexposed (risk in exposed attributable to the exposure); PAR = Incidence in total population − Incidence in unexposed (proportion of disease that would be prevented if exposure eliminated from population)

PAR is used in public health to prioritize interventions. If smoking causes 85% of lung cancers (PAR = 85%), eliminating smoking would prevent 85% of lung cancers. PAR depends on both the relative risk and the prevalence of exposure in the population.

**Q:** What is a systematic review vs. meta-analysis?

**A:** Systematic review: comprehensive, reproducible literature search and synthesis of evidence on a specific question; Meta-analysis: statistical method to pool results from multiple studies to calculate a summary effect size (gives more power and precision)

Levels of evidence: meta-analysis/systematic reviews > RCTs > cohort studies > case-control > case series. GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework used by Canadian guidelines to rate evidence quality and recommendation strength.

**Q:** What is a randomized controlled trial (RCT) and why is it the gold standard?

**A:** RCT: participants randomly assigned to intervention vs. control — randomization eliminates selection bias and balances known and unknown confounders between groups, allowing causal inference

RCT limitations: expensive, time-consuming, ethical constraints, external validity (generalizability). Double-blind RCT (neither participant nor assessor knows allocation) minimizes performance and detection bias. Intention-to-treat analysis preserves randomization benefits. CONSORT checklist for reporting RCTs.

**Q:** What is the difference between a type I error and a type II error?

**A:** Type I error (alpha error): false positive — rejecting null hypothesis when it is true; probability = p-value (typically <0.05); Type II error (beta error): false negative — failing to reject null hypothesis when it is false; probability = β (1-β = power, typically 80-90%)

Type I error consequences: accepting a treatment that is actually ineffective. Type II error consequences: missing a real treatment effect. Larger sample size reduces both types of errors. Power (1-β) reflects ability to detect a true effect if it exists.

**Q:** What is the 95% confidence interval and what does it mean if it does not cross 1.0?

**A:** 95% CI: range of values within which the true population parameter lies with 95% certainty; if 95% CI for RR or OR does NOT include 1.0 → statistically significant at p<0.05

Narrow CI = more precise estimate (larger sample size). Wide CI = less precise (small sample). If CI crosses 1.0 for RR: result is NOT statistically significant. CI is more informative than p-value alone because it provides information about effect size and precision.

**Q:** What is lead time bias in screening studies?

**A:** Lead time bias: screening appears to prolong survival time because it detects disease earlier (advancing the apparent start of disease), but actual lifespan is not extended — survival appears longer without actually being longer

Screening survival studies must use disease-specific mortality rather than 5-year survival to avoid lead time bias. Length bias: screening is more likely to detect slowly-growing cancers (more time to screen) vs. aggressive fast-growing cancers, making screened cohort appear to do better.

**Q:** What is the basic reproduction number (R0) and its significance in infectious disease?

**A:** R0: average number of secondary cases generated by one infectious case in a fully susceptible population; R0 >1 = epidemic can spread; R0 <1 = outbreak dies out; herd immunity threshold = 1 - (1/R0) × 100%

COVID-19 original strain: R0 ~2-3; Omicron: R0 ~8-15; Measles: R0 12-18 (one of highest known). Herd immunity for measles: (1-1/15) × 100% = 93%. Vaccination coverage must exceed herd immunity threshold to prevent outbreaks. R0 changes with interventions (R effective, Rt).

**Q:** What is the Cochrane Collaboration and how do Canadian physicians use it?

**A:** Cochrane Collaboration: independent network producing systematic reviews and meta-analyses of healthcare interventions; Cochrane Library contains >8000 systematic reviews; used by clinicians to find highest-quality evidence to inform clinical decisions

Canadian clinical practice guidelines frequently cite Cochrane reviews as the primary evidence source. Cochrane reviews use GRADE to rate evidence quality. Limitations of meta-analysis: garbage in, garbage out — pooling poor-quality studies does not improve evidence quality. Publication bias can skew results.

**Q:** How do you calculate NNT from absolute risk reduction?

**A:** NNT = 1/ARR. ARR = CER - EER (control event rate minus experimental event rate)

NNT of 10 means treating 10 patients to prevent 1 event. NNH = 1/ARI. Relative risk reduction = ARR/CER. RRR can be misleading without baseline risk context.

**Q:** What is sensitivity vs specificity?

**A:** Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN) = ability to detect disease (SnNout: sensitive test rules OUT). Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) = ability to detect non-disease (SpPin: specific test rules IN)

PPV depends on prevalence. High prevalence increases PPV. Likelihood ratios: LR+ = sens/(1-spec), LR- = (1-sens)/spec. ROC curve plots sensitivity vs 1-specificity.

**Q:** What is the difference between incidence and prevalence?

**A:** Incidence: new cases/population/time (rate). Prevalence: total cases/population at a point (proportion). Prevalence ~ incidence x duration

Incidence = risk (etiologic studies). Prevalence = burden (health services planning). Chronic diseases: high prevalence relative to incidence. High fatality: low prevalence relative to incidence.

**Q:** Rank epidemiological study designs by evidence strength.

**A:** 1) SR/meta-analysis 2) RCT 3) Cohort 4) Case-control 5) Cross-sectional 6) Case series 7) Expert opinion. Cohort measures RR; case-control measures OR

RCTs strongest for interventions but ethical limitations. Cohort: prospective, measures incidence. Case-control: efficient for rare diseases. Cross-sectional: prevalence only, no temporality. Ecological fallacy in aggregate studies.

**Q:** Differentiate relative risk, odds ratio, and hazard ratio.

**A:** RR = risk exposed/risk unexposed (cohort). OR = (ad)/(bc) from 2x2 (case-control). HR = instantaneous risk ratio from survival analysis with censoring

OR approximates RR when outcome is rare (<10%). Case-control cannot calculate RR directly. HR accounts for variable follow-up and censoring. All measure association not causation.

**Q:** What are the Bradford Hill criteria for causation?

**A:** Strength, Consistency, Specificity, Temporality (only absolute), Biological gradient, Plausibility, Coherence, Experiment, Analogy

Temporality is the ONLY essential criterion. Strong associations less likely confounded. Originally applied to smoking and lung cancer (1965). Help distinguish causation from correlation in observational studies.

**Q:** Differentiate confounding from effect modification.

**A:** Confounding: third variable distorts exposure-outcome relationship (bias to eliminate). Effect modification: exposure effect differs across subgroups (biological finding to report stratified). Test: adjusted vs crude differs >10% = confounding

Address confounding: randomization, restriction, matching, stratification, multivariable analysis. Effect modification: report stratum-specific estimates. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables always possible in observational studies.

**Q:** What is the difference between internal and external validity?

**A:** Internal: results free from bias within study (accuracy). External: results generalizable to other populations. Internal is necessary but not sufficient for external

Threats to internal: selection bias, information bias, confounding. Threats to external: narrow inclusion, single-center, volunteer bias. RCTs often high internal but low external. Pragmatic trials sacrifice some internal for generalizability.

**Q:** Define and differentiate key measures of disease frequency.

**A:** Incidence rate = cases/person-time. Cumulative incidence = cases/population at risk. Attack rate = cases during outbreak/at-risk. Mortality rate = deaths/population. CFR = deaths/cases x 100%

Person-time accounts for variable follow-up. Age standardization allows population comparisons (Canada uses 2011 population for direct standardization). Attack rate used in outbreak settings.

**Q:** What are Type I and Type II errors?

**A:** Type I (alpha=0.05): false positive, rejecting true null. Type II (beta=0.20): false negative, failing to reject false null. Power = 1-beta >= 0.80

Larger sample reduces both errors. Multiple comparisons inflate Type I (Bonferroni correction). Underpowered studies risk Type II. A priori sample size calculation essential. Non-significant result does not prove null.

**Q:** What do p-values and confidence intervals represent?

**A:** p-value: probability of results as extreme if null true. 95% CI: 95% of repeated CIs would contain true parameter. CI excluding null (0 for diff, 1 for ratio) = significant

p-value is NOT probability null is true. Wide CI = imprecision (small sample). CI more informative than p-value (gives effect size + precision). Statistical significance does not equal clinical significance.

**Q:** What are the key nationally notifiable diseases in Canada?

**A:** TB, HIV/AIDS, measles, invasive meningococcal, pertussis, syphilis, gonorrhea, hepatitis B/C, cholera, Ebola, mpox (~60 total). Report: clinician to local PH to province to PHAC

Mandated by provincial legislation. Urgent diseases (measles, meningococcal) require immediate reporting. PHAC coordinates via CNDSS. List varies slightly by jurisdiction.

**Q:** What are the steps in an outbreak investigation?

**A:** 1) Verify diagnosis 2) Confirm outbreak (observed>expected) 3) Case definition 4) Case finding 5) Descriptive epi (person/place/time, epi curve) 6) Hypotheses 7) Analytical study 8) Control measures 9) Communicate

Epi curve shape: point source (single peak), continuous (plateau), propagated (successive peaks). Do not delay control measures for investigation completion. R0 guides control: need 1-1/R0 reduction.

**Q:** Name and define the levels of prevention.

**A:** Primary: prevent disease (vaccination). Secondary: early detection (screening). Tertiary: reduce disability (rehab). Quaternary: prevent overdiagnosis/overtreatment (deprescribing). Primordial: prevent risk factors (policy)

Population-level primary prevention has greatest impact. Most screening is secondary. Periodic health exam incorporates all levels. Quaternary prevention increasingly emphasized to combat medicalization.

**Q:** How are attributable risk and population attributable risk calculated and used?

**A:** AR = risk_exposed - risk_unexposed. AR% = AR/risk_exposed. PAR = risk_total - risk_unexposed. PAR% = PAR/risk_total. NNT = 1/AR

PAR depends on both RR AND prevalence of exposure. High-prevalence modest-RR exposures may have larger PAR than rare high-RR exposures. PAR guides public health priority setting. AR guides individual patient counseling.

**Q:** What are the key elements of the Canadian Pandemic Influenza Preparedness plan?

**A:** Surveillance, public health measures (isolation/quarantine), mass vaccination, antiviral stockpiling (oseltamivir), healthcare surge capacity, risk communication, F/P/T coordination via PHAC

COVID-19 lessons: importance of early border measures, domestic vaccine manufacturing, equity in distribution, mental health supports. NESS maintains PPE stockpiles. Pandemic preparedness requires all-of-government approach.

**Q:** What are the Canadian adult immunization schedule highlights?

**A:** Tdap x1 then Td q10y. Influenza annually >=6mo. PCV20 or PCV15+PPSV23 >=65 or high-risk. Shingrix >=50 x2 doses. HPV catch-up to 26. COVID per NACI

NACI provides evidence-based recs; provinces implement variably. HCW requirements: influenza, HepB, varicella. Immunocompromised: avoid live vaccines (MMR, varicella, LAIV). Pregnancy: Tdap each pregnancy 27-32wk.

**Q:** What are Choosing Wisely Canada's key recommendations?

**A:** Avoid: routine preop testing for low-risk surgery, imaging for uncomplicated low back pain <6wks, antibiotics for viral URI, Foley catheters without indication, benzodiazepines in elderly

Resource stewardship: ordering only tests/treatments with clear benefit. Overdiagnosis leads to overtreatment cascades. Shared decision-making helps patients understand benefit vs harm tradeoffs.

**Q:** What is Choosing Wisely Canada, and what are three of its most impactful recommendations for primary care?

**A:** Choosing Wisely Canada is a physician-led campaign to reduce unnecessary tests and treatments. Key primary care recommendations include: (1) Don't order imaging for uncomplicated low back pain within the first 6 weeks, (2) Don't use antibiotics for upper respiratory tract infections (most are viral), (3) Don't order routine preoperative tests (CBC, ECG, CXR) for low-risk surgeries in healthy patients.

Choosing Wisely Canada was launched in 2014 and now includes recommendations from over 70 Canadian specialty societies. It is modeled on the American Board of Internal Medicine Foundation initiative. The campaign addresses overdiagnosis and overtreatment, which contribute to patient harm, healthcare costs, and resource waste in the Canadian publicly-funded system.

**Q:** A 30-year-old healthy patient is scheduled for elective knee arthroscopy. The surgeon orders preoperative CBC, electrolytes, INR, ECG, and chest X-ray. Is this appropriate?

**A:** No. For a healthy patient under 50 undergoing low-risk surgery, routine preoperative testing is not recommended. Choosing Wisely Canada and the Canadian Anesthesiologists' Society recommend against routine preoperative tests in asymptomatic patients having low-risk procedures, as they rarely change management and can lead to false positives, anxiety, and unnecessary follow-up.

The Canadian Anesthesiologists' Society's Choosing Wisely recommendation specifically targets this scenario. Studies show routine preoperative testing in low-risk patients does not improve surgical outcomes. Preoperative tests should be guided by patient history, physical examination, and the nature of the procedure, not ordered by protocol.

**Q:** A 35-year-old presents with acute low back pain of 3 days duration, no red flags. They request an MRI. What is the evidence-based response?

**A:** Imaging is not indicated for acute low back pain without red flags (e.g., cauda equina syndrome, cancer, fracture, infection). Most acute low back pain is self-limiting. Explain that early imaging does not improve outcomes, may reveal incidental findings leading to unnecessary interventions, and recommend conservative management with reassurance, activity modification, and analgesia.

This is one of the top Choosing Wisely Canada recommendations. The Canadian Association of Radiologists endorses avoiding imaging for non-specific low back pain within the first 6 weeks. Studies show that early imaging leads to increased surgery, opioid use, and healthcare costs without improving patient outcomes. Red flags requiring imaging include progressive neurological deficits, suspected cancer, infection, or cauda equina syndrome.

**Q:** What is a QALY (Quality-Adjusted Life Year), and how is it used in Canadian healthcare decision-making?

**A:** A QALY combines quantity and quality of life: 1 QALY = 1 year of life in perfect health. A year of life at 0.5 quality = 0.5 QALYs. Cost-effectiveness is expressed as cost per QALY gained. In Canada, CADTH (Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health) commonly uses a willingness-to-pay threshold of approximately $50,000-$100,000 per QALY to evaluate new health technologies.

CADTH (formerly CCOHTA) uses cost-per-QALY analyses to make drug and technology funding recommendations to provincial formularies. The pan-Canadian Pharmaceutical Alliance (pCPA) negotiates drug prices informed by CADTH reviews. Understanding QALYs is essential for appreciating how Canada allocates finite healthcare resources in a publicly funded system.

**Q:** What is overdiagnosis, and how does it differ from a false positive?

**A:** Overdiagnosis is the detection of a true condition that would never have caused symptoms or death during the patient's lifetime (e.g., indolent prostate cancer found on screening). A false positive is an abnormal test result in someone who does not actually have the condition. Overdiagnosis leads to real diagnoses and treatments that cause net harm; false positives lead to further testing before the diagnosis is excluded.

Overdiagnosis is a major concern in cancer screening programs. For example, the Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care weighs overdiagnosis risks in PSA screening and thyroid cancer screening recommendations. Overdiagnosed patients receive unnecessary treatment with real side effects but experience no benefit, as their condition would never have become clinically significant.

**Q:** What is a 'cascade of care' (or diagnostic cascade), and how does it relate to low-value care?

**A:** A cascade of care occurs when an initial unnecessary test yields an incidental or borderline finding, prompting further tests, referrals, procedures, and treatments — each carrying its own risks — that ultimately provide no net benefit to the patient. Example: routine CXR reveals a benign pulmonary nodule, leading to CT, then PET scan, then biopsy with pneumothorax.

Cascades of care are a key concept in Choosing Wisely Canada's messaging. They illustrate how a single low-value test can trigger a chain of escalating interventions with cumulative risk and cost. Recognizing cascade potential is important for MCCQE1 questions about appropriate test ordering and explains why the initial decision to test must be clinically justified.

**Q:** The Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care recommends against routine PSA screening for prostate cancer in average-risk men. What is the rationale?

**A:** The harms of PSA screening (overdiagnosis of indolent cancers, unnecessary biopsies with complications, overtreatment with side effects including incontinence and erectile dysfunction, anxiety) outweigh the modest reduction in prostate cancer mortality. Many men diagnosed through screening have cancers that would never become clinically significant in their lifetime.

The Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care issued this recommendation after systematic review. While PSA can detect cancer early, the majority of screen-detected prostate cancers are low-grade and indolent. Active surveillance for low-risk cancers is now preferred over immediate treatment, but screening itself generates cascades leading to overtreatment.

**Q:** A patient with a viral upper respiratory infection requests antibiotics. They insist they 'always get antibiotics' for colds. How should you manage this using resource stewardship principles?

**A:** Acknowledge the patient's concern, explain that antibiotics are ineffective against viruses and carry real risks (allergic reactions, C. diff, antimicrobial resistance), offer evidence-based symptomatic treatments, provide a safety-net plan (return if symptoms worsen or don't improve within expected timeframe), and consider a delayed prescription strategy if appropriate.

Unnecessary antibiotic prescribing for URTIs is a top Choosing Wisely Canada target. Antimicrobial resistance is recognized by the WHO and Public Health Agency of Canada as a critical threat. The delayed prescription strategy (providing a prescription to fill only if symptoms worsen) has been shown to reduce antibiotic use by approximately 50% while maintaining patient satisfaction.

**Q:** What is the role of CADTH in Canadian healthcare resource allocation?

**A:** CADTH (Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health) is an independent, not-for-profit organization that provides evidence-based assessments of drugs, medical devices, and clinical procedures. It conducts health technology assessments (HTAs), provides drug reviews for formulary listing decisions, and produces optimal use recommendations. Provincial drug plans use CADTH recommendations to guide funding decisions.

CADTH serves all Canadian provinces and territories except Quebec (which has its own INESSS). Its Common Drug Review (CDR) and pan-Canadian Oncology Drug Review (pCODR) evaluate new drugs for cost-effectiveness. CADTH recommendations inform the pan-Canadian Pharmaceutical Alliance (pCPA) negotiations, directly impacting which drugs are publicly funded.

**Q:** A colleague routinely orders daily CBC and electrolytes on all hospitalized patients regardless of clinical indication. What resource stewardship concerns does this raise, and how would you address it?

**A:** This represents low-value care: routine daily bloodwork in stable patients increases costs, contributes to hospital-acquired anemia (from repeated phlebotomy), causes patient discomfort, may yield incidental findings triggering cascades, and wastes laboratory resources. Address it through: respectful peer discussion, citing Choosing Wisely Canada recommendations, and supporting institutional initiatives to implement indication-based ordering.

Choosing Wisely Canada's hospitalist recommendations specifically advise against routine daily bloodwork without clinical indication. Studies show that reducing routine lab ordering by 30-50% does not adversely affect patient outcomes. Many Canadian hospitals have implemented order sets that require clinical indication for laboratory tests, reducing low-value ordering and iatrogenic anemia.

**Q:** What is the Framingham Risk Score used for in dyslipidemia management?

**A:** The Framingham Risk Score (FRS) calculates 10-year risk of a major CVD event based on gender, age, total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, systolic BP, and smoking. Risk categories: >20% high-risk; 10-19.9% moderate risk; <10% low-risk.

The FRS is used to guide statin therapy decisions in primary prevention. Note that FRS does NOT apply to patients with familial hypercholesterolemia — all FH patients are considered high-risk regardless of FRS. The Reynolds Risk Score additionally incorporates hs-CRP and family history of MI.

**Q:** What are the GRADE categories of recommendation strength and evidence quality used by the Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care?

**A:** Recommendation Strength: Strong (confident that desirable effects outweigh undesirable effects — most individuals will benefit from recommended action) or Conditional (desirable effects probably outweigh undesirable effects — different choices appropriate for different individuals, requiring shared decision-making). Evidence Quality: High (confident true effect is close to estimate), Moderate (likely close but may differ), Low/Very Low (true effect may differ substantially from estimate).

GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) is the standardized framework used by CTFPHC to communicate both the strength of recommendations and the quality of evidence. Understanding these distinctions is critical for interpreting screening and preventive care guidelines on the MCCQE1.

## Social Determinants of Health

**Q:** What are the social determinants of health (SDOH) and how do they affect health outcomes?

**A:** SDOH: non-medical factors influencing health — income and wealth, education, employment, housing, food security, social support, early childhood development, Indigenous status, disability, race/racism, gender — account for 30-55% of health outcomes

WHO Commission on SDOH (2008): unfair distribution of SDOH is responsible for most health inequities globally. In Canada: individuals in lowest income quintile have 7-10 year lower life expectancy than highest quintile. PHAC uses SDOH framework for population health policy.

**Q:** What is the health gradient and what does it demonstrate?

**A:** Health gradient: continuous dose-response relationship between socioeconomic status and health outcomes — those at the top are healthier than those just below them, all the way down the social ladder (not just poor vs. non-poor)

Whitehall studies (UK civil servants): demonstrated that even within a working, employed population, there is a continuous gradient of health — those with higher job grade had better health. Gradient reflects differential exposure to SDOH, not just absolute poverty.

**Q:** What are the health disparities faced by Indigenous Peoples in Canada?

**A:** Higher rates of: T2DM (3-5x), TB (10x), suicide (3x for First Nations adults, up to 10x for Inuit youth), infant mortality (2x), poverty, food insecurity, substance use, mental health issues; shorter life expectancy by 10-15 years

PHAC and Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC): health disparities in Indigenous peoples are the result of colonization, residential schools, systemic racism, and ongoing socioeconomic inequities. Cultural safety and Indigenous-led health services are essential. Physicians should be aware of trauma-informed care.

**Q:** What is structural racism and how does it affect healthcare?

**A:** Structural racism: laws, policies, and institutional practices that disadvantage racialized groups — manifests in healthcare as unequal access, implicit bias, delayed diagnoses, and treatment disparities for Black, Indigenous, and other racialized peoples in Canada

CMAJ (Canadian Medical Association Journal) and CMA have acknowledged systemic racism in Canadian healthcare. Examples: anti-Indigenous racism in emergency rooms (Joyce Echaquan), racial disparities in pain management, lower cancer screening rates. Antiracism training and cultural humility are required competencies.

**Q:** What is food insecurity and what are its health consequences?

**A:** Food insecurity: inadequate or uncertain access to enough food due to lack of resources; associated with: T2DM, cardiovascular disease, poor mental health, higher mortality — affects ~12% of Canadian households

PHAC statistics: food insecurity is highest among Indigenous peoples (28%), racialized communities, single-parent households, low-income families. Adequate income (basic income) is more effective than food banks for addressing food insecurity. Clinical screening: Hunger Vital Sign (2-question tool).

**Q:** What is housing insecurity and homelessness, and how does it affect health?

**A:** Housing insecurity/homelessness: associated with increased rates of infectious disease (TB, HIV, hepatitis C), mental illness, substance use, injury, trauma, and 3-10x higher mortality than housed individuals of same age

PHAC: ~235,000 Canadians experience homelessness annually. Housing First approach (Pathways to Housing): provide stable housing without prerequisites — reduces hospitalizations and emergency visits by 50%. Clinical: inquire about housing at every visit; connect with social services.

**Q:** What is the adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) framework?

**A:** ACEs: traumatic childhood experiences (abuse, neglect, household dysfunction) associated with dose-response increases in adult health risks (CVD, cancer, substance use, mental illness, premature death) — 4+ ACEs triples risk of many adverse outcomes

CDC ACE study: ACEs are extremely common (64% of Americans have ≥1; 12% have ≥4) and have lifelong health consequences via toxic stress mechanisms (HPA axis dysregulation, inflammation). Trauma-informed care is a core competency for Canadian physicians. Screening: ACE questionnaire.

**Q:** What is health literacy and why is it important in clinical care?

**A:** Health literacy: ability to obtain, process, and understand health information to make informed decisions; low health literacy associated with poor adherence, more ED visits, higher mortality — affects ~55% of Canadians

Canadian Institute for Health Information: health literacy varies across populations (lower in elderly, immigrants, low education). Strategies: plain language, teach-back method, pictorial instructions, avoiding medical jargon. All educational materials should be at Grade 6 reading level or lower.

**Q:** What is the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion?

**A:** Ottawa Charter (1986): first international conference on health promotion; 5 action areas: Build Healthy Public Policy, Create Supportive Environments, Strengthen Community Action, Develop Personal Skills, Reorient Health Services; prerequisites for health: peace, shelter, education, food, income, equity

The Ottawa Charter shifted focus from individual behavior to social determinants. Health promotion: enabling people to increase control over and improve their health. Social gradient of health recognized. Canadian contribution: Marc Lalonde report (1974) — first national health framework recognizing social determinants.

**Q:** What are the principles of cultural humility and cultural safety in Canadian healthcare?

**A:** Cultural humility: lifelong commitment to self-reflection, learning from others, power imbalances in healthcare; Cultural safety: environment where patients feel safe to express cultural identity without judgment; goes beyond cultural competence (mastering facts about cultures) to systemic power analysis

College of Family Physicians of Canada and Medical Council of Canada: cultural safety and humility are core competencies. Requires recognizing and addressing systemic racism, colonialism, and power differentials. Indigenous patients: duty to provide culturally safe care. Resource: Indigenous Cultural Safety Training.

**Q:** What are key social determinants of health?

**A:** Income/poverty, education, employment, housing, food security, early childhood development, social inclusion, Indigenous status, racism/discrimination

PHAC framework. Indigenous health disparities: lower life expectancy, higher suicide rates, infectious disease burden, intergenerational trauma from residential schools. Cultural safety in care.

**Q:** What are the social determinants of health recognized in Canada?

**A:** Income, education, employment, social/physical environments, biology, health services, gender, culture, Indigenous status, social support, childhood experiences. Income is strongest single predictor

PHAC Key Health Inequalities report documents disparities across 72 indicators. Health equity requires addressing root causes not just healthcare access. 1% increase in income inequality associated with increased mortality across provinces.

**Q:** What are the key health disparities facing Indigenous peoples in Canada?

**A:** Life expectancy 10-15y lower. Diabetes 3-5x, TB >40x (Inuit), youth suicide 5-7x, infant mortality 2-3x. Lower access to clean water, housing, healthcare

Rooted in colonization, residential schools, systemic racism. TRC Calls to Action include 7 health recommendations. Jordans Principle ensures First Nations children get needed services. Cultural safety training now mandatory.

**Q:** How does cultural safety differ from cultural competence?

**A:** Competence: learning about other cultures. Safety: recognizing power imbalances, addressing systemic racism, patient defines if encounter felt safe. Requires self-reflection on own biases

Developed from Maori nursing in NZ. Shifts from knowing the other to examining how colonial structures affect care. CPSO and regulators mandate training. NOT about memorizing cultural practices.

**Q:** What is the impact of poverty on health in Canada?

**A:** Lowest income quintile: 3-5y lower life expectancy, 2x diabetes, higher CVD/mental illness. Child poverty: more asthma, obesity, developmental delay. ~1 in 7 below poverty line

Food insecurity affects 1 in 8 households. Canada Poverty Reduction Strategy targets 50% reduction by 2030. Screen with single question: Do you have difficulty making ends meet? Poverty is a modifiable risk factor.

**Q:** How do adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) affect adult health?

**A:** >=4 ACEs: 4-12x substance use/depression/suicide risk; 2-4x CVD, cancer, COPD. Dose-response relationship (Felitti 1998 ACE Study)

Mechanisms: toxic stress on brain development, HPA axis dysregulation, health risk behaviors. Trauma-informed care recognizes widespread ACE impact. ACE screening increasingly used in Canadian primary care.

**Q:** What are the 5 principles of the Canada Health Act and its coverage gaps?

**A:** Public administration, Comprehensiveness, Universality, Portability, Accessibility. NOT covered: dental, vision, outpatient Rx, mental health, home care, LTC

~30% healthcare spending is private. National pharmacare proposed. Dental care program for low-income started 2022-23. Provinces administer own systems within CHA. Federal transfer payments conditional on CHA compliance.

**Q:** What is health literacy and how does it affect outcomes?

**A:** Ability to access, understand, and use health information. ~60% Canadian adults have inadequate health literacy. Low literacy: poorer self-management, more ED visits, higher mortality

Use plain language (grade 6-8), teach-back method, visual aids. Numeracy often worse than literacy. Ottawa Decision Aids are Canadian evidence-based shared decision-making tools. Vulnerable: elderly, immigrants, low SES.

**Q:** What are the health effects of homelessness in Canada?

**A:** Life expectancy 42-52y. 3-6x mortality. Mental illness 30-40%, high substance use, TB, HIV, HCV, frostbite. ~235,000 experience homelessness annually

Housing First model (no preconditions) has strong evidence. Federal Reaching Home strategy funds programs. Low-barrier care, harm reduction, trauma-informed approach. Physicians should connect with social services and advocate for housing.

**Q:** What is harm reduction and what programs exist in Canada?

**A:** Policies reducing substance use harms without requiring abstinence. Programs: supervised consumption sites, needle exchange, naloxone distribution, safe supply, OAT (methadone, suboxone)

Insite (Vancouver 2003) was first SCS. Evidence: reduced OD deaths, HIV, public drug use; increased treatment uptake. Health Canada grants CDSA exemptions. Safe supply provides pharmaceutical alternatives to toxic unregulated supply.

**Q:** What are the TRC health-related Calls to Action?

**A:** CTA 18: acknowledge disparities from government policies. 19: close health gaps. 22: value Indigenous healing. 23-24: increase Indigenous HCWs and cultural competency training for all

TRC documented residential school abuses (150,000 children, >4000 deaths). Intergenerational trauma contributes to current disparities. All Canadian medical schools now include Indigenous health curriculum. MCCQE expects this knowledge.

**Q:** What is implicit bias in healthcare?

**A:** Unconscious attitudes affecting decisions. Affects pain management (Black patients less analgesia), referrals, diagnostic workup, communication. IAT measures unconscious bias

Mitigate: awareness, perspective-taking, individuation, counter-stereotypic imaging, institutional policies, diverse workforce. Canadian regulators increasingly require EDI training. Systemic bias compounds individual bias.

**Q:** What should be screened in the refugee health assessment?

**A:** TB (CXR+IGRA), HIV, HBV, HCV, syphilis, malaria (endemic areas), parasites, iron deficiency, lead (children), dental, mental health (PTSD), catch-up vaccines, vision/hearing

CCIRH guidelines. IFHP covers government-assisted refugees. Latent TB 30-50% from high-burden countries. Mental health screening includes torture/trauma history with culturally appropriate tools.

**Q:** How does climate change affect health in Canada?

**A:** Direct: heat illness, wildfire smoke, extreme weather. Indirect: vector-borne expansion (Lyme northward), food/water insecurity, mental health. Disproportionate: Indigenous, northern, low-income

Canada warming 2x global average, Arctic 3x. Lyme cases up >10-fold since 2009. Wildfire smoke growing respiratory concern. Lancet Countdown Canada tracks 41 climate-health indicators.

**Q:** Describe the Canadian opioid crisis: epidemiology and response.

**A:** ~22 deaths/day (~8000/yr). 80%+ involve fentanyl. Response: Good Samaritan Act (2017), expanded naloxone, SCS, prescribing guidelines, safe supply pilots

BC highest death rate. Good Samaritan Act protects people calling 911 during OD. Canadian pain guidelines emphasize non-opioid multimodal analgesia first-line. Toxic unregulated supply is primary driver.

**Q:** What is MAID in Canada and what are the eligibility criteria?

**A:** >=18, capable, eligible for Canadian health services, grievous/irremediable condition causing intolerable suffering. Track 1: death reasonably foreseeable. Track 2: not foreseeable (extra safeguards). 2 independent assessments

Legal since 2016 (Carter v Canada). Bill C-7 (2021) expanded Track 2. Mental illness as sole condition eligible March 2027 (delayed). ~13,000 MAID deaths 2022 (4.1% of all deaths). Conscience rights protect objecting providers.

**Q:** The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) Call to Action #18 specifically addresses health disparities. What does it call upon the federal, provincial, territorial, and Aboriginal governments to do?

**A:** Acknowledge that the current state of Aboriginal health in Canada is a direct result of previous Canadian government policies, including residential schools, and to recognize and implement the healthcare rights of Aboriginal people as identified in international law, constitutional law, and under the Treaties.

TRC Call to Action #18 is foundational for understanding Indigenous health in Canada. It establishes that health disparities are not incidental but are direct consequences of colonial policies including the Indian Residential School system. This framing is essential for culturally safe medical practice.

**Q:** What is the approximate life expectancy gap between First Nations people and non-Indigenous Canadians?

**A:** First Nations males live approximately 8-9 years less and First Nations females approximately 7-8 years less than non-Indigenous Canadians. Inuit populations face an even larger gap of approximately 10-15 years.

Statistics Canada and the First Nations Information Governance Centre data consistently show significant life expectancy disparities. Inuit Nunangat regions have the largest gaps, driven by high rates of suicide, respiratory disease, and injuries. These disparities reflect cumulative effects of social determinants of health.

**Q:** The infant mortality rate among First Nations populations in Canada is approximately how many times higher than the non-Indigenous rate?

**A:** Approximately 2-3 times higher than the non-Indigenous Canadian infant mortality rate.

First Nations and Inuit infant mortality rates remain significantly elevated compared to non-Indigenous Canadians. Contributing factors include limited access to prenatal care in remote communities, maternal evacuation policies for birth, higher rates of SIDS, and socioeconomic disparities. The Inuit infant mortality rate is particularly high, approximately 3 times the national average.

**Q:** A 42-year-old First Nations woman presents with polyuria, polydipsia, and a random glucose of 14.2 mmol/L. Type 2 diabetes prevalence in First Nations populations is approximately how many times greater than the general Canadian population?

**A:** 3-5 times higher. Age-adjusted prevalence of type 2 diabetes in First Nations adults is approximately 17-20%, compared to roughly 5-7% in the general Canadian population.

The First Nations Regional Health Survey and PHAC data show dramatically elevated diabetes rates in Indigenous communities. This is driven by the nutrition transition from traditional diets to processed foods, food insecurity, genetic susceptibility (thrifty gene hypothesis), and intergenerational effects of colonization. Gestational diabetes rates are also 2-3 times higher in First Nations women.

**Q:** Suicide rates among Inuit youth in Canada are approximately how many times higher than the national average? What is a key protective factor identified in research?

**A:** Inuit youth suicide rates are approximately 5-11 times the national average. Key protective factors include cultural continuity, self-governance, community control over land, education, health services, and cultural facilities.

Chandler and Lalonde's landmark Canadian research demonstrated that First Nations communities with greater cultural continuity (self-governance, land claims, control of education/health/police/fire services, cultural facilities) had dramatically lower suicide rates. Some communities with all protective factors had zero youth suicides over the study period.

**Q:** Tuberculosis incidence in Inuit populations in Canada is approximately how many times higher than in Canadian-born non-Indigenous people?

**A:** Approximately 290-300 times higher. Inuit TB incidence rates are among the highest in the world for any defined population within a high-income country.

PHAC data shows Inuit TB rates are staggeringly elevated, with periodic outbreaks in Nunavut communities. Contributing factors include overcrowded housing (average 2-3 times national occupancy), food insecurity leading to immunocompromise, limited access to diagnostic and treatment services, and historical inadequacies in TB treatment programs. First Nations rates are approximately 34 times higher than the Canadian-born non-Indigenous rate.

**Q:** What is Jordan's Principle, and what event prompted its creation?

**A:** Jordan's Principle is a child-first principle ensuring First Nations children can access the public services they need when they need them. It is named after Jordan River Anderson, a Manitoba First Nations child with complex medical needs who died in hospital at age 5 in 2005 while the federal and provincial governments disputed which was financially responsible for his at-home care.

Jordan's Principle was adopted by the House of Commons in 2007. The 2016 Canadian Human Rights Tribunal ruling (First Nations Child and Family Caring Society v. Canada) found the government had applied Jordan's Principle too narrowly and ordered full implementation. It now covers all public services for all First Nations children, not just those with disabilities or complex needs.

**Q:** A 4-year-old First Nations child living on reserve needs speech-language therapy not available in their community. The provincial government says it is a federal responsibility; the federal government says it is provincial. Under Jordan's Principle, what should happen?

**A:** The government of first contact must pay for the service and provide it without delay. Jurisdictional disputes must be resolved later through interdepartmental or intergovernmental mechanisms. The child's needs come first.

Jordan's Principle eliminates service gaps caused by jurisdictional disputes between federal and provincial governments regarding First Nations children. Since the 2016 CHRT ruling, the principle applies to all First Nations children, covers all government services (health, education, social), and requires services comparable to those available to other Canadian children.

**Q:** What is the Non-Insured Health Benefits (NIHB) program, who is eligible, and what does it cover?

**A:** NIHB is a federal program administered by Indigenous Services Canada that covers eligible First Nations and Inuit clients for medically necessary health benefits not covered by provincial/territorial plans or private insurance. Benefits include prescription drugs, dental care, vision care, medical supplies and equipment, mental health counselling, and medical transportation.

NIHB is distinct from provincial health insurance and covers supplemental benefits. Eligibility includes registered First Nations under the Indian Act and recognized Inuit. Métis are generally not covered by NIHB. Medical transportation is a critical component, especially for remote communities requiring evacuation for specialized care.

**Q:** A physician prescribes a medication for a First Nations patient, but it requires NIHB prior approval. The patient cannot afford the medication out-of-pocket. What is an important consideration regarding NIHB drug coverage?

**A:** NIHB maintains a drug formulary with open, limited-use, and excluded categories. The physician should check if an alternative open-benefit medication is available, or submit a prior approval request. Delays in NIHB approval can create barriers to care, and physicians should advocate for timely approval and consider interim solutions.

NIHB prior approval processes can create significant access barriers, particularly in remote communities. Physicians working with First Nations and Inuit patients should be familiar with the NIHB formulary, the prior approval process, and how to advocate effectively. The NIHB provider guide is available through Indigenous Services Canada.

**Q:** What is intergenerational trauma in the context of Indigenous health in Canada, and how does it manifest clinically?

**A:** Intergenerational trauma refers to the transmission of trauma effects from residential school survivors and other colonial violence to subsequent generations who did not directly experience those events. Clinical manifestations include higher rates of mental health disorders, substance use, family violence, attachment difficulties, parenting challenges, and adverse childhood experiences in the children and grandchildren of survivors.

Research by Amy Bombay and others at Dalhousie University has demonstrated that the children and grandchildren of residential school survivors have elevated rates of depression, suicidal ideation, and substance use disorders. Epigenetic mechanisms, disrupted attachment patterns from institutional upbringing, and loss of cultural/parenting knowledge all contribute. The National Indian Residential School Crisis Line (1-866-925-4419) supports affected individuals.

**Q:** What are the four key diagnostic features used in the Canadian FASD diagnostic guidelines (2015 update), and what replaced the previous 4-Digit Diagnostic Code?

**A:** The 2015 Canadian FASD guidelines use: (1) prenatal alcohol exposure (confirmed or unknown), (2) neurodevelopmental assessment across 10 brain domains, (3) sentinel facial features (short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, thin upper lip), and (4) assessment of other conditions. The diagnosis uses the terms 'FASD with sentinel facial features' and 'FASD without sentinel facial features,' replacing FAS/pFAS/ARND terminology.

The 2015 Canadian guidelines (Cook et al., updated from Chudley et al. 2005) shifted to a simplified diagnostic framework. Three or more brain domains must be significantly impaired (≥2 SD below mean). Canada uses a multidisciplinary team approach for diagnosis. FASD prevalence in some Indigenous communities is elevated due to colonial disruption, but stereotyping must be avoided — FASD affects all populations.

**Q:** A 7-year-old child in care presents with learning difficulties, impulsivity, and poor social skills. Physical exam shows short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, and thin upper lip. Prenatal alcohol exposure is confirmed. How is this child diagnosed and managed under Canadian FASD guidelines?

**A:** Diagnosis: FASD with sentinel facial features. Management includes multidisciplinary neurodevelopmental assessment across 10 brain domains, early intervention programs, educational accommodations (IEP), behavioural supports, caregiver education, and stable placement. There is no pharmacological cure; management is supportive and adaptive.

Canadian FASD guidelines require assessment of 10 brain domains: motor skills, neuroanatomy, cognition, language, academic achievement, memory, attention, executive function, affect regulation, and adaptive behaviour. Early diagnosis improves outcomes. FASD is a leading cause of developmental disability in Canada, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 4% in the general population.

**Q:** What are the key social determinants of health disproportionately affecting Indigenous communities in Canada regarding housing?

**A:** Indigenous communities face overcrowding (Inuit homes average 2-3x national occupancy rates), inadequate housing (mould, poor ventilation, lack of running water), housing shortages on reserves, and homelessness in urban centres. These conditions contribute to increased respiratory infections, TB transmission, mental health impacts, and family violence.

The 2021 Census showed 16.4% of First Nations on reserve lived in housing needing major repairs versus 6.0% nationally. Overcrowding is a major driver of the TB epidemic in Inuit Nunangat. The National Housing Strategy has Indigenous-specific components, but significant gaps remain. Housing is a critical upstream determinant of Indigenous health outcomes.

**Q:** As of recent data, approximately how many long-term drinking water advisories affect First Nations communities in Canada, and what are the health implications?

**A:** While the federal government has lifted many long-term advisories, dozens of First Nations communities remain under drinking water advisories. Health implications include gastrointestinal illness, inability to practice proper hygiene, skin conditions, reliance on bottled water or sugary beverages contributing to dental caries and diabetes, and significant mental health impacts from chronic insecurity.

Access to clean drinking water is a fundamental determinant of health. Many First Nations communities have experienced boil water advisories lasting years or decades. The Neskantaga First Nation had the longest-running advisory in Canada (over 25 years). Lack of clean water also limits food preparation and wound care, compounding health disparities.

**Q:** How does food insecurity affect Indigenous communities in Canada, particularly in Northern and remote regions?

**A:** Food insecurity rates in Nunavut approach 46-70% of households (highest in Canada). Factors include extreme costs of market food in remote communities, declining access to traditional/country food due to climate change and contaminants, loss of traditional knowledge, and poverty. Health impacts include malnutrition, obesity paradox, diabetes, iron deficiency anemia, and childhood developmental delays.

The Nutrition North Canada program subsidizes perishable foods shipped to remote communities but has been criticized for inadequacy. Traditional/country food (caribou, seal, Arctic char, berries) is nutritionally superior to market alternatives but faces threats from climate change and environmental contaminants. Food sovereignty is a key concept in Indigenous health advocacy.

**Q:** What is the Indian Act (1876, amended), and how does it affect healthcare access for Indigenous peoples in Canada?

**A:** The Indian Act is federal legislation governing First Nations people and reserves. Healthcare implications include: only registered (Status) Indians are eligible for NIHB, creating coverage gaps for non-Status First Nations and Métis; the Act created the reserve system limiting healthcare infrastructure; it historically banned Traditional practices (potlatch, sun dance) disrupting healing traditions; and it established the framework for residential schools.

The Indian Act remains in force and creates a two-tiered system where Status Indians have different health coverage than other Indigenous peoples. The Act's registration provisions (including historical gender discrimination under Section 6) determined who received federal health benefits. Bill S-3 (2017) addressed some gender-based inequities in registration but gaps remain.

**Q:** What was the Sixties Scoop, and what are its lasting health impacts?

**A:** The Sixties Scoop (1960s-1980s) was the mass removal of Indigenous children from their families by child welfare authorities and their placement with non-Indigenous foster or adoptive families across Canada and internationally. Lasting health impacts include identity loss, cultural disconnection, attachment disorders, elevated rates of depression, PTSD, substance use disorders, and suicidality among survivors.

An estimated 20,000+ Indigenous children were removed during the Sixties Scoop. In 2017, the Ontario Superior Court ruled the federal government liable for the harm caused. The Sixties Scoop is distinct from but related to residential schools, and its legacy continues in the ongoing overrepresentation of Indigenous children in the child welfare system — Indigenous children represent over 50% of children in care despite being approximately 7.7% of the child population.

**Q:** TRC Calls to Action #19 and #20 address measurable health outcomes. What do they specifically request?

**A:** Call #19 asks the federal government to establish measurable goals to identify and close the gaps in health outcomes between Indigenous and non-Indigenous communities, with annual reporting. Call #20 asks for funding to address gaps including Indigenous healing centres, mental health and addiction treatment, and culturally appropriate prevention programs.

These Calls to Action demand accountability through measurable targets and dedicated funding. They recognize that eliminating health disparities requires both tracking progress (Call #19) and investing in Indigenous-led solutions (Call #20). The annual reports by the Yellowhead Institute consistently show slow implementation of TRC Calls to Action across Canadian governments.

**Q:** What is health literacy and how does it affect patient care?

**A:** Ability to obtain, process, and understand basic health information to make appropriate health decisions. Low health literacy associated with: worse outcomes, higher hospitalization rates, lower medication adherence

60% of Canadians have inadequate health literacy. Teach-back method: ask patient to explain in own words. Use plain language, visual aids, limit information to 3 key points per visit.

**Q:** What is the difference between cultural safety and cultural competence?

**A:** Cultural competence: provider learns about other cultures (knowledge-based). Cultural safety: provider examines own biases and power imbalances, creating environment where patient feels safe (relationship-based)

Cultural safety is the standard in Canadian healthcare, especially for Indigenous populations. Determined by the patient, not the provider. Requires ongoing self-reflection on privilege, power, and systemic racism.

**Q:** A 45-year-old patient presents with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes. On screening, they report food insecurity and inability to afford medications. Which social determinants of health are most directly contributing to their poor glycemic control?

**A:** Income/socioeconomic status and food insecurity are the primary social determinants. Others include access to healthcare (medication costs), health literacy, and potentially housing instability. In Canada, approximately 1 in 8 households experiences food insecurity, which directly impacts dietary management of diabetes.

The Public Health Agency of Canada identifies 12 key determinants of health. Food insecurity affects approximately 12-15% of Canadian households and is strongly associated with poor chronic disease outcomes. Physicians should recognize that prescribing dietary modifications without addressing food access is insufficient.

**Q:** What is health equity, and how does it differ from health equality?

**A:** Health equality means providing the same resources to everyone. Health equity means allocating resources according to need so that everyone has a fair opportunity to achieve their full health potential. Equity acknowledges that different populations face different barriers and may require different levels of support.

The Public Health Agency of Canada uses the health equity framework to guide policy. For example, providing the same clinic hours to all communities (equality) may not serve shift workers or rural populations. Equity-oriented approaches tailor services to address barriers faced by marginalized groups, such as offering evening clinics or mobile outreach.

**Q:** What validated screening question can a physician use to quickly assess food insecurity in clinical practice?

**A:** The Hunger Vital Sign uses two validated questions from the USDA Household Food Security Survey Module: (1) 'Within the past 12 months, we worried whether our food would run out before we got money to buy more' and (2) 'Within the past 12 months, the food we bought just didn't last and we didn't have money to get more.' An affirmative response to either is a positive screen.

The Hunger Vital Sign has 97% sensitivity for food insecurity and is endorsed by Canadian paediatric and family medicine organizations. PROOF (a Canadian research program at the University of Toronto) has shown that food insecurity in Canada is primarily an income problem, not a food supply problem, and is best addressed through income-based interventions.

**Q:** What is the difference between 'upstream' and 'downstream' health interventions? Give a Canadian example of each.

**A:** Downstream interventions treat illness after it occurs (e.g., treating a patient's pneumonia with antibiotics). Upstream interventions address root causes before illness develops (e.g., Canada's national housing strategy to reduce homelessness, which prevents health problems associated with inadequate shelter).

The upstream/downstream metaphor originates from public health and is central to the Health Advocate role. Canada's Poverty Reduction Strategy and the Canada Child Benefit are upstream interventions that address income as a determinant of health. The MCCQE1 expects candidates to recognize that clinical care alone cannot address health inequities.

**Q:** A physician notices that multiple patients from a local Indigenous community present with water-borne illness. What distinguishes individual-level from population-level advocacy in this scenario?

**A:** Individual-level advocacy: ensuring each patient receives appropriate treatment, connecting them to clean water sources, and addressing their immediate health needs. Population-level advocacy: reporting the issue to public health authorities, advocating for infrastructure improvements (e.g., lifting boil-water advisories), and supporting community-led initiatives to address the root cause.

As of recent years, dozens of long-term drinking water advisories remain in effect in First Nations communities across Canada. CanMEDS expects physicians to advocate at both individual and population levels. Population-level advocacy may involve writing to government officials, supporting community organizations, or contributing to policy recommendations.

**Q:** How should a physician assess a patient's health literacy, and why does it matter clinically?

**A:** Health literacy can be assessed using validated tools such as the Newest Vital Sign (NVS) or by using teach-back method (asking patients to explain instructions in their own words). Low health literacy is associated with poorer medication adherence, higher hospitalization rates, and worse chronic disease outcomes.

Approximately 60% of Canadian adults have inadequate health literacy (Canadian Council on Learning). The teach-back method is recommended as a practical clinical tool by the Canadian Literacy and Learning Network. Low health literacy crosses all demographics and is often invisible, making universal precautions (plain language for all patients) the best approach.

**Q:** A family physician identifies that a patient's chronic pain is exacerbated by precarious employment and lack of workplace accommodations. What advocacy actions are appropriate?

**A:** The physician can: write a letter supporting workplace accommodations under provincial human rights legislation, refer to occupational therapy for functional assessment, connect the patient with community legal services, provide documentation for disability benefits if applicable, and advocate for the patient with their employer (with consent).

In Canada, provincial Human Rights Codes and the duty to accommodate require employers to make reasonable accommodations for disability. Physicians play a key role by providing medical documentation. The CanMEDS Health Advocate role encompasses identifying social factors affecting health and taking action within one's sphere of influence.

**Q:** What is the Clinical Poverty Tool developed by Canadian researchers, and what domains does it screen?

**A:** The Clinical Poverty Tool (developed through Canadian research initiatives) screens for income-related social determinants including: income adequacy, housing stability, food security, and ability to afford medications. It uses simple questions that can be integrated into routine clinical encounters.

Poverty is the strongest social determinant of health in Canada, with low-income Canadians having significantly higher rates of chronic disease, mental illness, and premature mortality. Tools like poverty screening questions help physicians identify patients who may benefit from referrals to income support programs, community resources, or social workers.

**Q:** What are the key components of the 'Health in All Policies' approach, and how does it relate to physician advocacy?

**A:** Health in All Policies (HiAP) is an approach that systematically considers the health implications of government decisions across all sectors (transportation, housing, education, agriculture). It emphasizes intersectoral collaboration and aims to improve population health and health equity by addressing upstream determinants.

The WHO and Public Health Agency of Canada endorse HiAP as a framework for addressing social determinants. For example, urban planning decisions about bike lanes and green spaces impact physical activity and mental health. Physicians can contribute by providing expert testimony, participating in policy consultations, and supporting evidence-informed advocacy.

**Q:** A refugee patient newly arrived in Canada presents with untreated hypertension and diabetes. What system-level barriers to care should you anticipate and address as a health advocate?

**A:** Barriers include: waiting period for provincial health insurance (varies by province; some have 3-month wait), language barriers, unfamiliarity with the Canadian health system, cost of medications (no drug coverage initially), transportation, cultural differences in health beliefs, and potential trauma history affecting trust in authorities.

Physicians should be aware of IFHP coverage, connect patients with settlement agencies, and arrange interpreter services to facilitate equitable care.

## Public Health

**Q:** What is the difference between incidence rate and prevalence?

**A:** Incidence rate measures the number of NEW cases in a population over a specific time period, while prevalence measures the TOTAL number of existing cases at a point or over a period of time.

Prevalence is a function of incidence rate and disease duration. Prevalence favors chronic over acute cases and is useful for measuring disease burden and planning services. Point prevalence is assessed at one time, while period prevalence includes new cases and excludes terminations over a defined period.

**Q:** A study finds that countries with higher red wine consumption have lower rates of heart disease and concludes that red wine prevents heart disease. What type of fallacy is this?

**A:** Ecological fallacy

An ecological fallacy occurs when population-level data is erroneously used to draw conclusions about individuals. Just because countries with higher red wine consumption have lower heart disease rates does not mean that individuals who drink red wine have lower heart disease risk. Individual-level data is needed to support such conclusions.

**Q:** What are the five levels of evidence in evidence-based medicine?

**A:** Level I: large RCTs or meta-analyses of RCTs. Level II: small RCTs (may show non-significant trends). Level III: non-randomized studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional). Level IV: expert committees/consensus guidelines. Level V: individual expert opinion.

These levels reflect the nature of evidence, not direct evaluations of quality. While RCTs tend to be most credible, Level III evidence gains credibility when multiple studies from different locations report consistent findings. Level IV-V reflects decision-making necessary in the absence of published evidence.

**Q:** In Canada, which diseases require IMMEDIATE reporting to the Medical Officer of Health?

**A:** Diseases marked with asterisk (*) including: anthrax, botulism, cholera, diphtheria, Ebola, measles, plague, poliomyelitis, rabies, SARS, smallpox, and novel coronavirus (COVID-19), among others. These must be reported immediately by telephone or fax.

Mandatory disease reporting is required by provincial law. Failure to report can result in suspension of medical license. Reasons include: outbreak identification/control, preventing spread of significant threats, immunization-preventable diseases, surveillance, and cases requiring education/treatment/partner notification. Other diseases can be reported the next working day.

**Q:** What is a DALY and how is it calculated?

**A:** Disability Adjusted Life Year = years of life lost due to premature mortality + years lost due to disability, where 0 = perfect health and 1 = death. Used to assess burden of diseases in a population.

DALYs account for both premature death and time spent with disability (physical or mental). In contrast, QALYs (Quality Adjusted Life Years) weight years of life by quality (utility), ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health), and are used as an economic measure for medical interventions. Top causes of DALYs in Canada include neoplasms, cardiovascular disease, and musculoskeletal disorders.

**Q:** What occupational and workplace prevention strategies exist, and what rights do workers have under occupational health legislation?

**A:** Workers have the right to: (1) know about workplace hazards, (2) participate in health/safety committees, (3) refuse dangerous work, (4) stop dangerous work. Prevention follows the hierarchy of controls: elimination/substitution > engineering controls > administrative controls > PPE.

Workplace primary prevention uses the hierarchy of controls to minimize exposure. Secondary prevention involves medical surveillance (e.g., blood lead testing, PFTs for asthma, audiograms for hearing loss). Tertiary prevention focuses on treatment and return-to-work planning. Physicians distinguish between restrictions, limitations, abilities, and tolerance.

**Q:** What are the six core functions of public health in Canada?

**A:** 1. Health protection, 2. Health surveillance, 3. Disease and injury prevention, 4. Population health assessment, 5. Health promotion, 6. Emergency preparedness and response.

These functions are delivered by local public health units across Canada. Health protection involves regulation (e.g., safe water/food), while health promotion involves advocating for improved health through community and government measures as outlined in the Ottawa Charter.

**Q:** What are the five key strategies of the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion (1986)?

**A:** 1. Building healthy public policy, 2. Creating supportive environments, 3. Strengthening community action, 4. Developing personal skills, 5. Re-orienting health services.

The Ottawa Charter defines health promotion as the process of enabling people to increase control over their health. It emphasizes that health promotion is primarily done at the population level by public health professionals, though it can also occur through clinical interactions.

**Q:** What is the mnemonic SPIN and SNOUT for interpreting diagnostic tests?

**A:** SPIN: A SPecific test rules IN a diagnosis (few false positives, so a positive result is likely true). SNOUT: A SeNsitive test rules OUT a diagnosis (few false negatives, so a negative result is likely true).

Sensitivity and specificity are characteristics of the test itself and do not depend on disease prevalence. PPV and NPV, however, depend on the prevalence of the disease in the population being tested.

**Q:** What is the formula for Number Needed to Treat (NNT)?

**A:** NNT = 1 / Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR), where ARR = CER - EER (control event rate minus experimental event rate).

NNT represents the number of patients who need to be treated to achieve one additional favorable outcome. A lower NNT indicates a more effective treatment. NNT must be interpreted in context of the severity of the outcome being prevented.

**Q:** What are the Bradford Hill criteria for causation?

**A:** 1. Strength of association, 2. Consistency, 3. Specificity, 4. Temporal relationship, 5. Biological gradient, 6. Biological plausibility, 7. Coherence, 8. Experimental evidence, 9. Analogy.

Not all criteria must be fulfilled to establish causation. Modern EBM emphasizes experimental evidence as superior. Temporal relationship (exposure before outcome) is considered the most essential criterion for establishing causation.

**Q:** What distinguishes lead-time bias from length-time bias in screening?

**A:** Lead-time bias: overestimation of survival time from earlier detection by screening rather than truly improved outcomes. Length-time bias: screening detects more slowly progressing (less aggressive) cases, overestimating survival.

Both are important biases in evaluating screening programs. Lead-time bias occurs because screening advances the time of diagnosis without necessarily changing the time of death. Length-time bias occurs because aggressive, fast-moving diseases are less likely to be caught by periodic screening.

**Q:** What is the difference between Type I and Type II errors in statistical testing?

**A:** Type I (alpha) error: falsely rejecting the null hypothesis (concluding an effect exists when it doesn't). Type II (beta) error: falsely accepting the null hypothesis (missing a real effect).

Studies are typically designed to minimize Type I error since it can have larger clinical significance. Power = 1 - beta, representing the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. Increasing sample size decreases both types of errors.

**Q:** What are the ideal criteria for a screening test?

**A:** Disease: causes significant suffering, understood natural history, detectable asymptomatic stage, early intervention improves outcomes. Test: high sensitivity, safe, rapid, easy, inexpensive, acceptable. System: adequate capacity for follow-up and treatment, cost effective, sustainable.

Screening differs from diagnostic testing in that it targets apparently healthy individuals. Universal screening tests the entire population (e.g., PKU in newborns), while selective screening targets high-risk subgroups (e.g., mammography in women >50).

**Q:** What are the three levels of disease prevention with examples?

**A:** Primary: prevent disease onset (immunization, smoking cessation). Secondary: early detection of subclinical disease (mammography, Pap smears). Tertiary: treat/rehabilitate to prevent progression (DM monitoring with HbA1c, medication management).

Primordial prevention is an additional level that aims to prevent the development of risk factors themselves, such as childhood education about healthy behaviors. Each level progressively addresses disease further along its natural history.

**Q:** What are the ten steps to control a disease outbreak?

**A:** 1. Identify investigation team, 2. Establish existence of outbreak, 3. Verify diagnosis, 4. Define a case, 5. Find cases systematically, 6. Perform descriptive epidemiology, 7. Evaluate hypotheses, 8. Implement control measures, 9. Communicate findings, 10. Continue surveillance.

Case definition requires three components: Person, Place, Time. Epidemic curves are created during step 6 and can be point source, continuous source, or propagated. Control measures can be implemented at any stage of the investigation.

**Q:** What additional precautions are needed for airborne infections like TB, measles, and varicella?

**A:** N95 mask (fit-tested) and negative pressure room.

Contact precautions (gloves, gown, dedicated equipment) are used for gastroenteritis, scabies, and conjunctivitis. Droplet precautions (mask/respirator with eye protection) are used for mumps, pertussis, and meningococcal disease. Seasonal influenza and COVID-19 require contact + droplet precautions.

**Q:** What are the mandatory reporting requirements for physicians in Ontario?

**A:** Reportable diseases to public health, live births/stillbirths/deaths to Registrar General, child abuse to CAS, gunshot/stab wounds to police, unfit to drive to Ministry of Transportation, abuse of LTC residents to regulatory authority.

Failure to report can result in suspension of license to practice. Self-inflicted knife wounds are NOT reportable. Diseases marked with asterisk require immediate reporting by phone/fax; others can be reported the next working day. Each province has similar but specific legislation.

**Q:** What is the Walkerton tragedy and its public health significance?

**A:** In May 2000, Walkerton, Ontario's drinking water was contaminated with E. coli O157:H7 and Campylobacter jejuni. Over 2300 people became ill, 27 developed hemolytic uremic syndrome, and 7 died.

This event highlighted the importance of water safety regulation and public health infrastructure. Water biological hazards include E. coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas, Shigella, Giardia, and Cryptosporidium. Indigenous peoples, Black Nova Scotians, and rural Canadians are at higher risk for waterborne disease.

**Q:** What is the Stages of Change (Transtheoretical) Model?

**A:** 1. Precontemplation (not considering change), 2. Contemplation (considering change within 6 months), 3. Preparation (planning change within 30 days), 4. Action (actively making change), 5. Maintenance (sustaining change), with potential Relapse at any stage.

This model provides a framework for facilitating behaviour change and is applied to health behaviours such as smoking cessation. Behaviour results from predisposing factors (knowledge, attitudes), enabling factors (skills, supports), and reinforcing factors (HCPs, social context).

**Q:** What is the difference between efficacy, effectiveness, and efficiency in healthcare?

**A:** Efficacy: beneficial result under ideal conditions (e.g., RCT). Effectiveness: benefit under usual clinical care conditions. Efficiency: economy of an intervention with known effectiveness (optimal use of resources).

A treatment may have high efficacy in trials but lower effectiveness in practice due to poor adherence, limited access, or suboptimal administration. Efficiency considers cost, time, and personnel in addition to the known effectiveness of an intervention.

**Q:** What is the Precede-Proceed model in health program planning?

**A:** PRECEDE: Phase 1-4 identify desired results, health issues, predisposing/enabling/reinforcing factors, and administrative/policy factors. PROCEED: Phase 5-8 cover implementation, process evaluation, impact evaluation, and outcome evaluation.

This is a comprehensive tool for designing, implementing, and evaluating health interventions. It moves from identifying the ultimate desired result backward to determining what factors influence the behaviors and conditions that need to change, then forward through implementation and evaluation.

**Q:** What are the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) Calls to Action relevant to healthcare?

**A:** The TRC Final Report (2015) includes 94 Calls to Action, some directed to HCPs and medical schools, calling for culturally safe care, addressing intergenerational trauma from residential schools, and physicians playing an active role in reconciliation with Indigenous peoples.

Residential schools (1870s-1996) caused lasting physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual harm. Survivors report poorer health, increased chronic/infectious diseases, depression, substance use, and suicide. These outcomes extend to subsequent generations through intergenerational trauma.

**Q:** What is environmental racism?

**A:** The deliberate and disproportionate development of environmental hazards and toxic facilities near communities without a strong voice (Indigenous, immigrant, racialized groups, lower SES).

Examples in Canada include oil and gas industries on Indigenous lands without permission, lack of access to potable water (e.g., Attawapiskat), and mercury contamination at Grassy Narrows. Colonial and racist practices contribute to diminished political power to advocate against these hazards.

**Q:** What are the key differences between a cohort study and a case-control study?

**A:** Cohort: starts with exposed/unexposed groups, follows forward for outcomes, calculates relative risk. Case-control: starts with cases/controls (outcome), looks back at exposures, calculates odds ratio. Cohort is better for common outcomes; case-control is better for rare diseases.

Cohort studies provide stronger evidence for causation and can assess multiple outcomes. Case-control studies are less costly and time-consuming but are subject to recall bias and selection bias. Neither is randomized, so confounding remains a concern in both designs.

**Q:** What is population health, as defined in public health medicine?

**A:** Population health refers to the health of defined groups of people, their health determinants, trends in health, and health inequalities. It is influenced by physical, biological, social, environmental, and economic factors; personal health behaviours; and access to and quality of healthcare services. It accounts for socioeconomic, policy, and historical issues.

Population health is distinct from individual clinical care — it focuses on groups rather than individual patients. This perspective informs public health interventions, policy decisions, and health equity initiatives.

**Q:** What is the definition of public health?

**A:** An organized effort by society to promote, protect, improve, and when necessary, restore the health of individuals, specified groups, or the entire population. It is a combination of sciences, skills, and values that function through collective societal activities involving programs, services, and institutions aimed at protecting and improving population health.

Public health in Canada operates at federal (PHAC), provincial (e.g., Public Health Ontario), and municipal/local levels. It is usually organized and funded separately from the healthcare system.

**Q:** What are the six core functions/essential activities of public health in Canada?

**A:** 1) Health protection: measures for population-level health risk management through regulation (e.g., safe water/food supply). 2) Health surveillance. 3) Health promotion. 4) Disease prevention. 5) Health emergency preparedness and response. 6) Population health assessment.

These core functions guide the work of all public health units in Canada. The Public Health Agency of Canada (federal), provincial agencies, and local public health units all carry out these functions at their respective levels.

**Q:** What is epidemiology?

**A:** The study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

Epidemiology is the foundational science of public health. It describes 'who gets disease, where, when, and why' and uses this to develop and evaluate interventions. Key measures include incidence, prevalence, relative risk, and odds ratios.

**Q:** What is the mission of the Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC)?

**A:** To promote and protect the health of Canadians through leadership, partnership, innovation, preparedness, and action in public health.

PHAC operates at the federal level and coordinates national public health strategies, emergency preparedness, infectious disease surveillance, and health promotion. Provincial agencies (e.g., Public Health Ontario) work in parallel at the provincial level.

**Q:** What are the key acronyms and their definitions for public health epidemiology measures: AR, RR, OR, NNT, NNH, PPV, NPV?

**A:** AR: attributable risk. RR: relative risk. OR: odds ratio. NNT: number needed to treat. NNH: number needed to harm. PPV: positive predictive value. NPV: negative predictive value.

These measures are foundational for interpreting epidemiological studies and clinical research. RR is used in cohort studies; OR is used in case-control studies. NNT and NNH quantify clinical significance of treatment effects.

**Q:** What are the 5 main reasons that certain diseases are mandatorily reportable in Canada?

**A:** 1. To identify and control an outbreak
2. To prevent spread if the disease presents a significant threat to individuals or a subset of the population (e.g., Lassa Fever)
3. If the disease is preventable with immunization (e.g., polio, diphtheria, congenital rubella)
4. If infected individuals require education, treatment, and/or partner notification (e.g., gonorrhea, TB)
5. Surveillance (to monitor disease trends over time)

In Canada, mandatory disease reporting is a legal obligation under provincial law and is also outlined by provincial physician licensing Colleges (e.g., CPSO). Failure to report can result in suspension of a license to practice. The rationale encompasses outbreak control, prevention, immunization-preventable disease tracking, contact tracing, and ongoing epidemiological surveillance.

**A:** Diseases marked with * in the reportable disease list must be reported immediately. Key examples include: Anthrax, Botulism, Brucellosis, Cholera, COVID-19 (novel coronavirus including SARS and MERS), Diphtheria, Ebola virus disease, Marburg virus disease, Lassa Fever, Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome, Hemorrhagic fevers, Meningococcal disease (invasive), Plague, Poliomyelitis, Q Fever, Rabies, SARS, Smallpox and other Orthopoxviruses (including Monkeypox), Tuberculosis, Tularemia. Also Influenza in institutions must be reported immediately.

Other diseases can be reported the next working day by fax, phone, or mail. The immediacy requirement reflects the potential for rapid spread and the need for urgent public health response.

**Q:** A physician fails to report a reportable disease to public health authorities. What is the potential consequence?

**A:** Suspension of the physician's license to practice medicine.

Physician reporting of certain diseases is required by provincial law in Canada and is also outlined by provincial physician licensing Colleges (e.g., CPSO in Ontario). Failure to comply with mandatory reporting obligations can result in disciplinary action including suspension of the license to practice.

**Q:** A physician suspects a patient has been the victim of child abuse. What is the legal obligation in Ontario?

**A:** The physician must report to the local Children's Aid Society (CAS). This obligation applies when there are 'reasonable grounds to suspect' child abuse or neglect, including physical harm, emotional harm, sexual harm, and neglect. There is an ongoing duty to report — if additional reasonable grounds are suspected, a further report to CAS is necessary.

In Ontario, child abuse reporting is mandatory under provincial child welfare legislation. The threshold is 'reasonable grounds to suspect' — not certainty. This is deliberately a low threshold to protect vulnerable children. The ongoing duty to report means each new suspicion triggers a fresh reporting obligation, even if a previous report has already been made.

**Q:** A patient with a gunshot wound presents to the emergency department. What is the physician's reporting obligation? What about self-inflicted stab wounds?

**A:** All patients with gunshot or stab wounds must be reported to the local Police Service as soon as is practical. However, self-inflicted knife wounds are NOT reportable.

In Ontario, gunshot and stab wounds are mandatorily reportable to police, reflecting public safety concerns about interpersonal violence. The exception for self-inflicted knife wounds recognizes that these represent a different public safety context (self-harm rather than violence by another party), and over-reporting could deter patients from seeking care.

**Q:** A physician has a patient with dementia who continues to drive. What is the physician's legal reporting obligation?

**A:** The physician must report to the Provincial Ministry of Transportation. Physicians are legally required to report all patients with a medical condition (e.g., dementia, untreated epilepsy, ophthalmological conditions) that may impede their driving ability. If a physician does not report and the driver gets into an accident, the physician may be held liable.

Fitness-to-drive reporting is a mandatory physician obligation in Ontario (reported to the Ministry of Transportation). This protects both the driver and the public. Physicians can be held liable for failure to report if the patient subsequently causes a motor vehicle accident. This creates tension with patient confidentiality, but public safety takes precedence.

**Q:** Under what circumstances must a physician contact a coroner or police regarding a patient's death?

**A:** Physicians must contact a coroner or police if the patient is suspected to have died:
1. From violence, misadventure, negligence, misconduct, or malpractice
2. By unfair means
3. During pregnancy or postpartum from circumstances attributed to pregnancy
4. Suddenly and unexpectedly
5. From an illness not treated by a legally qualified medical practitioner
6. Under circumstances that may require investigation

In Ontario, while all live and stillbirths must be reported to the Registrar General within 2 business days, deaths that fall outside normal expected death from disease require coroner notification. The coroner's role is to investigate circumstances of death to protect public safety and identify systemic issues. Sudden unexpected death, deaths in pregnancy, and medically unattended deaths are key triggers.

**Q:** What is the mandatory reporting obligation for physicians regarding residents of long-term care homes or retirement homes?

**A:** Any resident suspected of being subject to or at risk of improper or incompetent treatment or care, abuse or neglect, or unlawful conduct (including financial abuse) must be reported IMMEDIATELY to the Registrar of the Retirement Homes Regulatory Authority or Long-Term Care Home Director.

Long-term care and retirement home residents are a vulnerable population with heightened legal protections. The mandatory reporting obligation is immediate and encompasses a broad range of harms including physical abuse, neglect, incompetent care, and financial exploitation. This reflects the power imbalance between residents and care providers.

**Q:** What did the Swedish Two-County Trial demonstrate about mammographic screening?

**A:** At 29 years of follow-up, mammographic screening led to a large significant reduction in breast cancer mortality (relative risk = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.56–0.84). Invitation to mammographic screening leads to a large significant decrease in breast cancer-related mortality.

The Swedish Two-County Trial (published in Radiology 2011) is a landmark randomized trial involving 133,065 women aged 40–74 years. It demonstrated a ~31% relative reduction in breast cancer mortality with mammographic screening over 29 years of follow-up. This trial forms the evidentiary basis for mammography screening programs in Canada.

**Q:** What did the MASS trial demonstrate regarding abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) screening?

**A:** In men screened for AAA by ultrasound, there was a 48% relative reduction in AAA-related mortality (0.46% vs. 0.87% deaths) over 10 years. The cost-effectiveness ratio was £7,600 per life-year gained, with cost-effectiveness increasing over time.

The MASS trial (BMJ 2009) demonstrated sustained mortality benefit for men screened for AAA by ultrasound at 10 years of follow-up. This evidence supports one-time ultrasound screening for AAA in men aged 65–75 years who have ever smoked, which is recommended in several Canadian guidelines.

**Q:** What did the COLONPREV trial show comparing colonoscopy vs. FIT testing for colorectal cancer screening?

**A:** CRC incidence did not differ between colonoscopy and FIT groups (0.1% in each group; OR 0.99, p=0.99). More advanced adenomas were detected with colonoscopy (1.9% vs. 0.9%). Participation was higher in the FIT group (34.2% vs. 24.6% for colonoscopy). Conclusion: Colonoscopy and FIT have equivalent CRC detection rates, but participation is higher with FIT.

The COLONPREV trial (NEJM 2012) compared one-time colonoscopy to FIT every 2 years in adults aged 50–69 at average CRC risk. The higher adenoma detection with colonoscopy did not translate to lower CRC incidence in this study. The higher participation rate with FIT is clinically important — a test with better compliance may offer superior population-level benefit even if per-test sensitivity is lower.

**Q:** What were the key results of the NLST trial regarding lung cancer screening with low-dose CT?

**A:** In high-risk patients (heavy smokers), low-dose CT screening resulted in a 20% relative reduction in lung cancer mortality compared to chest X-ray (95% CI 6.8–26.7; p=0.004). Positive screening test rates were 24.2% (CT) vs. 6.9% (CXR). Lung cancer incidence: 645 vs. 572 cases per 100,000 person-years in CT vs. CXR groups.

The NLST trial (NEJM 2011) enrolled 53,454 high-risk patients (heavy smokers aged 55–74) randomized to annual low-dose CT vs. CXR for 3 years. The 20% relative mortality reduction with LDCT is the basis for lung cancer screening recommendations in Canada (e.g., for patients aged 55–74 with ≥30 pack-year smoking history). The high false-positive rate (24.2%) with CT is an important limitation requiring careful patient selection.

**Q:** What did the VAXICOL trial show about influenza vaccination of nursing home staff?

**A:** Staff vaccination rates were 69.9% (vaccination arm) vs. 31.8% (control arm). A strong inverse correlation between staff vaccination coverage and all-cause mortality of residents was found (correlation coefficient = -0.42, p=0.007). Conclusion: Vaccinating nursing home staff against influenza reduces all-cause mortality of residents.

The VAXICOL trial (JAGS 2009) used a cluster-randomized design across 40 nursing homes. The finding that vaccinating STAFF reduces RESIDENT mortality demonstrates the concept of indirect protection (herd immunity). This supports mandatory or strongly encouraged influenza vaccination programs for healthcare workers in long-term care settings — a recommendation adopted in many Canadian provinces.

**Q:** What was the efficacy of the BNT162b2 mRNA COVID-19 vaccine in the pivotal RCT?

**A:** 95% efficacy in preventing COVID-19 in people aged 16 and older (8 cases in vaccine group vs. 162 in placebo group; 95% CI: 90.3–97.6). Serious adverse events were low and comparable between groups.

The BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) vaccine trial (NEJM 2020, NCT04368728) enrolled 43,548 participants. The 95% efficacy result was the basis for emergency authorization and subsequent approval. The mRNA vaccine platform represents a novel approach using lipid nanoparticle-delivered mRNA encoding the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein.

**Q:** A physician has a patient who is a flight crew member or air traffic controller with a medical condition affecting aviation safety. What is the reporting obligation?

**A:** The physician must report to the Federal Ministry of Transportation (NOT provincial). This applies to all patients believed to be flight crew members or air traffic controllers with a medical or optometric condition that is likely to constitute a hazard to aviation safety.

Aviation safety reporting is a FEDERAL obligation (unlike driving fitness which is provincial). This distinction is tested — driving = provincial Ministry of Transportation; flying = federal Ministry of Transportation. The threshold is any condition 'likely to constitute a hazard to aviation safety,' reflecting the catastrophic public safety implications of impaired pilot performance.

**Q:** What are the tetanus prophylaxis guidelines for wound management?

**A:** Unknown/fewer than 3 doses: give Tdap or Td for ALL wounds; give TIG only for 'all other wounds' (not clean minor). 3 or more doses: No Td for clean minor wounds; No Td for other wounds unless >5 years since last dose; No TIG needed. Tdap preferred over Td in adults who have never received Tdap.

Tetanus toxoid (Td/Tdap) boosts immunity; TIG provides immediate passive protection. The distinction matters clinically: a fully vaccinated patient presenting with a major contaminated wound still does not need TIG. Both agents are safe in pregnancy. Single-antigen tetanus toxoid (TT) is no longer available in the US.

## Public Health Epidemiology

**Q:** What is the Standardized Mortality Ratio (SMR) and why is standardization important in comparing mortality rates?

**A:** SMR = observed number of deaths / expected number of deaths. Standardization prevents bias when comparing crude rates from dissimilar populations (e.g., crude death rates over decades are not comparable because the population age distribution has changed over time).

Without standardization, differences in underlying population demographics (particularly age) can make crude mortality rates misleading. The SMR adjusts for these differences, allowing valid comparison across groups or time periods.

**Q:** Define Life Expectancy, Crude Death Rate, Infant Mortality Rate (IMR), and Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR).

**A:** Life Expectancy: expected years to be lived by a newborn based on age-specific mortality rates at a selected time. Crude Death Rate: mortality from all causes per 1000 in the population. IMR: deaths among children <1 yr per 1000 live births per year. MMR: deaths of women during pregnancy and due to puerperal causes per 1000 live births in the same year.

These are core mortality measures used in public health surveillance. IMR is especially sensitive and often used as a proxy for overall healthcare quality and socioeconomic development in a population.

**Q:** What is the difference between PYLL, DALY, and QALY as measures of disease burden?

**A:** PYLL (Potential Years of Life Lost): difference between actual age at death and expected age at death; gives increased weighting to mortality at younger ages. DALY (Disability-Adjusted Life Year): years lost to premature mortality + years lived with disability (0 = perfect health, 1 = death). QALY (Quality-Adjusted Life Year): years of life weighted by quality/utility (0 = death, 1 = perfect health); used as an economic measure to assess value of medical interventions.

DALYs are used by WHO for global burden of disease comparisons and account for both premature death and disability. QALYs are more often used in health economic evaluations (cost-utility analysis) and can be negative for states considered worse than death.

**Q:** What are the top causes of DALYs in Canada (2019)?

**A:** 1. Neoplasms, 2. Cardiovascular diseases, 3. Musculoskeletal disorders, followed by mental disorders, neurological disorders, other non-communicable diseases, unintentional injuries, chronic respiratory diseases, and substance use disorders.

The burden of disease in Canada is dominated by non-communicable diseases. Understanding the top contributors to DALYs informs health policy priorities and resource allocation in Canada.

**Q:** What is the difference between probability and non-probability sampling? Give examples of each.

**A:** Probability sampling: all individuals have a known chance of selection. Types: simple random (equal chance), systematic random (every nth person), stratified random (randomly selected from subgroups), cluster (groups selected rather than individuals). Non-probability sampling: chance of selection not known. Types: convenience (accessible persons), snowball (existing subjects recruit others — useful for hidden populations like persons who inject drugs).

Probability sampling reduces selection bias and allows statistical inference to the population. Non-probability sampling is quicker and cheaper but limits generalizability. Snowball sampling is valuable for studying stigmatized or hard-to-reach populations.

**Q:** What is selection bias? Describe three types including Berkson's bias.

**A:** Selection bias: a systematic error in recruitment or retention of study participants. Types: (1) Berkson's bias — in case-control studies using hospitalized controls, controls may not represent the general population due to complexity of their hospital admission. (2) Non-response bias — volunteers may be healthier than non-participants. (3) Loss to follow-up bias — dropout rates differ between groups and dropouts differ from completers.

Selection bias threatens the internal validity of a study by making the comparison group unrepresentative. Berkson's bias is classically tested because hospitalized controls share risk factors for hospitalization that may correlate with the exposure of interest.

**Q:** What is information bias? Describe recall bias and interviewer bias.

**A:** Information bias: the way information is collected about study participants is inadequate. Recall bias: individuals with disease are more likely to incorrectly recall/believe they were exposed to a possible risk factor than those without disease (common in case-control studies). Interviewer bias: interviewers are unblinded to outcome status, and this knowledge biases their behaviour.

Information bias distorts measured associations. Recall bias is particularly important in case-control studies because cases are more motivated to identify exposures. Blinding interviewers to case/control status minimizes interviewer bias.

**Q:** What is a confounder and how can confounding be minimized in a study?

**A:** A confounder is a variable related to both the exposure and outcome but is not in the causal pathway. It distorts the estimated effect of an exposure. Confounding can be minimized by: randomization, stratification, matching, and regression modelling — usually by balancing confounders equally across comparison groups rather than eliminating their presence.

Late maternal age confounding the relationship between high birth order and Down syndrome is a classic example. Randomization is the only method that can control for both known and unknown confounders simultaneously.

**Q:** Define sensitivity and specificity and their formulas.

**A:** Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN): proportion of people WITH disease who have a POSITIVE test. Specificity = TN/(TN+FP): proportion of people WITHOUT disease who have a NEGATIVE test. Sensitivity and specificity are characteristics of the test and do not depend on disease prevalence.

The mnemonic SPIN (SPecific test rules IN disease — very few false positives) and SNOUT (SENsitive test rules OUT disease — very few false negatives) help with clinical interpretation. Unlike PPV/NPV, sensitivity and specificity are not affected by prevalence.

**Q:** What is the SPIN/SNOUT mnemonic in clinical test interpretation?

**A:** SPIN: use a SPecific test to rule IN a diagnosis. A specific test has very few false positives — if the test is positive, it is likely a true positive. SNOUT: use a SENsitive test to rule OUT a diagnosis. A sensitive test has very few false negatives — if the test is negative, it is likely a true negative.

These mnemonics are widely used in clinical reasoning. For example, a highly sensitive test like D-dimer is used to rule out PE (SNOUT), whereas a highly specific test like biopsy is used to confirm/rule in disease (SPIN).

**Q:** What are Positive Predictive Value (PPV) and Negative Predictive Value (NPV), and what do they depend on?

**A:** PPV = TP/(TP+FP): proportion of people with a positive test who have the disease. NPV = TN/(TN+FN): proportion of people with a negative test who are free of disease. PPV and NPV depend on disease PREVALENCE in the population, unlike sensitivity and specificity.

In a low-prevalence population, even a highly specific test will have a low PPV because there are many more true negatives than true positives. This is crucial when interpreting screening test results in different clinical settings.

**Q:** What is the Likelihood Ratio (LR+) and LR-, and what are their formulas?

**A:** LR+ = Sensitivity / (1−Specificity) = [TP/(TP+FN)] / [FP/(TN+FP)]: how much the probability of disease INCREASES if the test is positive. LR− = (1−Sensitivity) / Specificity = [FN/(TP+FN)] / [TN/(TN+FP)]: how much the probability of disease DECREASES if the test is negative. LR depends on test characteristics (not prevalence).

Post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR. A high LR+ (>10) significantly increases disease probability; a low LR− (<0.1) significantly decreases it. LR is useful because it is prevalence-independent, unlike PPV/NPV.

**Q:** What is pre-test probability and how does it relate to post-test probability?

**A:** Pre-test probability: probability that a patient has a given disease before test results are known. Post-test probability: revised probability after a test result. Post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR+ (for positive test) or × LR− (for negative test). Recall: odds = probability/(1−probability). After each test, post-test probability becomes the new pre-test probability for subsequent investigations.

Clinical reasoning sequentially updates disease probability. Starting from clinical impression (pre-test probability), test results via LR modify the probability. This forms the basis for Bayesian reasoning in medicine, illustrated by Fagan's nomogram.

**Q:** What is the difference between efficacy, effectiveness, and efficiency of an intervention?

**A:** Efficacy: beneficial result under IDEAL conditions (e.g., RCT with high adherence, close monitoring). Effectiveness: benefit under USUAL conditions of clinical care — considers both efficacy and real-world impact (access, adherence, proper administration). Efficiency: economy of an intervention with known effectiveness — considers optimal use of resources (cost, time, personnel).

A vaccine may be 95% efficacious in an RCT (ideal) but only 70% effective in the real world due to cold chain failures or low uptake. Efficiency goes further to weigh cost-effectiveness, which is critical for healthcare resource allocation.

**Q:** What is Number Needed to Treat (NNT), how is it calculated, and what factors influence its interpretation?

**A:** NNT = 1/ARR (absolute risk reduction). ARR = CER − EER (control event rate minus experimental event rate). It represents the number of patients who need to be treated to achieve one additional favourable outcome. Interpretation depends on: cost, feasibility, severity of outcome (a higher NNT is acceptable when preventing death vs. a minor outcome).

Example: if 40% die on drug A vs. 50% on placebo, ARR = 10%, NNT = 1/0.1 = 10. Do not be swayed by large relative risk reductions alone — the NNT and absolute risk reduction provide a clinically meaningful picture. Find NNT summaries at thennt.com.

**Q:** What is Number Needed to Harm (NNH)?

**A:** NNH: the number of patients who, if they received the experimental treatment, would lead to one additional patient being harmed compared with the control treatment. It is calculated as 1/absolute risk increase.

NNH is the harm-equivalent of NNT. Comparing NNT and NNH helps clinicians weigh therapeutic benefit against potential harm. A treatment with NNT of 20 and NNH of 100 is generally favourable.

**Q:** Why can a large relative risk (RR) be misleading? How does absolute risk reduction (ARR) provide additional context?

**A:** A large RR can appear impressive even when the absolute risk difference is tiny. For example, reducing an event from 0.10% to 0.05% in a population gives a RR of 50% (relative), but ARR is only 0.05% — clinically unimpressive. ARR and NNT provide clinically meaningful estimates of benefit.

This distinction is critical in evidence-based medicine. Pharmaceutical marketing often highlights RR to make modest effects appear dramatic. Clinicians should always calculate ARR (and NNT) to properly assess the value of an intervention.

**Q:** Compare qualitative vs. quantitative study designs in terms of approach, purpose, and output.

**A:** Qualitative: bottom-up, generates hypotheses (Why? What does it mean?), observation → pattern → hypothesis → theory, narrative rich contextual data from small samples. Quantitative: top-down, tests hypotheses (What? How much?), theory → hypothesis → observation → confirmation, numeric frequency/severity/associations from large samples.

Qualitative methods (e.g., interviews, focus groups) are used to explore meanings and generate theory. Quantitative methods (e.g., RCTs, cohort studies) provide numerical evidence to test hypotheses. Both are complementary in health research.

**Q:** What is an ecological study design, what is its main advantage, and what is its key limitation?

**A:** Ecological study: units of analysis are populations or groups rather than individuals. Advantage: quick, easy, uses readily available data, generates hypotheses. Key limitation: ecological fallacy — incorrect inference from group-level data to individuals (e.g., countries with higher red wine consumption have lower CVD rates, but this does not mean individuals who drink red wine have lower CVD risk).

The ecological fallacy is a fundamental pitfall of population-level data. Ecological studies can identify associations for hypothesis generation but cannot establish causation at the individual level, making them low in the evidence hierarchy.

**Q:** Compare cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort study designs: definition, advantages, and disadvantages.

**A:** Cross-sectional: exposure and outcome measured at one point in time. Advantage: quick, cheap, estimates prevalence. Disadvantage: cannot determine temporal relationship or causation. Case-control: samples based on outcome (cases vs. controls), assesses past exposure retrospectively. Advantage: good for rare diseases and long latency; measures OR. Disadvantage: recall bias, selection bias, can only measure one outcome. Cohort: samples based on exposure, follows forward for outcomes. Advantage: can assess multiple outcomes, provides incidence/RR/AR, stronger causal evidence. Disadvantage: costly, time-consuming, confounding, loss to follow-up.

Richard Doll's case-control study linked tobacco to lung cancer; the Framingham Heart Study is a classic cohort study. Knowing which study design produces which measure of association (OR vs. RR) is essential for MCCQE1.

**Q:** For a case-control study: what measure of association is calculated, and what is the formula for the odds ratio (OR)?

**A:** Measure of association: Odds Ratio (OR). Formula: OR = (A×D)/(B×C), where A = exposed cases, B = unexposed cases, C = exposed controls, D = unexposed controls in a 2×2 table.

The OR approximates the RR when disease prevalence is low (<10%). Case-control studies are retrospective — cases are identified first, then controls are selected to represent the general population. Matching controls to cases on key variables reduces confounding.

**Q:** What is a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)? Why is it the gold standard for evidence?

**A:** RCT: participants randomly allocated to intervention or control groups; outcomes compared after follow-up. Gold standard because: randomization minimizes confounding (including unmeasured confounders) and self-selection bias; allows prospective assessment; minimizes threats to validity with sufficient sample size. Blinding (single/double/triple) further reduces bias.

Randomization is unique among study designs in its ability to control for both measured and unmeasured confounders. RCTs form the basis of evidence-based medicine. Limitations include ethical constraints, poor generalizability, cost, and difficulty studying rare events.

**Q:** What are the advantages and disadvantages of randomized controlled trials (RCTs)?

**A:** Advantages: gold standard for causation, minimizes confounding and self-selection bias, prospective assessment, strong internal validity. Disadvantages: some exposures cannot be randomized (ethical/feasibility issues), difficult to study rare events (requires large samples), costly, contamination/co-intervention/loss to follow-up can limit inferences, poor generalizability if trial participants are healthier than the average patient.

Despite being the gold standard, RCTs are often not feasible for studying harmful exposures (e.g., smoking), rare diseases, or conditions requiring very long follow-up. Pragmatic RCTs attempt to improve generalizability by using broader inclusion criteria.

**Q:** What is the difference between per-protocol (PP) analysis and intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis in RCTs?

**A:** Per-protocol (PP) analysis: only patients who complete the entire study are counted in results. Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis: groups are analyzed exactly as they existed upon randomization — includes all patients, even those who did not complete the study.

ITT analysis preserves the benefits of randomization and provides a conservative estimate of treatment efficacy, reflecting real-world adherence. PP analysis may overestimate efficacy by excluding non-compliant patients. ITT is preferred to avoid bias from differential dropout.

**Q:** What is a meta-analysis? What are its advantages and disadvantages?

**A:** Meta-analysis: statistical aggregation of all relevant studies addressing the same research question to increase statistical precision. Advantages: overcomes reduced power of small studies; can answer subgroup questions. Disadvantages: studies may be heterogeneous (different populations, exposures, outcomes), making combination inappropriate; publication bias (negative studies less likely to be published) can skew results toward finding an effect.

Meta-analyses sit near the top of the evidence pyramid. Publication bias is a major concern — tools like funnel plots can help detect it. Heterogeneity can be assessed statistically (e.g., I² statistic). The Cochrane Library is the gold-standard source for meta-analyses.

**Q:** What is the difference between a systematic review, a narrative review, and a scoping review?

**A:** Systematic review: rigorous synthesis aimed at a specific question with explicit methodology and appraisal to inform practice/policy. Narrative review: less rigorous, provides overview of general or specific topic by identifying patterns and trends; limited for concrete clinical guidance. Scoping review: maps key concepts and identifies types of evidence and knowledge gaps for a given topic; does not produce a critically appraised answer to a specific clinical question.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are at the top of the evidence pyramid. Scoping reviews are used when the field is emerging and it is unclear what specific questions can be answered. Narrative reviews are useful for education but prone to author bias.

**Q:** Describe the normal distribution. What percentage of observations fall within 1 and 2 standard deviations of the mean?

**A:** Normal (Gaussian) distribution: bell-shaped, symmetric. Mean = median = mode. 68% of observations fall within 1 SD of the mean; 95% of observations fall within 2 SDs of the mean. For skewed distributions, median is a better measure of central tendency than mean.

Understanding the normal distribution is fundamental to interpreting confidence intervals and p-values. In a negatively skewed distribution, the mean is pulled left of the median. In positively skewed data, the mean is pulled right.

**Q:** What are the 4 levels of measurement in quantitative data?

**A:** 1. Categorical (nominal): blood type, marital status — no order. 2. Ordinal: ordered categories (low, medium, high) — differences not meaningful. 3. Interval: ordered with meaningful differences but no true zero (°C, time of day). 4. Ratio: ordered, meaningful differences, and true zero (serum cholesterol, hemoglobin, age).

The level of measurement determines which statistical tests are appropriate. Parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) require at least interval data; non-parametric tests are used for ordinal or non-normal data. Confusing levels of measurement is a common analysis error.

**Q:** What is the difference between Type I (alpha) error and Type II (beta) error in statistical testing?

**A:** Type I (alpha) error: null hypothesis is FALSELY REJECTED — concluding an intervention is effective when it is not (false positive). Probability = p-value. Type II (beta) error: null hypothesis is FALSELY ACCEPTED — concluding an intervention is ineffective when it is (false negative). Power = 1 − beta = probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis.

Studies are typically designed to minimize Type I error (usually alpha = 0.05). By convention, a higher level of beta error is accepted (often 0.20). Increasing sample size reduces both error types and increases power.

**Q:** What is statistical power and how is it affected by sample size?

**A:** Power = 1 − beta = probability of correctly rejecting a null hypothesis when it is false (i.e., finding a true difference when one exists). Power increases with increasing sample size. Power decreases with smaller effect sizes and greater variability.

A study with low power (< 80%) risks a Type II error — missing a true treatment effect. Power calculations are performed before a study to determine the sample size needed to detect a clinically meaningful effect at a given significance level (alpha) with adequate power (typically ≥ 80%).

**Q:** What is a confidence interval (CI), and how should a 95% CI be interpreted?

**A:** A 95% CI provides a range of values that would contain the true population parameter in 95 out of 100 repetitions of the study. A wider CI implies less precision; a narrower CI implies more precision. Increasing sample size narrows the CI (increases precision).

CI and p-value are complementary. If the 95% CI for a RR or OR does not cross 1.0, the result is statistically significant at p < 0.05. Unlike p-values, CIs also convey the magnitude and direction of the effect, making them more clinically informative.

**Q:** What is the difference between statistical significance and clinical significance?

**A:** Statistical significance: probability that the association is due to random chance; usually set at p < 0.05. Clinical significance: measure of clinical usefulness — a result can be statistically significant but clinically meaningless (e.g., 1 mmHg BP reduction). Clinical significance depends on cost, availability, adherence, side effects, and patient-important outcomes.

With very large sample sizes, tiny and clinically irrelevant differences can become statistically significant. Clinicians must always interpret results in terms of both statistical AND clinical significance to make sound decisions.

**Q:** What is the difference between validity and reliability (of a measurement tool)?

**A:** Validity (accuracy): how closely a measurement reflects the entity it claims to measure. Reliability (precision): how consistent multiple measurements are when the underlying subject has not changed. Types of reliability: inter-rater reliability (different observers at same time) and test-retest reliability (same observer under different conditions). A test can be reliable without being valid.

The classic analogy is archery: tight grouping = reliable; hitting the bullseye = valid. A scale that consistently reads 5 kg too heavy is reliable but not valid. Internal validity = findings truly represent the study population; external validity (generalizability) = findings apply to other populations.

**Q:** When should you use a Chi-squared test vs. ANOVA vs. t-test?

**A:** Chi-squared test: tests whether two categorical variables are independent (e.g., family history of breast cancer and having breast cancer). ANOVA: compares means of a continuous outcome across 2+ groups (e.g., BP across 3 towns). Two-sample t-test (Z-test): compares means of a continuous outcome between 2 groups (e.g., average BP between men and women).

Choosing the right statistical test depends on the type of dependent and independent variables and the number of groups. Chi-squared requires expected cell counts ≥5. ANOVA requires normally distributed data and equal variances. Using the wrong test is a common exam pitfall.

**Q:** What is the difference between linear regression and logistic regression?

**A:** Linear regression: models the relationship between a continuous dependent variable and one or more explanatory variables. Logistic regression: models the relationship between a categorical (usually dichotomous) dependent variable and one or more explanatory variables. Logistic regression produces odds ratios; linear regression produces coefficients (slopes).

Linear regression assumes a linear relationship and normally distributed errors; logistic regression assumes linearity on the logit scale. Both require independent observations and no highly influential outliers. Logistic regression is commonly used in epidemiology for binary outcomes (e.g., disease vs. no disease).

**Q:** List the 9 Bradford Hill criteria for causation.

**A:** 1. Strength of association, 2. Consistency (across populations/study designs), 3. Specificity (unique exposure-outcome link), 4. Temporal relationship (exposure precedes outcome), 5. Biological gradient (dose-response), 6. Biological plausibility, 7. Coherence (consistent with current knowledge), 8. Experimental evidence (improvement with removal of exposure), 9. Analogy (similar associations exist).

Not all 9 criteria must be fulfilled; temporal relationship is the most essential — exposure MUST precede outcome. Modern EBM emphasizes experimental evidence (RCTs) as superior. The criteria are used when a causal relationship is proposed from observational data.

**Q:** What are the five levels of evidence used in guideline/consensus statement classifications?

**A:** Level I: RCTs or meta-analyses of RCTs — low risk of FP/FN. Level II: small RCTs — may show positive trends but non-significant, or high FN risk. Level III: non-randomized controlled, cohort, case series, case-control, or cross-sectional studies. Level IV: expert opinion/consensus conferences/guidelines. Level V: opinion of guideline authors based on experience/knowledge.

These levels reflect the nature of the evidence, not direct quality assessments. RCT-based evidence (Level I) is most credible, but Level III gains credibility with consistent findings across multiple studies. Level IV/V guides decisions when published evidence is absent.

**Q:** How do you calculate the relative risk (RR) and attributable risk (AR) in a cohort study?

**A:** Relative Risk (RR) = (Incidence in exposed) / (Incidence in unexposed) = [A/(A+B)] / [C/(C+D)]. Attributable Risk (AR) = Incidence in exposed − Incidence in unexposed = A/(A+B) − C/(C+D). AR represents the rate of disease in exposed individuals that can be attributed to the exposure.

RR >1 indicates increased risk in exposed; RR <1 indicates protection. AR is important for public health — it estimates how much disease could be prevented by eliminating the exposure. Cohort studies are the only observational design that directly provides RR.

**Q:** What is Pearson's correlation coefficient? What do values of -1, 0, and +1 represent?

**A:** Pearson's correlation coefficient assesses the strength of the linear relationship between two continuous variables. Values range from -1 (perfect negative association) to +1 (perfect positive association); 0 indicates no linear relationship. It requires both variables to be continuous and normally distributed.

Pearson's r is used for interval/ratio data. Spearman's rank correlation is used for ordinal or non-normal data. Correlation does not imply causation — a strong correlation may reflect confounding, reverse causation, or coincidence.

**Q:** What is the three-step critical appraisal framework for evaluating a research study?

**A:** A. Are the results valid? (study design, bias, randomization, blinding). B. What are the results? (treatment effect size, confidence intervals, power, precision). C. Will the results help my patients? (clinical significance, applicability, all outcomes considered, benefits vs. harm and cost).

Critical appraisal is the cornerstone of evidence-based medicine. Reading a study requires systematic evaluation — even a well-conducted RCT may not apply to a clinician's patient population if external validity is poor. This 3-step framework is widely used in Canadian medical education.

**Q:** A screening test for colon cancer (FOBT) is applied to a population. Of 284 people with advanced neoplasia, 68 test positive. Of 2381 people without advanced neoplasia, 2234 test negative. Calculate sensitivity, specificity, and NPV.

**A:** Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN) = 68/284 = 23.9%. Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) = 2234/2381 = 93.8%. NPV = TN/(TN+FN) = 2234/(2234+216) = 91.2% (approximately). Note: 216 = 284−68 = false negatives.

FOBT has low sensitivity for detecting advanced neoplasia on a single digital rectal examination sample — most cancers are missed. Its high specificity means a positive result is fairly reliable. NPV is high because the disease prevalence is low in the screened population. This illustrates why sensitivity is critical for screening tests.

**Q:** What increases sample size does NOT affect in a study?

**A:** Increasing sample size does NOT alter the risk of bias or confounding. It DOES increase statistical precision (narrower CIs), and decrease the probability of both Type I and Type II errors.

This is a classic exam distinction. No matter how large the sample size, if the study design has systematic bias (e.g., recall bias, selection bias), increasing N will not correct it. Bias must be addressed through study design, not sample size.

## Public Health Health System

**Q:** What is the difference between quality improvement (QI), quality assurance, quality control, and quality management in healthcare?

**A:** Quality Improvement (QI): evaluates and improves processes; focuses on systems and systematic causes of variation. Quality Assurance: guarantees quality through improvement and attainment of standards; focuses on performance of individuals/institutions. Quality Control: surveys quality of all factors to maintain standards. Continuous Quality Improvement: ongoing refinement via vigilant review and regular incorporation of improvements. Quality Management: combination of assurance, control, and improvement to maintain consistent quality.

The Swiss cheese model (James Reason) illustrates that even multiple safety barriers can have gaps, allowing patient harm to occur. QI initiatives (checklists, standardized protocols) reduce system-level errors rather than blaming individuals.

**Q:** What is the Swiss cheese model of error in patient safety?

**A:** Proposed by James Reason, the Swiss cheese model represents the idea that multiple barriers against failure each have gaps (holes in the cheese). When gaps in multiple layers align, an error can pass through all barriers and result in patient harm. This underpins the systems-based approach to patient safety.

The Swiss cheese model shifts blame from individuals to systems, promoting a culture of safety. Solutions focus on layering redundant defences (e.g., checklists, double-checks, protocols) to reduce the chance that all holes will align simultaneously.

## Public Health Preventive Medicine

**Q:** What is the purpose of a Pareto Chart in quality systems analysis?

**A:** A Pareto Chart is an x vs. y chart where the x-axis lists defect categories and the y-axis shows frequency, with cumulative frequency plotted on the right y-axis. Its purpose is to highlight the most important factors among a large set contributing to defects or poor quality (80/20 principle).

Pareto Charts are a classic quality improvement tool used in health care settings to identify which problems to address first. By visually displaying which defect categories account for the largest proportion of total defects, teams can prioritize interventions where they will have the greatest impact.

**Q:** What is an Ishikawa (Fishbone) Diagram and what is it used for?

**A:** An Ishikawa (Fishbone) Diagram is a systems analysis tool that identifies generic categories of problems (e.g., lack of time, lack of training, equipment, environment, staff, procedures) that contribute to an overall effect or outcome, such as nosocomial infections. It is used to visually map root causes of a quality problem.

The Ishikawa diagram gets its name from its fish-bone shape. In Canadian health care quality improvement, it is used alongside tools like the 5 Whys, Defect Check Sheets, and Pareto Charts as part of Total Quality Management frameworks to identify and remove system-level barriers to quality care.

**Q:** What are the four phases of the PDSA Planning Cycle?

**A:** Plan, Do, Study, Act.

The PDSA cycle is a widely used quality improvement framework in Canadian health systems. It is iterative: first Plan a change, Do (implement) it on a small scale, Study (measure and analyze) the results, then Act (adopt, adapt, or abandon) based on findings. It is often used alongside APIE and the Precede-Proceed Model for health program planning.

**Q:** What are the two phases and eight steps of the Precede-Proceed Model?

**A:** PRECEDE (phases 1-4): Phase 1 – Identify the ultimate desired result; Phase 2 – Identify health issues and their behavioural/environmental determinants; Phase 3 – Identify predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors; Phase 4 – Identify administrative and policy factors. PROCEED (phases 5-8): Phase 5 – Implementation; Phase 6 – Process evaluation; Phase 7 – Impact evaluation; Phase 8 – Outcome evaluation.

The Precede-Proceed Model is a comprehensive tool for designing, implementing, and evaluating health interventions or programs. PRECEDE guides the diagnostic phase while PROCEED guides the implementation and evaluation phases. It is commonly referenced in Canadian public health program planning and the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) and how is it calculated?

**A:** The ICER shows the extra cost of the more expensive intervention compared to the cheaper intervention divided by the difference in health outcome. ICER = (Cost of intervention A – Cost of intervention B) / (Health outcome A – Health outcome B). The denominator is expressed in natural (health) units such as years of life gained or clinical events avoided.

ICER is the key metric in Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA). It quantifies how much more must be spent per unit of additional health benefit gained by the more expensive option. Health technology assessment bodies in Canada (e.g., CADTH) use the ICER to inform drug coverage decisions, with a common threshold around $50,000–$100,000 per QALY.

**Q:** What distinguishes Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA) from Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)?

**A:** CUA is a special case of CEA where the health outcome (benefit) is expressed as a utility-weighted measure that reflects patient preferences, most commonly Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs). In CEA, the denominator is expressed in natural health units (e.g., lives saved, cases avoided).

CUA allows comparison across different disease areas by converting all outcomes to a common utility-based measure (QALY). This is the preferred method for health technology assessment in Canada. By contrast, CEA is condition-specific and uses single clinical outcomes as the denominator.

**Q:** Define: Outbreak, Endemic, Epidemic, and Pandemic.

**A:** Outbreak: incidence of new cases beyond the usual frequency of disease in a population in a given time. Endemic: consistent existence of an infectious agent or disease in a given population or area. Epidemic: a sudden increase in cases above what is usually expected in a community or region. Pandemic: an epidemic that has spread across international or intercontinental boundaries affecting a large number of people (e.g., COVID-19).

These definitions are foundational for MCCQE1. Knowing the distinctions helps apply the correct response framework. SARS is the classic Canadian epidemic example; COVID-19 is the most recent pandemic. The Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) coordinates the national response to epidemics and pandemics.

**Q:** How is the Attack Rate calculated and what does it measure?

**A:** Attack Rate = [(# of new cases of disease) / (initial population size)] × 100%. It measures the proportion of an initially disease-free population that develops the disease over a specified time period.

The attack rate is used during outbreak investigations to quantify how widespread illness is within an exposed group. It is particularly useful in point-source outbreaks (e.g., a contaminated meal at a restaurant). The Secondary Attack Rate measures spread from primary to susceptible contacts during the incubation period.

**Q:** How is the Secondary Attack Rate (SAR) calculated and what does it measure?

**A:** SAR = [(total # of cases – initial # of cases) / (# of susceptible individuals – initial # of cases)] × 100%. It measures the proportion of susceptible contacts who develop disease after exposure to primary cases during the incubation period; it is a measure of infectiousness.

The SAR reflects how easily a pathogen spreads from an infected person to susceptible household or close contacts. High SAR values indicate highly transmissible pathogens. It differs from the Basic Reproduction Number (R0) in that R0 applies to a fully susceptible population under no control measures.

**Q:** What is the Basic Reproduction Number (R0) and what conditions are required for its calculation?

**A:** R0 is the average number of secondary infections that arise from one infection. It can only be calculated in a fully susceptible population (no immunity, no control measures).

R0 is a key measure of pandemic potential. An R0 >1 means the infection will spread; <1 means it will die out. COVID-19 had an R0 of approximately 2-3 for the original strain. R0 is distinct from the effective reproduction number (Re or Rt), which accounts for immunity and interventions in the real population.

**Q:** Define Virulence and Case-Fatality Rate (CFR), and provide their formulas.

**A:** Virulence: a measure of an infectious agent's ability to cause significant sickness = (# of cases that are severely ill or died) / (total # of cases). Case-Fatality Rate (CFR): proportion of individuals with the disease who died as a result of the illness during a specified time period = [(# of cases that died) / (total # of cases)] × 100%. CFR must be clearly differentiated from the mortality rate.

Virulence and CFR are both measures of disease severity but have different denominators and interpretations. CFR only counts deaths among confirmed cases, while mortality rate measures deaths in the whole population. High CFR with low transmissibility (e.g., Ebola) differs from low CFR with high transmissibility (e.g., seasonal influenza) in terms of public health impact.

**Q:** What are the 10 steps of the outbreak investigation approach?

**A:** 1. Identify investigation team and resources (local, provincial, federal); 2. Establish existence of an outbreak; 3. Verify the diagnosis; 4. Define a case (Person, Place, Time); 5. Find cases systematically and create a line list; 6. Perform descriptive epidemiology and develop hypotheses; 7. Evaluate hypotheses and conduct additional studies; 8. Implement control measures; 9. Communicate findings; 10. Continue surveillance.

This 10-step approach is the standard framework taught by public health authorities in Canada and used by PHAC, provincial public health units (e.g., Toronto Public Health), and the Ministry of Health. Steps do not always occur strictly in sequence — for example, control measures (step 8) can be implemented at any stage without waiting for full investigation completion.

**Q:** What study design is preferred in outbreak investigations when all exposed persons can be identified versus when they cannot?

**A:** When all exposed persons can be found: Cohort study. When not all exposed persons can be found: Case-control study.

In outbreak investigations, the choice of analytical study design depends on the feasibility of enumerating the entire exposed population. Cohort studies provide direct relative risk estimates. Case-control studies are more practical for large, diffuse outbreaks where the full exposed cohort cannot be identified.

**Q:** Differentiate a point source epidemic curve from a propagated source epidemic curve.

**A:** Point source: all cases are exposed at the same time/place; epidemic curve shows a sharp rise then fall within one incubation period. Propagated (person-to-person): successive waves of cases, each separated by roughly one incubation period; epidemic curve shows a gradual rise with multiple peaks. Continuous source: ongoing exposure produces a plateau pattern on the epidemic curve.

Reading epidemic curves is a core skill for MCCQE1. The shape of the curve identifies the mode of transmission and guides control measures. A point source (e.g., contaminated food at a banquet) calls for removing the source; a propagated outbreak (e.g., measles) requires interrupting person-to-person transmission through isolation and vaccination.

**Q:** What are the components of Routine Practices in Canadian infection prevention and control (IPAC)?

**A:** Routine Practices include: (1) risk assessment, (2) hand hygiene, (3) personal protective equipment, (4) environmental controls (cleaning, patient placement, engineering controls), and (5) administrative controls (policies, staff education, compliance monitoring). They are recommended by PHAC for use with ALL patients/clients during ALL care in ALL health care settings.

Routine Practices are the Canadian equivalent of Universal Precautions. They apply regardless of a patient's known diagnosis and are the first line of defence against transmission of microorganisms in health care settings. Additional Precautions are layered on top for specific pathogens or presentations.

**Q:** What PPE and room requirements are required for Airborne precautions versus Droplet precautions in Canada?

**A:** Airborne: N95 mask (fit-tested) + negative pressure room. Examples: TB, measles, varicella. Droplet: facial protection (mask or respirator + eye protection) + single room with dedicated toilet/sink; cohorting may be acceptable. Examples: mumps, whooping cough, meningococcal disease.

The distinction between droplet and airborne transmission drives PPE and room requirements. Airborne pathogens (particle size <5 µm) require negative pressure rooms and fit-tested N95 respirators to prevent inhalation. Droplet pathogens (>5 µm) fall rapidly and require standard masks and eye protection but not negative pressure rooms.

**Q:** What PPE and room requirements are required for Contact precautions? Give two clinical examples.

**A:** Contact precautions require: gloves, gown, dedicated equipment, cleaning and disinfection of shared care items, and a single room with dedicated toilet and patient sink. Examples: gastroenteritis (e.g., C. difficile), scabies, conjunctivitis.

Contact precautions prevent transmission via direct or indirect contact with the patient or their environment. They are required when pathogens can be spread by touching contaminated surfaces. In Canadian health care, they are commonly applied for MRSA, VRE, C. difficile, and other healthcare-associated pathogens.

**Q:** What is a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) under the International Health Regulations (IHR)?

**A:** A PHEIC is defined as 'an extraordinary event which is determined to constitute a public health risk to other States through the international spread of disease and to potentially require a coordinated international response.' It is declared by the WHO Director-General based on recommendations from the Emergency Committee. Examples: smallpox, poliomyelitis (wild-type), human influenza (new subtype), COVID-19, SARS, mpox.

The IHR is an international agreement involving 196 nations. A PHEIC declaration triggers international cooperation and temporary recommendations by the WHO. Canadian physicians should understand the PHEIC mechanism as it drives national response protocols through PHAC and influences public health travel advisories.

**Q:** What does the mnemonic CHCHOPD represent in environmental health history-taking?

**A:** C – Community; H – Home; C – Community (second C relates to neighbourhood); H – Hobbies; O – Occupation; P – Personal habits; D – Diet and Drugs. This mnemonic guides clinicians to ask about environmental exposures across multiple domains.

Taking a thorough environmental health history is essential when evaluating patients with potentially environment-related conditions. After identifying a possible hazard using CHCHOPD, the clinician then investigates Time (when symptoms began/exposure began), Place (where symptoms worsen/hazard location), and Person (others with similar symptoms).

**Q:** What are the four steps of the National Academies of Sciences Environmental Risk Assessment framework (1984)?

**A:** 1. Hazard Identification – What is the hazard? 2. Dose-Response – What health outcomes are associated with the hazard and at what doses? 3. Exposure Assessment – Is the patient's exposure sufficient to cause the symptoms? 4. Risk Characterization – Is there a causal relationship given the exposure and dose-response?

This four-step framework (also called the HIRA framework) is used by public health agencies and environmental health practitioners worldwide, including in Canada. It provides a systematic approach to determining whether an environmental exposure is likely responsible for a patient's or community's health effects.

**Q:** What are the four common types of environmental hazards and four main environmental reservoirs?

**A:** Four types of hazards: chemical, biological, physical, and radiation. Four main reservoirs: air, food, water, and soil. Three main routes of exposure: inhalation, ingestion, and absorption (skin).

Understanding the sources, reservoirs, and routes of environmental exposures is fundamental to taking an environmental health history and performing a risk assessment. In Canadian public health, water, air, and soil contamination are regulated across multiple jurisdictional levels (municipal, provincial, federal).

**Q:** What are the health effects of ground-level ozone, carbon monoxide, and sulphur dioxide as air chemical hazards?

**A:** Ground-level ozone: main component of smog; worsens asthma, irritates upper airway. Carbon monoxide: aggravates cardiac disease at low levels; headache, nausea, dizziness at moderate levels; fatal at high levels. Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides: contribute to acid rain and exacerbate breathing difficulties.

These chemical air pollutants are regulated under Canadian federal and provincial legislation. Carbon monoxide poisoning is a medical emergency managed with high-flow O2 or hyperbaric oxygen. Ozone and SO2 are particularly relevant to patients with pre-existing cardiorespiratory conditions during air quality advisories.

**Q:** What is the significance of radon gas as a radiation hazard to indoor air quality?

**A:** Radon is naturally produced by soil containing uranium or radium and can contaminate indoor air. It is associated with approximately 20% of lung cancers in Canada (second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking).

Radon is a colourless, odourless, radioactive gas. Health Canada recommends testing home radon levels and taking action if levels exceed 200 Bq/m³. Risk is highest in basements and poorly ventilated spaces. Radon testing and mitigation (sealing, ventilation) are important preventive measures discussed in Canadian public health guidelines.

**Q:** What was the Walkerton tragedy and what pathogens were responsible?

**A:** In May 2000, the drinking water system in Walkerton, Ontario became contaminated with Escherichia coli O157:H7 and Campylobacter jejuni. Over 2,300 individuals became ill; 27 developed hemolytic uremic syndrome and 7 died.

The Walkerton tragedy is the most significant water contamination disaster in modern Canadian history. It was caused by inadequate water treatment and regulatory oversight. The subsequent Walkerton Inquiry (Justice O'Connor) led to major overhauls of Ontario's Safe Drinking Water Act and reinforced the critical role of public health authorities in water safety regulation.

**Q:** What is the recommended fluoride concentration in municipal water in Canada and what is the benefit?

**A:** The recommended fluoride concentration in municipal water is 0.7 mg/L (ppm). At this concentration, fluoride reduces dental cavities (caries) by 18-40% and is considered one of the greatest public health achievements of the 20th century.

Water fluoridation is a safe, effective, and cost-effective public health intervention endorsed by Health Canada and the Canadian Dental Association. Concerns about harm have been widely debunked. Some Canadian communities (e.g., Calgary) have opted out, resulting in increased rates of dental caries, particularly in children.

**Q:** Which populations are at highest risk of waterborne illness in Canada? Name three waterborne bacterial pathogens.

**A:** Indigenous peoples, Black Nova Scotians, and rural Canadians are at highest risk. Three waterborne bacterial pathogens: Escherichia coli (e.g., Walkerton), Salmonella, Shigella. Waterborne protozoa include Giardia and Cryptosporidium (e.g., North Battleford, SK).

Access to clean water is a recognized social determinant of health. Structural inequities mean that racialized and Indigenous communities disproportionately lack access to safe drinking water in Canada. As of 2020, 61 long-term drinking water advisories remained on reserves. Cryptosporidium is notably resistant to standard chlorination and requires filtration.

**Q:** What are the key food sources and main health effects of Clostridium botulinum and Listeria monocytogenes?

**A:** Clostridium botulinum: unpasteurized honey and canned foods → dizziness, weakness, respiratory failure (and GI symptoms: thirst, nausea, constipation). Listeria monocytogenes: unpasteurized cheeses, prepared salads, cold cuts → listeriosis (nausea, vomiting, fever, headache, rarely meningitis or encephalitis); particularly dangerous in pregnancy.

Both pathogens require specific clinical awareness. Botulism is a medical emergency requiring antitoxin and potentially mechanical ventilation. Listeria is particularly dangerous for pregnant women, neonates, the elderly, and immunocompromised patients. In Canada, listeriosis outbreaks have been linked to deli meats processed in federally regulated facilities.

**Q:** What food sources and health effects are associated with prions (BSE)?

**A:** Prions (bovine spongiform encephalopathy, BSE): source is beef and beef products → Variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans.

BSE ('mad cow disease') caused a major food safety crisis in the UK and Canada in the 1990s–2000s. Variant CJD is a uniformly fatal prion disease with a long incubation period. Canada implemented strict cattle feed bans and surveillance programs in response. Prions are resistant to standard sterilization methods.

**Q:** What are nitrites and sulphites as food chemical hazards and what are their associated health risks?

**A:** Nitrites: highest concentration in cured meats; can be converted in the body to carcinogenic nitrosamines (risk of colorectal cancer). Sulphites: commonly used as food preservatives; associated with sulphite allergy (hives, nausea, anaphylactic shock).

These food additives are regulated by Health Canada under the Food and Drug Regulations. Nitrosamine formation is enhanced in high-temperature cooking of nitrite-cured meats. Sulphite allergy, while uncommon, can be severe and must be considered in patients presenting with allergic reactions after eating dried fruits, wine, or processed foods.

**Q:** What is Environmental Racism and provide two Canadian examples.

**A:** Environmental racism is the deliberate and disproportionate development of environmental hazards and toxic facilities near communities without a strong voice (Indigenous, immigrant, racialized groups, lower SES). Two Canadian examples: (1) Grassy Narrows, Ontario – mercury contamination from a paper mill causing severe neurotoxicity in Indigenous residents; (2) Africville, Nova Scotia – placement of oil plant, tar factory, prison, hospital for infectious diseases, and open garbage dump within a Black community, with denial of basic municipal services.

Environmental racism is a key social determinant of health topic in Canadian public health. Grassy Narrows (mercury poisoning) and Africville (forced relocation of a Black community for industrial use) exemplify how systemic racism intersects with environmental policy. These cases have significant implications for health equity, Indigenous rights, and the UNDRIP.

**Q:** What health effects does climate change contribute to, according to the WHO's projections and Toronto Notes?

**A:** WHO projects 250,000 additional deaths annually by 2030 due to climate change. Health effects include: (1) extreme weather events (injuries, deaths); (2) reduced air quality (respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, allergies); (3) altered vector distribution (increased Lyme disease, West Nile virus, Malaria, Dengue); (4) crop damage (malnutrition, foodborne illness); (5) water supply damage (waterborne diseases, algal blooms); (6) droughts and rising sea levels (poverty, conflict, migration).

Climate change is recognized as the greatest public health threat of the 21st century. Canadian physicians are encouraged to consider climate-related factors in patient presentations (e.g., heat stroke, new vector distribution). Public health responses involve mitigation (reducing greenhouse gases) and adaptation (building resilience in health systems).

**Q:** What are the five phases of disaster response and how does each differ?

**A:** 1. Prevention: actions to stop potential disasters from occurring (e.g., food/water safety standards); 2. Mitigation: actions to lessen impact of unavoidable disasters (e.g., earthquake-proof buildings); 3. Preparedness: planning, training, and education; 4. Response: actions before, during, and immediately after a disaster to prevent loss of life (e.g., search and rescue); 5. Recovery: restoring damaged infrastructure and resuming regular operations.

The Medical Council of Canada defines a disaster as a serious disruption causing widespread losses exceeding a society's ability to cope using only its own resources. These five phases are the standard framework in Canadian emergency management. Physicians play key roles particularly in the response and recovery phases.

**Q:** What are the key components of an Emergency Response Plan (ERP), Incident Management System (IMS), and Emergency Operations Centre (EOC)?

**A:** ERP: document outlining policies, procedures, roles, and responsibilities of various parties to coordinate action during disasters (e.g., Ontario provincial ERP). IMS: provides guidelines for standardizing responses during the response phase, including personnel, facilities, equipment, financing, procedures, and communications. EOC: a centralized facility to coordinate the entire disaster response. COOP (Continuity of Operations Plan): guidelines for maintaining critical functions during a disaster.

These emergency management structures are used at all levels of government and health care organizations in Canada. The IMS is based on the Incident Command System (ICS) framework. Understanding these structures is important for physicians who may be required to participate in organized disaster responses.

**Q:** What does the mnemonic WHACS stand for in occupational health history-taking?

**A:** W – What do you do (job duties)? H – How do you do it (process)? A – Are you exposed to anything (chemical, biological, physical, ergonomic hazards)? C – Co-workers with similar symptoms? S – Satisfied with your job (psychosocial factors)?

Taking a thorough occupational history using WHACS helps identify work-related causes of disease that can look identical to non-occupational illness. Occupation is a key social determinant of health. Without identifying an occupational link, opportunities for prevention, workers' compensation, and workplace safety improvement may be missed.

**Q:** What are the six categories of occupational hazards and provide one health effect for each?

**A:** 1. Physical (e.g., noise → hearing loss; ionizing radiation → cancer; temperature extremes → heat stroke; vibration → hand-arm vibration syndrome). 2. Chemical (e.g., asbestos → asbestosis/mesothelioma; benzene → acute myeloid leukemia; carbon monoxide → chemical asphyxiation; silica → silicosis). 3. Biological (e.g., mould → farmer's lung/hypersensitivity pneumonitis; blood/body fluids → HIV, Hepatitis B/C). 4. Psychosocial (e.g., bullying, harassment, shift work → PTSD, mental illness, absenteeism). 5. Ergonomic (e.g., manual materials handling → musculoskeletal disorders; improper lighting → headaches). 6. Safety (e.g., working from heights, heavy machinery → severe injury and death).

These six categories are standard in Canadian occupational medicine curriculum. Physicians must consider all categories when evaluating work-related illness or injury. Each category requires specific assessment strategies (e.g., audiograms for noise, PFTs for dust, whole blood lead for chemical exposure).

**Q:** What are the five levels of the hierarchy of controls for occupational hazards, in order from most to least effective?

**A:** 1. Elimination (physically remove the hazard, e.g., driverless car instead of driver). 2. Substitution (replace hazard with safer alternative, e.g., replace with train). 3. Engineering controls (isolate workers from hazard, e.g., airbags, ventilation). 4. Administrative controls (change how work is done, e.g., traffic laws, rotation schedules). 5. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) (protect worker, e.g., seatbelts, respirators) — least effective.

The hierarchy of controls is the fundamental framework for reducing occupational exposures in Canada and globally (based on CDC/NIOSH framework). Elimination and substitution are preferred because they remove the hazard entirely. PPE is the last resort as it depends on correct and consistent use by workers and does not remove the hazard.

**Q:** What are the three rights of workers under Ontario's Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA), and which workers have restricted right-to-refuse dangerous work?

**A:** Three rights: (1) Right to Know (training and information about workplace hazards); (2) Right to Participate (representatives on joint health and safety committees); (3) Right to Refuse dangerous work. Restriction: some occupations cannot refuse if the danger is a normal part of their job or refusal would endanger others (e.g., police, firefighters, some health care workers).

The OHSA is Ontario's key occupational health legislation, enforced by Ministry of Labour inspectors. Understanding worker rights is important for physicians advising patients about workplace safety. There is also a right for 'certified' workers to stop work they feel endangers other workers. Similar legislation exists in all Canadian provinces.

**Q:** What is the Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB) in Ontario and what benefits does it provide?

**A:** The WSIB is an autonomous Ontario government agency established under the Workplace Safety and Insurance Act that oversees workplace safety training and administers insurance for workers and employers. Benefits include: loss of earned income, non-economic loss (physical/functional/psychological), loss of retirement income, health care expenses (first-aid, medical treatment), and survivor benefits (for dependants/spouses). It is a no-fault insurance system; workers cannot sue the employer but are guaranteed compensation for accepted claims. Negligence is not considered a factor.

WSIB represents the workers' compensation system for Ontario; each province has its own equivalent legislation and board. Physicians play a critical role in WSIB claims by documenting injuries, providing functional assessments, and supporting return-to-work planning. WSIB covers 74-99% of the workforce depending on the province.

**Q:** What are the three levels of prevention in occupational medicine and what does each entail?

**A:** Primary prevention: proactive efforts to anticipate, recognize, evaluate, and control workplace hazards before illness or injury occurs (hierarchy of controls). Secondary prevention: medical surveillance (periodic examinations) of exposed workers to identify early signs of overexposure or disease (e.g., whole blood lead testing, pulmonary function tests, audiograms). Tertiary prevention: treatment and rehabilitation to facilitate safe and timely return to work, including WSIB, retraining, and job modification.

These three levels align with the general prevention framework applied across all of public health. In occupational medicine, secondary prevention is particularly important because occupational diseases are often detectable before they become clinically manifest. Physicians must also delineate worker restrictions vs. limitations vs. abilities vs. tolerance in return-to-work assessments.

**Q:** What specific secondary prevention investigations are used in occupational medicine for lead exposure, dust-related asthma, and noise-induced hearing loss?

**A:** Lead exposure: whole blood lead testing (identifies effectiveness of controls and guides removal from exposure). Dust-related asthma: pulmonary function tests (spirometry). Noise-induced hearing loss: audiograms.

Medical surveillance in occupational medicine requires condition-specific tests that detect early subclinical changes. Whole blood lead levels in Ontario must be reported to the Medical Officer of Health above certain thresholds. Audiometric testing and PFTs are performed at regular intervals in workers with significant occupational noise or dust exposure.

**Q:** What occupational exposure is associated with mesothelioma and asbestosis, and what chemical causes acute myeloid leukemia?

**A:** Asbestos exposure → asbestosis (restrictive lung disease from fibrosis) and mesothelioma (pleural malignancy). Benzene exposure (prolonged) → acute myeloid leukemia (AML).

Asbestos is a major occupational carcinogen historically used in construction, shipbuilding, and insulation in Canada. Mesothelioma has a very long latency period (20-50 years) and is virtually pathognomonic for asbestos exposure. Benzene is found in petroleum products and industrial solvents. Both exposures are regulated under WHMIS in Canada.

**Q:** What is Farmer's Lung, its cause, and its pathological mechanism?

**A:** Farmer's Lung is an occupational lung disease caused by exposure to mould (typically thermophilic actinomycetes from mouldy hay/grain). The pathological mechanism is hypersensitivity pneumonitis (extrinsic allergic alveolitis), an immune-mediated inflammatory reaction in the lung parenchyma.

Farmer's Lung is classified as a biological occupational hazard. It presents with fever, chills, cough, and dyspnoea 4-8 hours after antigen exposure. Repeated exposure can lead to progressive fibrosis. Management includes avoidance of the antigen and corticosteroids for acute episodes. It is an important example of an occupational lung disease in the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the distinction between worker restrictions, limitations, abilities, and tolerance in occupational medicine return-to-work assessments?

**A:** Restrictions: what the worker cannot do even if they want to, because it would worsen their condition. Limitations: what the worker is unable to do due to injury or impairment. Abilities: what the worker is still able to do (focus here to maximize function). Tolerance: what the worker is willing to do.

This distinction is critical for physicians completing functional capacity assessments and return-to-work reports in Canada (e.g., for WSIB). Clear delineation of restrictions vs. limitations helps employers make appropriate workplace accommodations and avoids unnecessary prolonged absence from work, which itself can worsen outcomes.

**Q:** What is the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System (WHMIS) and what is its scope in Canada?

**A:** WHMIS is a national system for providing information about hazardous materials used in Canadian workplaces. It is universal across Canada and includes requirements for: corporate responsibility to maintain a safe workplace, safety data sheets (formerly Material Safety Data Sheets/MSDSs) for chemical exposures, and joint health and safety committees (workers + management). Jurisdiction is primarily provincial (90% of workers), with 16 federally-regulated industries covered under the Canada Labour Code.

WHMIS was updated to WHMIS 2015 to align with the Globally Harmonized System (GHS) of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals. Workers can request safety data sheets to understand chemical hazards in their workplace. Joint health and safety committees are mandatory in workplaces with 20+ employees in Ontario.

**Q:** What is Environmental Racism in the context of Grassy Narrows, Ontario — what happened and what was its health impact?

**A:** Grassy Narrows is a First Nations reserve in northwest Ontario where an upstream paper mill dumped tonnes of untreated mercury into the water supply over several years. Residents began developing symptoms of severe mercury poisoning (neurotoxicity) in the 1970s. It decimated two major employment sources (fishing and guiding), contaminated food and water supplies, and left the community with limited resources. The federal government did not agree to build a mercury treatment facility until 2020 (~50 years later), and construction had not begun at time of writing.

Grassy Narrows is one of the most significant examples of environmental racism in Canada. The Minamata disease-like syndrome caused by organic mercury (methylmercury) causes severe and irreversible neurological damage. The prolonged government inaction despite documented harm illustrates systemic injustice in environmental protection. Canada's adoption of UNDRIP aims to provide better protections for Indigenous communities.

**Q:** What is the Source-Path-Receiver Model in environmental health and how is it applied?

**A:** The Source-Path-Receiver Model provides a framework for preventing workplace injuries and environmental exposures. Strategies can be implemented at three levels: (1) Source – modify or eliminate the hazard at its origin; (2) Path – modify the route of transmission between source and receiver; (3) Receiver – protect the receiver (worker/person) from the hazard. This corresponds to the hierarchy of controls (elimination → PPE).

This model is widely used in occupational and environmental health to identify intervention points. Addressing the source is most effective (elimination/substitution), modifying the path involves engineering controls, and protecting the receiver involves administrative controls and PPE. The model aligns with the general principle of controlling hazards before they reach the individual.

**Q:** What are the public health mitigation vs. adaptation approaches to climate change?

**A:** Mitigation: efforts to slow, stabilize, or reverse climate change by directly reducing greenhouse gas emissions (e.g., transitioning to renewable energy, reducing fossil fuel use). Adaptation: anticipating and preparing for the effects of climate change (e.g., conducting health vulnerability and adaptation assessments, identifying at-risk populations, evaluating resilience of current health systems).

Both mitigation and adaptation are necessary components of the public health response to climate change. Physicians can contribute through climate-sensitive clinical practice, advocacy, and health system planning. Key Canadian policies include the Pan-Canadian Framework on Clean Growth and Climate Change. Vulnerable populations (elderly, Indigenous, low-income) require targeted adaptation strategies.

**Q:** What is the role of the Chief Public Health Officer (CPHO) of Canada?

**A:** The CPHO is the federal government's lead public health professional who:
- Provides health advice to the Minister of Health and President of PHAC
- Collaborates with other governments, jurisdictions, and agencies
- Communicates public health information to professionals, stakeholders, and the public
- In an emergency (outbreak or natural disaster), directs PHAC staff including medical professionals, scientists, and epidemiologists to coordinate emergency response

The CPHO is appointed under the Public Health Agency of Canada Act and serves as Canada's top public health advisor. Understanding the CPHO's role is important for Canadian medical licensing exams.

**Q:** What is the Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) and when was it created?

**A:** PHAC is the main Government of Canada agency responsible for public health. It was created as an independent body post-SARS to strengthen public health capacity and response. Functions include:
- Preventing chronic diseases and injuries
- Responding to public health emergencies and infectious disease outbreaks
- CTFPHC guideline secretariat and knowledge brokers
- Overseeing immigration screening and protecting Canadian borders (airport health inspection)
- Liaising with the WHO on global health issues

PHAC was established in 2004 following the 2003 SARS outbreak to fill gaps in Canada's public health infrastructure. It is a federal agency distinct from Health Canada.

**Q:** What is the role of the Medical Officer of Health (MOH) in Ontario and what legislation governs their powers?

**A:** The MOH (also called 'Medical Health Officer' in other provinces) is:
- Appointed to each public health unit
- A Public Health and Preventive Medicine specialist physician
- Responsible for oversight of a multidisciplinary team that collects/analyzes epidemiological data, conducts health surveillance, and implements health programs

Powers are outlined in the Health Protection and Promotion Act (HPPA), specifically Sections 13 and 22, which allow the MOH to require an individual/premise/agency to take or refrain from any action due to a public health hazard.

The MOH is the key local public health authority. Section 22 of the HPPA gives the MOH authority to issue orders to control health hazards, which is a common examination topic.

**Q:** Distinguish between health equity and health equality.

**A:** Health equity: when all people have 'the opportunity to attain their full health potential' and no one is 'disadvantaged from achieving this potential because of their social position or other socially determined circumstance.' Health inequities are systematic differences considered unjust.

Health equality: where populations have equal or similar health status. Health inequalities are systematic differences in the health of groups that do not necessarily carry a moral judgement.

The distinction is important: equity focuses on fairness and removing unjust disadvantages, while equality simply refers to sameness of outcomes without moral judgment. This is a common conceptual question on public health exams.

**Q:** Define the following: impairment, disability, and handicap.

**A:** Impairment: 'any loss or abnormality of psychological, physiological, or anatomical structure or function'

Disability: 'any restriction or lack of ability to perform an activity within the range considered normal for a human being'

Handicap: a disadvantage for an individual arising from impairment or disability — 'limits or prevents the fulfillment of an individual's normal role as determined by society and depends on age, sex, social, and cultural factors'

These WHO definitions form a hierarchy: impairment → disability → handicap. Understanding the distinction is important for disability assessment and public health policy in Canada.

**Q:** What did the 1974 Lalonde Report contribute to Canadian public health?

**A:** The Lalonde Report ('A New Perspective on the Health of Canadians,' 1974), published by federal Minister of Health Marc Lalonde, outlined four factors that determine health:
1. Human biology
2. Environment
3. Lifestyle
4. Health care organizations

This was groundbreaking as it expanded the concept of health determinants beyond just medical care, forming the basis for the modern social determinants of health framework.

The Lalonde Report was a landmark Canadian document that shifted thinking about health from a purely biomedical model to a broader socioecological model. It is frequently referenced in Canadian public health education.

**Q:** What are the social determinants of health (SDOH) as per the WHO (2023)?

**A:** The SDOH are 'non-medical factors that influence health outcomes,' including 'the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, and the wider set of forces and systems shaping the conditions of daily life.'

Key Canadian SDOH include:
- Income and social status
- Employment and working conditions
- Education and literacy
- Childhood experiences
- Physical environments
- Social supports and coping skills
- Health-related behaviours
- Access to health services
- Biology and genetic endowment
- Gender, culture
- Exposure to colonization and racialized prejudice/racism

The SDOH framework is central to Canadian public health practice and is tested on the MCCQE1. Colonization and racism are explicitly included as determinants in Canadian guidelines.

**Q:** Distinguish between cultural humility, cultural safety, cultural awareness, cultural sensitivity, and cultural competency.

**A:** Cultural awareness: attitude that includes awareness about differences between cultures

Cultural sensitivity: recognizes differences between cultures and that these differences are important to acknowledge in healthcare

Cultural competency: focuses on practitioners attaining skills, knowledge, and attitudes to work with people of different cultures including Indigenous peoples

Cultural humility: a respectful, person-centered, life-long process of learning and self-reflexivity; humble acknowledgement of oneself as a learner regarding a patient's experience

Cultural safety: developed by Dr. Irihapeti Ramsden; considers power relationships between health professionals and patients; recipients of care are empowered to decide what is culturally safe; practitioners are self-reflective about their position of power

These terms are often confused. Cultural safety (developed by a Maori nurse) is more transformative than cultural competency—it addresses power imbalances rather than just knowledge acquisition. Both are increasingly emphasized in Canadian medical education.

**Q:** What are the three distinct groups of Indigenous peoples in Canada as recognized by the Constitution Act 1982?

**A:** Per Section 35 of the Constitution Act 1982:
1. First Nations (status and non-status) — includes over 600 diverse communities; status/non-status refers to registration under the Indian Act (1876)
2. Métis — descendants of First Nations and European settlers; nearly 2/3 reside in cities, greatest proportion in Ontario and prairie provinces
3. Inuit — roughly 75% of ~70,000 Inuit reside in Inuit Nunangat (Nunavut, Nunavik, Nunatsiavut, Inuvialuit); majority in Nunavut (30,135)

In 2021, more than 1.8 million Canadians self-identify as Indigenous (~5% of total population); growing fastest of any group (9.4% between 2016-2021); youngest population (28% under age 25).

This is essential knowledge for Canadian medical licensing. Indigenous peoples are a distinct, growing, and young demographic with specific health equity considerations.

**Q:** What were residential schools and what are their health impacts on Indigenous peoples?

**A:** Residential schools (1870s–1996): government-mandated schools where Indigenous children were forced to attend and assimilate. Effects included:
- Poor nutrition, hygiene, living conditions
- Physical, sexual, and psychological abuse
- Spiritual harms through loss of traditional language and culture

Health impacts:
- Poorer general and self-rated health
- Increased rates of chronic and infectious diseases
- Mental distress, depression, substance use, suicide
- Intergenerational trauma (affects subsequent generations)
- 'Residential school syndrome': extends PTSD criteria to include tendency to misuse alcohol/drugs from young age, loss of cultural knowledge, violent outbursts, difficulty parenting

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) Final Report (2015) contains 94 Calls to Action, some directed at HCPs and medical schools.

Physicians must be aware of the lasting impacts of residential schools. The TRC Calls to Action include responsibilities for HCPs to respond to and support reconciliation. This is a key Canadian public health topic.

**Q:** What is the current tuberculosis (TB) disparity between Inuit and non-Indigenous Canadians?

**A:** In 2016, the average annual incidence rate of TB among Inuit in Canada was roughly 296 times higher than Canadian-born non-Indigenous people.

Historical context: From 1930–1940, TB death rates in Inuit populations were ~700 per 100,000 — among the highest ever recorded in a human population (for comparison, global TB crude death rate in 2016 was 17 per 100,000).

The Canadian government forcibly relocated many Indigenous people to TB sanatoriums/'Indian hospitals,' often hundreds of kilometres away, with average length of stay 2.5 years and many patients never returning home.

In 2018, Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK) and the Government of Canada committed to the Inuit Tuberculosis Elimination Framework: reduce TB rates by 50% by 2025 and eliminate TB by 2030.

The massive TB disparity in Indigenous communities is a direct consequence of colonization, forced relocation, and overcrowding. This is a high-yield Canadian public health topic for the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What was the Sixties Scoop and what are its ongoing health implications?

**A:** The Sixties Scoop (1951–1980s): government-mandated practice of removing Indigenous children from their families without consent for placement in foster care or adoption. Indigenous parents were deemed 'unfit'; the goal was assimilation into non-Indigenous families.

Ongoing implications:
- Indigenous children still overrepresented in foster care
- In 2016, Indigenous youth ages 0–4 made up ~half of all foster children in private households, despite being only 8% of this age group
- Youth with history in government care: greater risk for substance misuse, street involvement, incarceration
- Many Indigenous mothers/families avoid accessing healthcare for fear of child apprehension
- Displacement from language and culture hinders family resilience

The Sixties Scoop is a form of cultural genocide whose legacy persists. Healthcare providers must understand why Indigenous families may distrust health services, including fear of child welfare involvement.

**Q:** What were the coerced sterilizations of Indigenous women in Canada?

**A:** Throughout the 20th century, eugenics programs existed across Canada:
- 1920s–1930s: Alberta and British Columbia legalized eugenic policies in Sexual Sterilization Acts (not repealed until 1970s)
- Indigenous women were disproportionately targeted
- Coerced sterilization included: tubal ligations without consent, being falsely told procedures are reversible, being pressured to sign consent forms while in labour or on operating tables, ultimatums (tubal ligation or risk child apprehension)
- Many sterilizations occurred outside legislation in federally run 'Indian hospitals'
- Documented as recently as 2019; at least 100 Indigenous women have come forward spanning 1970s–2019

Coerced sterilization of Indigenous women is a violation of reproductive rights and bodily autonomy with deep roots in Canadian history. Knowledge of this history is essential for culturally safe care.

**Q:** What is the approach to Indigenous health and wellness based on traditional frameworks?

**A:** Key principles:
- Restoring balance in four realms: spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical health
- Acting as an individual, as a member of family, community, and nation
- Medicine Wheel (First Nations), Learning Blanket (Inuit), Métis Tree Model — all share a worldview of holistic lifelong learning and wellness
- Indigenous medicines include song, dance, smudge, ceremonies, plant medicines — practiced by experts with decades of apprenticeship
- Focus on quality of life, not just cure; different understanding of illness causation than biomedical model

For physicians: begin with curiosity (ask 'What do I need to know about you as a person to give you the best care possible?'); do not assume interest in traditional medicine; traditional medicine is unlikely to interfere with Western therapies.

Cultural humility is the recommended approach for Indigenous patient care. The NIHO offers 8 guidelines on culturally safe care for Indigenous patients including access to ceremony, family support, and appropriate handling of death.

**Q:** What health issues disproportionately affect Indigenous peoples in Canada and what are population-specific interventions?

**A:** Health issues:
- Higher rates of obesity (secondary to poor access to high-quality nutrition, food insecurity)
- Higher rates of smoking, substance misuse, and suicide
- Higher rates of TB (296x higher in Inuit vs. non-Indigenous Canadians)
- Mental health issues (higher rates of depression secondary to intergenerational trauma)
- Homelessness off-reserve
- Exposure to environmental toxins (poor drinking water from land dispossession)

Interventions:
- Movements towards decolonization and TRC recommendations
- Mental health awareness and increasing health literacy
- Indigenous-specific chronic disease management (e.g., DM)
- Culturally appropriate harm reduction, substance use treatment, smoking cessation
- Cultural continuity (language and cultural programs protective against depression and suicide)
- Incorporation of Traditional Medicine into care plan
- Health practitioner training in cultural humility and safety

Cultural continuity — maintaining language, traditions, and community connections — is a protective factor against depression and suicide in Indigenous youth. This is an important and testable concept.

**Q:** How can primary care practitioners screen for poverty in clinical settings?

**A:** Poverty affects 20% of families in Ontario and is a risk factor for chronic diseases, cancer, and mental illness. Groups at highest risk: women, Indigenous peoples, new immigrants, LGBTQ+.

Two-question screening tool:
1. Step 1: 'Do you ever have difficulty making ends meet at the end of the month?'
   - Sensitivity 98%, specificity 40% for living below the poverty line
2. Step 2: 'Have you filled out and sent in your tax forms?'
   - Tax returns required to access income security benefits (GST/HST credit, working income tax benefits, property tax credits, child benefits)

Next step: Connect patients to social and financial supports (e.g., ODSP, Ontario Works) and resources (e.g., free community tax clinic).

Social prescribing — connecting patients to community resources — is an increasingly important role for primary care physicians. The two-question poverty screen is a validated and high-yield tool for the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What mandatory medical exams are required for new immigrants to Canada?

**A:** Mandatory exams on entry include:
- Chest X-ray: report to local public health
- Syphilis serology for persons ≥15 yr
- HIV testing for applicants ≥15 yr, as well as children who received blood/blood products, have a known HIV-positive mother, or have identified risk
- Serum creatinine for persons ≥15 yr and children with history of HTN (resting BP >150/90), DM, kidney disease, or signs of impaired renal function

Additional components:
- Compassionate psychosocial assessment (increased prevalence of mental health issues, e.g., PTSD, intimate partner violence)
- Assess immunization documents and develop catch-up schedule

Immigration medical exams are a public health tool to identify and manage communicable diseases. The specific tests required (CXR, HIV, syphilis, creatinine) are testable on the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the 'healthy immigrant effect'?

**A:** The healthy immigrant effect describes the phenomenon where new immigrants to Canada arrive with better health status than the general Canadian population, but their health worsens over time to match (or sometimes exceed the burden of) the general population.

Contributing factors to worsening health over time:
- Barriers finding employment matching skills/qualifications
- Exposure to cultural discrimination and isolation
- Cultural or religious expectations
- Acculturation to less healthy Canadian lifestyle habits

The healthy immigrant effect is a well-documented public health phenomenon in Canada. It highlights that immigrant health trajectories are shaped by social determinants after arrival rather than by their origin characteristics.

**Q:** What health issues disproportionately affect the LGBTIQ2S population in Canada and what are recommended clinical approaches?

**A:** Health issues:
- Higher rates of depression, anxiety, OCD, suicidality, and self-harm
- Increased risk of alcohol, tobacco, and other substance misuse
- Double the risk for PTSD compared to heterosexual individuals
- Greater participation in high-risk sexual practices (HIV risk)
- Mental health deterioration from internalized queerphobia and limited sociomedical infrastructure
- Over-representation in youth homeless population
- Victims of hate crimes (higher fatality in racialized LGBTQ+ communities)

Clinical approaches:
- Apply an intersectional lens (racialized, gender-diverse, cultural roles)
- Use gender-neutral language and avoid heteronormative assumptions
- Invite patients to self-identify as gender or sexual minorities
- Increased awareness of social, legal, and medical context in which LGBTIQ2S individuals live
- Recognize individuals belonging to multiple marginalized communities face additional barriers

LGBTIQ2S health is increasingly tested in Canadian medical education. The mnemonic helps recall the full spectrum: Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Trans, Intersex, Questioning/Queer, Two-Spirited, and Asexual.

**Q:** What are the three types of surveillance used in public health?

**A:** 1. Passive surveillance: Reporting of disease data by all institutions that see patients, relying on cooperation of HCPs (laboratories, hospitals, health facilities, private practitioners)
   - Most common, least expensive, but difficult to ensure completeness and timeliness

2. Active surveillance: Regular visits to health facilities to review medical records for suspected cases, or active testing of a population
   - Resource-intensive; used when a disease is targeted for eradication (every case must be investigated) or for outbreak investigations

3. Sentinel surveillance: Selective reporting from a limited network of carefully selected reporting sites with high probability of seeing cases
   - Can signal trends, identify outbreaks, and monitor disease burden in a cost-effective manner
   - May not identify rare diseases or those outside the catchment area

Surveillance is the continuous, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health-related data. Understanding the three types and their trade-offs is essential for epidemiology questions on the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What are the ideal criteria for a screening test? (Wilson-Jungner criteria adapted)

**A:** Disease criteria:
- Causes significant suffering and/or death
- Natural history must be understood
- Must have an asymptomatic stage detectable by a test
- Early detection and intervention must result in improved outcomes

Test criteria:
- High sensitivity
- Safe, rapid, easy, relatively inexpensive
- Acceptable to HCPs and the population
- Continuously utilized

Health Care System criteria:
- Adequate capacity for reporting, follow-up, and treatment of positive screens
- Cost effective
- Sustainable program
- Clear policy guidelines on who to treat

These criteria help determine whether a screening program should be implemented. High sensitivity is prioritized (to minimize missed cases), while specificity matters for confirmatory/diagnostic testing. These criteria are frequently tested.

**Q:** Differentiate lead-time bias and length-time bias in screening.

**A:** Lead-time bias: Overestimation of survival time 'from diagnosis' when the estimate is made from the time of screening, instead of the later time when the disease would have been diagnosed without screening.
- Screen-detected patients appear to 'live longer' after diagnosis, but this is because diagnosis occurred earlier in the disease course, not because of improved outcomes.

Length-time bias: Overestimation of survival time due to screening at one time point including more stable (slower-progressing) cases than aggressive (rapidly fatal) cases of disease.
- Aggressive cancers with shorter presymptomatic stages are less likely to be caught at screening; screening preferentially identifies indolent disease, making the screened population appear to have better prognosis.

These are the two most important biases in screening program evaluation. Lead-time bias affects the apparent survival from diagnosis; length-time bias affects which disease subtypes are captured. Both can make a screening program appear more effective than it is.

**Q:** What is the difference between screening and case finding?

**A:** Screening: A strategy used in a population to identify the possible presence of an as-yet-undiagnosed disease in individuals without signs or symptoms. Done at the population level as part of a structured program (e.g., provincial breast cancer screening program).

Case finding: Individual identification of at-risk patients during clinical encounters (e.g., screening by primary care/family physicians). Less systematic than population-level screening.

Key distinction: Both seek to risk-stratify for further investigation. Screening tests are not diagnostic tests — they are followed by confirmatory diagnostic tests for screen-positive individuals. Screening is best done at population level to minimize biases and harms.

The MCCQE1 tests the distinction between screening and diagnostic testing. Screening has lower specificity (acceptable false positives) because the goal is to not miss true cases; diagnostic tests must be highly specific.

**Q:** What are the types of screening programs?

**A:** 1. Universal screening: Screening ALL members of a population for a disease
   - Example: Phenylketonuria (PKU) and hypothyroidism in all newborns

2. Selective (targeted) screening: Screening of targeted subgroups at risk for a disease
   - Example: Mammography in women >50 years

3. Multiphasic screening: Use of many measurements and investigations to look for many disease entities
   - Example: Periodic health examination

Knowing the three types of screening helps categorize programs. Universal newborn screening for metabolic disorders (PKU, congenital hypothyroidism) is a classic Canadian public health example.

**Q:** Compare the disease prevention model and health promotion model.

**A:** Disease Prevention:
- Health = absence of disease
- Medical model (passive role of individual)
- Aimed mainly at high-risk groups
- One-shot strategy aimed at a specific pathology
- Directive and persuasive strategies enforced in target groups
- Focused mostly on individuals
- Led by professional groups from health disciplines

Health Promotion:
- Health = positive and multidimensional concept
- Participatory model
- Aimed at the population in its total environment
- Diverse and complementary strategies aimed at a network of issues/determinants
- Facilitating and enabling approaches by incentives offered to the population
- Focused on a person's health status and environment
- Led by non-professional organizations, civic groups, local/municipal/regional/national governments

This comparison is a classic MCCQE1 topic. Health promotion is upstream, participatory, and empowering; disease prevention is downstream, individual-focused, and medically directed.

**Q:** What are the methods of healthy public policy?

**A:** Healthy public policy creates a supportive environment to enable people to lead healthy lives, making healthy choices easier for citizens. Methods include:

1. Fiscal: Imposing additional costs (e.g., taxes on tobacco and alcohol)
2. Legislative: Implementing legal deterrents (e.g., smoking bans, legal alcohol drinking age)
3. Social: Improving health beyond universally funded health care (e.g., providing affordable housing)

The Nuffield Intervention Ladder provides a framework for ranking the level of intrusion and the need for proportionate benefit in health promotion interventions at a population level.

Fiscal and legislative methods are powerful upstream determinants of health. The MCCQE1 tests knowledge of policy as a health promotion tool, especially tobacco taxation which is one of the most effective public health interventions.

**Q:** Describe the Health Belief Model and its six concepts.

**A:** The Health Belief Model (1975) is a psychological model explaining and predicting individual health behaviours based on beliefs and attitudes. An individual will adopt a beneficial health behaviour if three beliefs are present:
1. The negative health outcome is avoidable
2. Expects the health outcome can be prevented if the recommended behaviour is adopted
3. The individual can be successful in adopting the behaviour

Six concepts:
1. Perceived susceptibility — belief about personal risk
2. Perceived severity — belief about seriousness of disease
3. Perceived benefits — belief that the recommended action will reduce risk
4. Perceived barriers — belief about costs/obstacles to the action
5. Cues to action — stimuli that trigger health action
6. Self-efficacy — confidence in ability to take the health action

The Health Belief Model is widely used in public health. The four 'readiness to act' concepts (susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers) are often tested. Self-efficacy is Bandura's contribution to the updated model.

**Q:** Describe the Stages of Change (Transtheoretical) Model and its five stages.

**A:** The Stages of Change Model provides a framework for facilitating behaviour change (e.g., quitting smoking). Five stages:

1. Precontemplation: Not seriously considering change; not interested in any intervention
2. Contemplation: Thinking about changing behaviour in the next 6 months
3. Preparation: Beginning to experiment with small changes; resolves to make a serious attempt (usually within 30 days)
4. Action: Actively involved in making the change, using different techniques
5. Maintenance: Must successfully cope with temptations to return to previous behaviour

Relapse: return to a previous stage — a normal part of the change process.

Developed by Prochaska, DiClemente, and Norcross (1992).

The Stages of Change model is clinically applied in counselling for smoking cessation, substance use, diet, and exercise. Identifying the patient's stage allows tailored counselling — e.g., motivational interviewing for precontemplators.

**Q:** What are the three predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors that determine behaviour?

**A:** Behaviour results from three factors (PRECEDE model):
1. Predisposing factors: Knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, values, intentions
   - E.g., knowing smoking causes cancer
2. Enabling factors: Skills, supports
   - E.g., access to nicotine replacement therapy, ability to afford a gym
3. Reinforcing factors: HCPs and the social context of family and community
   - E.g., family members who smoke undermining cessation efforts; physician encouragement

Health education serves to: increase knowledge and skills, and promote healthy behaviours.

These three factors form the basis of the PRECEDE-PROCEED health program planning model. Understanding them helps clinicians design effective behaviour change interventions that address upstream as well as individual-level factors.

**Q:** Define incidence rate and prevalence and explain their relationship.

**A:** Incidence rate: Number of new cases in a population over a specific period of time
- Measures disease occurrence

Prevalence: Total number of cases in a population over a defined period of time
- Point prevalence: assessed at one point in time
- Period prevalence: assessed over a period (includes new cases, excludes cases that terminate)

Relationship: Prevalence ≈ Incidence Rate × Disease Duration
- Prevalence favours inclusion of chronic over acute cases
- May underestimate disease burden if those with short disease duration are missed
- Prevalence estimates useful for measuring disease burden and planning facilities/services

This is a fundamental epidemiology concept. High prevalence can result from high incidence OR long disease duration (e.g., HIV has high prevalence due to effective treatment prolonging survival). Understanding this relationship is essential for the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is age-standardized rate and why is it used?

**A:** Age-standardized rate: Adjustment of the crude rate of a health-related event using a 'standard' population with a known number of persons in each age and sex group.

Why used: When comparing a health measure (e.g., mortality) between two populations that differ in characteristics known to influence that outcome (e.g., age), standardization is used to control for the possible effect of that factor (confounding by age).

Types of standardization:
1. Direct standardization: Applies known age-specific rates to a standard population
2. Indirect standardization: Expressed as standardized outcome ratio (e.g., SMR = Standardized Mortality Ratio)
   - SMR >100 = higher than expected deaths
   - SMR <100 = fewer than expected deaths

Age-standardization is essential for valid comparison of health statistics across populations with different age structures. The SMR is a common indirect standardization measure tested on the MCCQE1.

**Q:** What is the natural history of disease and its stages?

**A:** The natural history of disease describes the course of a disease from onset to resolution:

1. Pathological onset: The biological beginning of disease
2. Presymptomatic stage: From onset to first appearance of symptoms/signs (disease is present but not yet clinically apparent — this is the window for secondary prevention/screening)
3. Clinical manifestation of disease: May regress spontaneously, be subject to remissions and relapses, or progress to death

Importance: Understanding natural history determines where in the disease course different levels of prevention can be applied.

The natural history of disease framework underpins all levels of disease prevention. The presymptomatic stage is the target of screening programs, and understanding this is fundamental to interpreting screening lead-time bias.

**Q:** What is harm reduction and how does it differ from risk reduction? Provide examples.

**A:** Risk reduction: Lowering the risk to health without eliminating it
- Example: Avoiding sun exposure to lower risk of skin cancer

Harm reduction: A set of strategies aimed to reduce the negative consequences of drug use and other risky behaviours without necessarily stopping the behaviour
- Examples: Needle exchange programs, supervised injection sites (SIS)

Evidence for supervised injection sites:
- Associated with 88 fewer overdose deaths per 100,000 person-years
- 67% fewer ambulance calls for treating overdoses
- 35% decrease in HIV infections
- Effects on hospitalizations unknown

Note: Harm reduction is a passive prevention approach (operates without requiring the person's active involvement in stopping the behaviour).

Harm reduction is a pragmatic public health approach that accepts that some individuals will continue risky behaviours and focuses on minimizing harms. SIS evidence from Canada (Insite in Vancouver) is a landmark public health case study frequently referenced in Canadian medical licensing.

**Q:** What are the services provided by municipal/local public health units in Canada?

**A:** Examples of services delivered by municipal/local public health include:
1. Infectious disease control: follow-up of reported diseases and management of local outbreaks
2. Inspection of food premises: hospitals, nursing homes, restaurants
3. Family health services: pre-conception, preschool, school-aged, and adult health programs
4. Tobacco control legislation enforcement
5. Assessment and management of local environmental health risks
6. Collection and dissemination of local health status reports
7. Oral health programs

Local public health units are the frontline of public health service delivery in Canada. Their roles bridge individual/family health services and population-level health protection. The Ontario Public Health Standards specify mandatory programs.

**Q:** What are rates, ratios, and proportions in epidemiology?

**A:** Rate: Measures the frequency of an event in a defined population over a specific period of time
- Example: Number of opioid overdoses in Canada in one year
- Includes a time component

Ratio: Compares the magnitude of one quantity to another (numerator and denominator are not necessarily related)
- Example: Ratio of women to men with lupus (3:1 overall, but 9:1 in reproductive years)

Proportion: A ratio where the numerator is a part of the denominator (ranges 0–1 or 0–100%)
- Example: Proportion of deliveries complicated by placental abruption

Distinguishing rates, ratios, and proportions is fundamental to interpreting epidemiological data. Incidence rate is a true rate (includes time); case fatality rate is actually a proportion (deaths/cases, no time element despite the name).

**Q:** What are the special health considerations for refugees arriving in Canada?

**A:** Refugees have forced migration due to well-founded fear of persecution and are given protection by the Government of Canada.

Health challenges:
- Post-traumatic stress disorders and depression
- Adjustment problems
- Diseases from country of origin (e.g., malaria, TB, onchocerciasis)
- Direct and indirect effects of war (trauma, injury)
- Long-standing lack of access to health care (chronically neglected problems)

Healthcare access: Partial health coverage via Interim Federal Health Program (IFHP) for refugee claimants

Key interventions:
- Vaccinations and catch-up immunization
- Women's health and mental health services
- Comprehensive medical exam
- Dental and vision screening
- Political advocacy
- Language training and workplace support
- Community integration support

The IFHP provides basic coverage to refugee claimants pending determination. Physicians should be aware that refugees may have untreated chronic conditions and significant mental health burden from trauma and displacement.

**Q:** What are the key health disparities and interventions for people experiencing homelessness?

**A:** Definition: An individual who lacks permanent housing or adequate housing.

Health risks:
- Exposure to temperature extremes
- Exposure to communicable diseases in shelters
- Greater risk of experiencing violence due to lack of housing and protection
- Higher rates of adverse childhood events leading to substance use
- Mental illness, addictions, food insecurity

Determinants: Low income, mental illness, substance use

Interventions:
- Housing First policies (stable housing as prerequisite for addressing other health issues)
- Safe housing
- Addictions support
- Mental health services

Housing First is an evidence-based approach to homelessness showing that providing stable housing first (before addressing mental health or substance use) leads to better outcomes across all domains. This is a key policy position in Canadian public health.

**Q:** What are the steps of a community needs assessment?

**A:** A community needs assessment studies a community's health gaps, pairs identification of gaps with existing resources and strengths to find solutions. Steps:

1. Define the community and understand its history and demographic characteristics to provide context for data collection
2. Understand what matters to community stakeholders (e.g., interviews, surveys, focus groups)
3. Use evidence (e.g., mortality rate, feasibility) to prioritize each concern
4. Identify barriers that may prevent a concern from being addressed and propose solutions using community-based resources

Strongly values interviewing community members to gather their concerns and proposed solutions.

Community needs assessments are participatory and community-centered — a key distinction from top-down public health approaches. Involving community members in identifying problems and solutions is central to effective health promotion.

**Q:** What is the opioid crisis in Canada — key statistics and commonly involved substances?

**A:** Key statistics (from Health Canada):
- In 2018: 4,460 apparent opioid-related deaths in Canada
- 5,019,490 hospitalizations and 17,000 emergency services related to opioids
- Individuals aged 25–34 are at greatest risk of overdose death (1 in 6 deaths)
- Most commonly unintentional

Most commonly involved substances: Heroin, fentanyl, hydromorphone

Highest rates: British Columbia — approximately 300 per million British Columbians died in relation to opioid use in 2017

Ontario: ~1,250 deaths from opioids; fentanyl involved in more than 70% of cases

Note: Fentanyl is approximately 100× more potent than morphine; illicitly manufactured fentanyl has driven the crisis.

The opioid crisis is a defining Canadian public health emergency. Fentanyl contamination of the drug supply, combined with inadequate treatment access, has driven unprecedented overdose death rates, particularly in British Columbia.

**Q:** What are the three main federal agencies responsible for public health in Canada and their specific roles?

**A:** 1. Health Canada:
   - Provides health services to Canadian military and veterans
   - Provides non-insured health benefits (NIHB) to status First Nations and Inuit; funds healthcare on reserve
   - Approves new drugs and medical devices
   - Maintains Canada's Food Guide

2. Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC):
   - Main Government of Canada agency responsible for public health
   - Created post-SARS; focuses on chronic disease prevention, injury prevention, and emergency response
   - Activities include CTFPHC guideline secretariat, immigration screening
   - Liaises with WHO

3. Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA):
   - Regulates federal food safety, labelling, and recalls
   - Deals with animal-related infections (zoonoses)

Understanding the jurisdictional division of public health responsibilities in Canada is essential for the MCCQE1. Health Canada is the regulator; PHAC is the public health operations agency; CFIA handles food safety.

**Q:** What are the nutritional trials conducted on Indigenous peoples in Canada and their ethical violations?

**A:** From 1942–1952, nutritional scientists (with Canadian government involvement) performed unethical research on Indigenous people, including the James Bay Survey on Attawapiskat and Rupert's House Cree First Nations.

Lead scientist: Dr. Frederick Tisdall (inventor of Pablum, former president of Canadian Paediatric Society).

Ethical violations:
- Lack of informed consent from parents or children
- Indigenous children kept malnourished over two years to establish a baseline
- One group given a flour mix not yet approved for sale — caused anemia, contributing to morbidity/possibly mortality, with no therapeutic intervention
- Dental care denied to observe progression of cavities and gingivitis in setting of malnutrition

Health impacts of sustained caloric restriction:
- Height stunting
- Physiological changes to prioritize fat over lean mass
- Increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes
- Stunting negatively impacts neurological, psychological, and immune systems
- Intergenerational: other generations at risk of higher BMI and obesity (fat-mass accumulation when nutrition becomes available)

These unethical experiments, like residential schools, represent the use of medicine as a tool of colonial oppression. The health legacy — including metabolic programming toward obesity and diabetes — is a direct cause of contemporary health disparities.

**Q:** What is health intersectionality and health stigma?

**A:** Health intersectionality: The concept that an individual's various social identities (e.g., race, weight, sex, class, ability) can combine and overlap to create unique experiences of health discrimination and/or privilege. No single aspect of identity fully determines one's health experience.

Health stigma: The reduction of a person's identity to negative stereotypes about their health status (e.g., weight stigma). Stigma may:
- Limit patient trust in healthcare providers
- Reduce healthcare quality
- Reduce accessibility of care

Clinical application: An intersectional lens is required when caring for marginalized populations — e.g., a racialized LGBTQ+ person faces compounded barriers compared to either group alone.

Intersectionality, originally developed by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw, has been adopted in public health to explain why some individuals face compounded health disadvantages. Weight stigma in healthcare is a well-documented barrier to care.
